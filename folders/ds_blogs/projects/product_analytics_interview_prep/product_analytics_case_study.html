Overview: The Five-Phase Analytics Lifecycle

This case study demonstrates how a product analyst applies the five-phase analytics framework to an AI-powered e-commerce recommendation engine.

The framework provides a structured approach to answering a single question at each phase:

  • Is there an opportunity?
  • Does it work?
  • Can it be measured?
  • Is the rollout successful?
  • Where should the team optimize next?

The underlying principle is simple: analytics should inform every decision before, during, and after launch.

A product analyst embeds measurement into the product lifecycle from the beginning, starting with market sizing and opportunity analysis, moving through validation and instrumentation, and concluding with adoption tracking, performance monitoring, and retention optimization.

Why This Framework Matters

Many product teams approach analytics reactively; building dashboards only after launch, waiting for problems to surface, or measuring metrics that do not connect to business outcomes.

The 5-phase framework inverts this: analytics drives the questions the team asks and the decisions they make at each phase.

Phase 1: Discovery

Core question: Is there a problem worth solving, and how big is it?

Primary deliverable: Market sizing, Total Addressable Market estimation, customer segmentation

Data source: Product Manager led. Industry reports, competitive benchmarks, and domain knowledge for Total Addressable Market. The analyst validates assumptions using internal product data (conversion gaps, search failure rates, segment behavior).

Analyst role: Sources evidence and validates sizing. The Product Manager synthesizes the go/no-go recommendation.

Phase 2: Validation

Core question: Does the product actually solve the problem?

Primary deliverable: Pilot design, success metrics, A/B test results

Data source: Pilot or minimum viable product experiment data. A controlled test on a small user segment using a prototype or minimal version of the feature, not the fully built product.

Analyst role: Designs the pilot, defines success thresholds, and measures baseline performance before the full product is built.

Phase 3: Build

Core question: How does the team measure the product's impact at scale?

Primary deliverable: Event taxonomy, metrics tree, dashboard design

Data source: No new data collected. The analyst defines what to track and where to source it, informed by the pilot results from Validation. Instrumentation is built here for collection during Rollout.

Analyst role: Works with engineering to define events before development so instrumentation is built in from the start, not bolted on after.

Phase 4: Rollout

Core question: Is the product working in the real world?

Primary deliverable: Adoption funnels, cohort analysis, model performance tracking

Data source: Production user data. Real customer activity, model predictions, and funnel events flowing through the instrumentation built in Phase 3.

Analyst role: Monitors adoption, measures impact against baselines, and applies the Expand / Iterate / Pause framework.

Phase 5: Scale

Core question: Where should the team optimize and where should it expand?

Primary deliverable: Retention analysis, churn prediction, expansion targeting

Data source: Longitudinal production data. Accumulated customer behavior over weeks or months, enabling retention curves, churn models, and lifetime value calculations.

Analyst role: Builds feedback loops that tell the team whether the product is delivering value and where to invest next, including the kill/pivot assessment.

The framework is repeatable. Whether the product is a recommendation engine, a SaaS application, or an AI-powered healthcare tool, the five phases and the core questions at each stage remain the same. Only the metrics and data sources change.

Phase 1: Discovery

Size the Opportunity

The Discovery phase answers the question: Is there a problem worth solving, and how large is the addressable market? Market sizing and Total Addressable Market estimation are typically driven by the product manager or business leadership. The product analyst's role in this phase is to:

  • Support with internal data analysis
  • Validate the Product Manager's assumptions with evidence
  • Identify the highest-value customer segments using existing datasets
Role Clarity
The Product Manager owns the strategic market sizing exercise and the go/no-go decision. The analyst provides the data backbone:
  • Pulling internal conversion metrics
  • Identifying behavioral patterns that signal unmet demand
  • Stress-testing the Product Manager's Total Addressable Market assumptions against real usage data
In organizations with a Business Intelligence team, market research and competitive intelligence may also come from that function.

Step 1: Market Sizing and Total Addressable Market

The Product Manager assembles the top-down market estimate using industry reports, competitive benchmarks, and domain knowledge. The analyst supports by pulling internal data that validates or challenges those assumptions. For an e-commerce recommendation engine, this means quantifying current search failure rates, product discovery friction, and conversion gaps that the recommendation engine could address.

1 Gather Market Data (Product Manager Led)

The Product Manager assembles baseline market statistics from public sources: total e-commerce market size, growth rates, adoption rates for AI tools, and benchmarks for recommendation engine performance.

  • Total US e-commerce market: $1.1 trillion (2026, United States only)
  • Merchants using AI recommendations: 35% adoption rate
  • Average recommendation revenue lift: 12% increase in transaction value
Show Math: Total Addressable Market Estimation (Top-Down)

Total Addressable Market = Total Market Size × Adoption Rate × Revenue Lift

Total Addressable Market = $1.1 trillion × 0.35 × 0.12
Total Addressable Market = $46.2 billion (US market only)

This is a top-down estimate based on industry averages. It answers: "How big could this market be if every eligible merchant adopted AI recommendations?" The Product Manager uses this to justify investment, but it does not tell the team whether the opportunity is real for this specific platform.

2 Validate with Internal Data (Analyst Led)

The analyst pulls internal metrics that ground the market estimate in actual product behavior: What percentage of users abandon search without clicking? How often do customers purchase only one item when the catalog suggests complementary products? These signals quantify the internal opportunity the recommendation engine could capture.

Show Math: Internal Opportunity Estimation (Bottom-Up)

The analyst uses the platform's own data to estimate the revenue the recommendation engine could recover. This is a bottom-up calculation that the team can compare against the Product Manager's top-down market estimate.

Platform baseline (from internal data):

Monthly sessions:        10,000,000
Conversion rate:         12.5% (from Validation baseline)
Average order value:     $65
Current annual revenue:  10M × 12 × 0.125 × $65 = $975M/year

Opportunity 1: Recovering abandoned search sessions

Sessions with no product click:   22% × 10M = 2,200,000/month
If recommendations capture 10% of those and convert at baseline rate:
  2,200,000 × 0.10 × 0.125 × $65 = $1.79M/month
  Annual recovery: ~$21.4M/year

Opportunity 2: Increasing cross-sell through recommendations

Current avg cart size:     1.3 items
Target with recommendations: 1.8 items (+0.5 items)
Avg price per added item:  $30
Incremental cross-sell:    10M × 0.125 × 12 × 0.5 × $30 = $225M/year

Combined internal opportunity: ~$246M/year for this one platform

Interpretation: If one mid-to-large e-commerce platform sees a $246M internal opportunity, and there are thousands of similar platforms across the US market, the Product Manager's $46.2 billion top-down estimate is plausible at an order-of-magnitude level. The internal data does not prove the market estimate is correct, but it confirms the problem is real for this platform and the scale is directionally consistent.

Step 2: Customer Segmentation (Analyst Led)

Not all customers are equal. The analyst segments the addressable market by characteristics that predict need, willingness to pay, and likelihood of adoption. This is squarely analyst territory, grouping merchants by:

  • Store size
  • Product catalog size
  • Current recommendation maturity
  • Traffic volume
Customer Segmentation Example
  • Segment A (High-Value): Large merchants ($10M+ annual GMV) with extensive catalogs (10,000+ SKUs) and existing recommendation systems. Pain point: optimizing conversion lift. Addressable market: $24B.
  • Segment B (Growth): Mid-market merchants ($1M-$10M GMV) with moderate catalogs. Pain point: starting to scale recommendations. Addressable market: $18B.
  • Segment C (Long-tail): Small merchants (<$1M GMV). Pain point: no recommendation capability. Addressable market: $4.2B.

Segmentation reveals that 52% of the total opportunity comes from Segment A (large merchants), but Segment B offers faster growth. The team's go-to-market strategy depends on this segmentation: invest in Segment A first for revenue, or Segment B first for growth and learning?

Step 3: Competitive Landscape (Product Manager Led, Analyst Supports)

The Product Manager maps existing solutions, their market coverage, and identifies whitespace. The analyst supports by pulling usage data that reveals where existing solutions fall short for current customers. For AI recommendations, the landscape includes established players (Amazon SageMaker, Google Recommendations AI, Shopify's built-in engine) and emerging startups.

Competitive Positioning Matrix

  • Established players (60% market share): Comprehensive but expensive. Require significant technical infrastructure. Best for large merchants.
  • Emerging SaaS (25% market share): Faster implementation, lower cost. Target mid-market and growth merchants.
  • Whitespace (15% market share): Long-tail merchants with no recommendation capability. Opportunity for a specialized, easy-to-integrate solution.
Discovery Insight
The competitive landscape analysis reveals the product's target market. If the market is dominated by well-funded competitors (Amazon, Google), the team must either find a defensible niche (e.g., specific vertical like fashion) or build a more specialized capability (e.g., live-stream shopping recommendations). If the market has whitespace, the team should prioritize speed-to-market and customer acquisition.

Phase 2: Validation

Define and Measure Success

The Validation phase answers: Does the product actually solve the problem? This phase moves from market analysis to pilot testing. The analyst designs a controlled experiment (A/B test), collects baseline data, and measures whether the product delivers the promised benefit.

Step 1: Design the Pilot and Set Baselines

Before running a full A/B test, the analyst must establish a baseline: the conversion rate, average order value, and other metrics without the recommendation engine. This baseline is the control group in the later experiment.

Baseline Metrics (Control Group)
  • Sample size: 2,000 users (4-week period)
  • Conversion rate: 12.5% (250 conversions / 2,000 sessions)
  • Average order value: $65
  • Primary metric: Purchase conversion rate

Step 2: Run the A/B Test

The analyst runs a controlled experiment, assigning half the traffic to the control group (no recommendations) and half to the treatment group (with the AI recommendation engine). The test runs until statistical significance is reached.

A/B Test Results (Treatment Group)
  • Sample size: 2,000 users (same 4-week period)
  • Conversion rate: 18.0% (360 conversions / 2,000 sessions)
  • Lift: +5.5 percentage points (43.75% relative increase)
  • Statistical significance: p < 0.0001 (highly significant)

Step 3: Statistical Analysis, The Z-Test

To confirm the lift is real and not due to randomness, the analyst performs a Z-test to compare the two proportions (control vs. treatment conversion rates).

Show Math: Two-Sample Z-Test for Proportions

Null Hypothesis (H₀): The treatment has no effect on conversion rate.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): The treatment increases conversion rate.

Given:
  n₁ = 2,000 (control group size)
  x₁ = 250 (control conversions)
  p₁ = 0.125 (control conversion rate)

  n₂ = 2,000 (treatment group size)
  x₂ = 360 (treatment conversions)
  p₂ = 0.180 (treatment conversion rate)

Step 1: Calculate pooled proportion
  p̂ = (x₁ + x₂) / (n₁ + n₂)
  p̂ = (250 + 360) / 4,000
  p̂ = 0.1525

Step 2: Calculate standard error
  SE = sqrt(p̂(1 - p̂) × (1/n₁ + 1/n₂))
  SE = sqrt(0.1525 × 0.8475 × (0.0005 + 0.0005))
  SE = sqrt(0.1292 × 0.001)
  SE = 0.01136

Step 3: Calculate Z-statistic
  Z = (p₂ - p₁) / SE
  Z = (0.180 - 0.125) / 0.01136
  Z = 0.055 / 0.01136
  Z = 4.84

Step 4: Find p-value
  p-value = P(Z > 4.84) < 0.0001

Conclusion: The Z-statistic of 4.84 far exceeds the critical value of 1.96
(for α = 0.05, two-tailed). The result is statistically significant.
The recommendation engine increases conversion rate.

Step 4: Bayesian Sequential Testing

For faster decision-making, the analyst can use Bayesian sequential testing, which allows stopping the test early if the posterior probability of the treatment effect crosses a decision threshold (e.g., 95% confidence).

Show Math: Bayesian Beta-Binomial Update

Prior belief: Assume a Beta(1, 1) prior (uniform).

Update with treatment data: After observing 360 conversions in 2,000 trials, the posterior follows Beta(361, 1,641).

Posterior: Beta(α_post, β_post)
  α_post = 1 + 360 = 361
  β_post = 1 + (2,000 - 360) = 1,641

Posterior mean: 361 / (361 + 1,641) = 0.180
Posterior 95% credible interval: [0.163, 0.197]

The analyst calculates P(p_treatment > p_control | data).
This probability is 0.997, indicating very strong evidence in favor
of the treatment effect.

Phase 3: Build

Instrument the Product for Measurement

The Build phase answers: How does the team measure the product's impact at scale? The analyst works with the engineering team to:

  • Design the event taxonomy
  • Create the metrics tree
  • Define dashboards
  • Set guardrail thresholds

Build is about embedding measurement into the product architecture.

Step 1: Event Taxonomy for an AI Product

For an e-commerce recommendation engine, the analyst defines events that capture the full user journey: recommendation display, click, add-to-cart, and purchase. The taxonomy includes context (which recommendation algorithm fired, position on page, user segment) that enables later segmentation and debugging.

Event Taxonomy for AI Recommendation Engine
recommendation_displayed
  ├─ recommendation_id: unique ID for this recommendation
  ├─ algorithm_version: "v1.0_collaborative" or "v2.0_hybrid"
  ├─ position: 1-10 (position on page)
  ├─ user_segment: "new", "returning", "vip"
  └─ product_id: ID of recommended product

recommendation_clicked
  ├─ recommendation_id: (links to display event)
  ├─ dwell_time: milliseconds spent viewing before click
  └─ conversion_within_30min: true/false (recorded later)

product_added_to_cart
  ├─ product_id, price, source_type: "recommendation" or "search"
  └─ recommendation_id: (if from recommendation)

purchase_completed
  ├─ order_value, num_items, items_from_recommendations: count
  └─ recommendation_influence: "direct" or "discovery"

Step 4: Power Analysis and Sample Size Calculation

Before launch, the analyst calculates the sample size needed to detect the expected effect size with adequate power. This ensures the team does not waste time on underpowered experiments.

Show Math: Power Analysis for Sample Size

Scenario: The team expects a 5 percentage point lift in conversion rate, from 13% (baseline) to 18% (treatment). They want 80% power and 5% significance level.

Given:
  p₀ = 0.13 (baseline conversion rate)
  p₁ = 0.18 (expected treatment rate)
  α = 0.05 (significance level, two-tailed) → Z_α/2 = 1.96
  β = 0.20 (Type II error rate) → Z_β = 0.84 (power = 1 - β = 0.80)

Formula (two-sample proportion test):
  n = ((Z_α/2 + Z_β)² × (p₀(1-p₀) + p₁(1-p₁))) / (p₁ - p₀)²

Step 1: Calculate variance terms
  p₀(1-p₀) = 0.13 × 0.87 = 0.1131
  p₁(1-p₁) = 0.18 × 0.82 = 0.1476

Step 2: Calculate standard variance
  p₀(1-p₀) + p₁(1-p₁) = 0.1131 + 0.1476 = 0.2607

Step 3: Plug into formula
  n = ((1.96 + 0.84)² × 0.2607) / (0.18 - 0.13)²
  n = (2.80² × 0.2607) / 0.0025
  n = (7.84 × 0.2607) / 0.0025
  n = 2.047 / 0.0025
  n = 818.8

Conclusion: The team needs approximately 819 samples per group
(1,638 total) to detect a 5 percentage point lift with 80% power
and 5% significance level. This is achievable in 2-3 weeks of traffic.

Step 5: Guardrail Definitions

The analyst defines guardrails: metrics that must NOT degrade when the new feature launches. For a recommendation engine, guardrails include:

  • User satisfaction (support tickets, returns)
  • Page load time
  • Null recommendation rates
Support Tickets (Guardrail)

Recommendation-related complaints per 1,000 users

Threshold: ≤ 5 per 1,000 users (baseline: 2 per 1,000)

Page Load Time (Guardrail)

Median load time (ms)

Threshold: ≤ 2,500ms (baseline: 2,000ms)

Null Recommendations (Guardrail)

% of users shown blank recommendations

Threshold: ≤ 2% (baseline: 0.5%)

Phase 4: Rollout

Monitor Adoption and Model Performance

The Rollout phase answers: Is the product working in the real world? This phase moves from controlled pilot to production. The analyst:

  • Tracks adoption funnels
  • Monitors model performance (precision and recall)
  • Measures recommendation acceptance rates
  • Decides whether to expand, iterate, or pause the rollout

Step 3: Kaplan-Meier Survival Analysis

For long-term product retention, the analyst uses Kaplan-Meier survival curves to estimate the probability that a user remains active (making purchases) over time. This accounts for users who churn at different points in time.

Interactive Chart: Kaplan-Meier Survival Curve

Show Math: Kaplan-Meier Survival Estimator

Purpose: Estimate the probability of surviving (remaining active) past time t, accounting for censoring (users who leave for other reasons).

S(t) = ∏ (1 - d_i / n_i)

Where:
  S(t) = probability of surviving past time t
  d_i = number of events (churn) at time i
  n_i = number at risk just before time i

Example calculation at Week 4:
  Time 0: n₀ = 3,730, d₀ = 0
  Time 1: n₁ = 3,650, d₁ = 80 (churned)
  Time 2: n₂ = 3,500, d₂ = 150 (churned)
  Time 3: n₃ = 3,300, d₃ = 200 (churned)
  Time 4: n₄ = 3,100, d₄ = 200 (churned)

S(4) = (1 - 80/3,650) × (1 - 150/3,500) × (1 - 200/3,300) × (1 - 200/3,100)
S(4) = 0.978 × 0.957 × 0.939 × 0.935
S(4) = 0.851

Interpretation: 85.1% of users who received recommendations are
still active at week 4.

Step 4: Model Performance Metrics

For AI-powered products, the analyst tracks model performance metrics: precision (are the recommendations good?), recall (does the model find all good recommendations?), and acceptance rate (do users like the suggestions?).

AI Recommendation Model Performance
  • Total recommendations shown: 10,150
  • True Positives (recommended & purchased): 5,088
  • False Positives (recommended but not purchased): 738
  • False Negatives (not recommended but purchased anyway): 0 (not directly observable)
  • Precision: 87.3% (good recommendations / all recommendations)
  • Recall: 100% (the model surfaces all good options)

Interactive Chart: Confusion Matrix

Show Math: Precision, Recall, and F1-Score

Definitions for AI recommendation model:

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
            = 5,088 / (5,088 + 738)
            = 5,088 / 5,826
            = 0.873 (87.3%)

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
       = 5,088 / (5,088 + 0)
       = 1.0 (100%)

F1-Score = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)
         = 2 × (0.873 × 1.0) / (0.873 + 1.0)
         = 1.746 / 1.873
         = 0.932 (93.2%)

Interpretation:
  - Precision (87.3%): Of 100 recommendations the model makes,
    87 will be purchased and 13 will be ignored. High precision
    means fewer wasted recommendations.
  - Recall (100%): The model recommends all good products.
    Users are not missing good options.
  - F1-Score (93.2%): Balanced measure of precision-recall
    trade-off. Very strong performance.
Rollout Insight
The funnel, retention, and model performance metrics tell a consistent story: the recommendation engine is working. Users engage with recommendations at high rates, convert to purchase, come back repeatedly, and the model is precise. The next question (Scale phase) is: how to expand to more users, optimize for retention, and increase revenue per user?

Phase 5: Scale

Optimize Growth and Retention

The Scale phase answers: Where should the team optimize and where should it expand? With the product validated and working in production, the analyst focuses on retention strategies, churn prediction, and customer lifetime value optimization. The goal is to maximize the total value delivered and captured by the business.

Step 1: Churn Risk Scoring

The analyst builds a predictive model that identifies users at risk of churning (not returning to purchase again). This enables proactive intervention: special offers, personalized recommendations, or support outreach.

Interactive Chart: Churn Risk Scoring Model Features

Show Math: Logistic Regression for Churn Prediction

Model: Logistic regression predicts churn probability based on user features.

P(Churn) = 1 / (1 + e^(-z))

Where z = β₀ + β₁×days_since_last_purchase + β₂×purchase_frequency
          + β₃×avg_order_value + β₄×support_tickets

Example fitted coefficients:
  β₀ (intercept) = -2.5
  β₁ (days since last purchase) = 0.08   (higher days → higher churn risk)
  β₂ (purchase frequency) = -0.15        (more frequent purchasers → lower risk)
  β₃ (average order value) = -0.002      (higher spending → lower risk)
  β₄ (support tickets) = 0.12            (more tickets → higher risk)

For a user with:
  days_since_last_purchase = 30
  purchase_frequency = 5
  avg_order_value = 80
  support_tickets = 1

z = -2.5 + 0.08×30 - 0.15×5 - 0.002×80 + 0.12×1
z = -2.5 + 2.4 - 0.75 - 0.16 + 0.12
z = -0.89

P(Churn) = 1 / (1 + e^0.89)
P(Churn) = 1 / (1 + 2.44)
P(Churn) = 0.29 (29% churn risk)

Action: Flag this user for intervention. Offer a personalized
discount or recommendation to re-engage.

The analyst creates a 2×2 matrix to prioritize optimization opportunities: which user segments should the team focus on (high value + high churn risk), which segments are stable, and which are growing?

Step 6: Calibration and Model Monitoring

As the recommendation model runs in production, the analyst monitors whether the model's predicted probabilities match observed reality. This is calibration: when the model predicts 70% conversion rate for a segment, does that segment actually convert 70% of the time?

Interactive Chart: Calibration Curves

Show Math: Calibration Error and Brier Score

Calibration Error: Measures how well predicted probabilities match observed outcomes.

For each confidence bin (e.g., 0-10%, 10-20%, ..., 90-100%),
calculate the mean predicted probability and mean observed accuracy.

Example:
  Confidence bin 70-80%:
    Mean predicted probability: 0.75
    Mean observed accuracy: 0.82 (82% of recommended items were purchased)
    Calibration error at this bin: |0.75 - 0.82| = 0.07

Mean Calibration Error (MCE) = (1/B) × Σ |predicted_i - actual_i|
                              = 0.168 (over all bins)

Brier Score = (1/n) × Σ (predicted_i - actual_i)²
            = 0.042

Interpretation:
  - MCE of 0.168 means the model is on average off by 16.8 percentage
    points. This is reasonable for e-commerce.
  - Brier score of 0.042 (lower is better) indicates good calibration.
  - Action: The model is slightly overconfident at lower confidence
    levels. Retrain with regularization to improve.
Scale Insight
At the Scale phase, the analyst's focus shifts from "Is it working?" to "How to grow and optimize?" The churn risk model, outcome matrix, and prioritization framework enable the team to allocate resources efficiently and maximize customer lifetime value. This is where good analytics becomes strategic advantage.

Competitive Landscape Mapping Discovery Feature matrix, market share analysis, whitespace identification Understand competitive positioning and market gaps
Two-Sample Z-Test Validation Z = (p₂ - p₁) / SE where SE = sqrt(p̂(1-p̂)(1/n₁+1/n₂)) Test whether A/B test results are statistically significant
Bayesian Sequential Testing Validation Posterior = Beta(α + successes, β + failures) Run A/B tests that stop early when evidence is conclusive
Chi-Square Test Validation χ² = Σ (observed - expected)² / expected Test independence between categorical variables (e.g., segment × conversion)
Power Analysis & Sample Size Build n = ((Z_α/2 + Z_β)² × (p₁(1-p₁) + p₂(1-p₂))) / (p₂ - p₁)² Calculate sample size needed for an experiment
Event Taxonomy Design Build Hierarchical event naming with context attributes Instrument product to enable measurement at scale
Metrics Tree (North Star Decomposition) Build North Star = Input Lever 1 × Input Lever 2 × Input Lever 3 Break down North Star into actionable levers
Guardrail Definition Build Set upper/lower bounds on acceptable metric values Protect against negative side effects during experiments
Funnel Analysis Rollout Conversion rate = Step N completions / Step N entries Identify where users drop off in the user journey
Cohort Retention (N-Week) Rollout Retention_N = Users active in week N / Users active in week 1 Measure long-term product engagement and sticky features
Kaplan-Meier Survival Rollout S(t) = ∏ (1 - d_i / n_i) for each time step i Estimate survival probability accounting for censoring
Precision, Recall, F1-Score Rollout Precision = TP/(TP+FP), Recall = TP/(TP+FN), F1 = 2×P×R/(P+R) Evaluate AI model recommendation quality
Confusion Matrix Rollout TP, FP, FN, TN cross-tabulation Visualize model performance across all outcome types
Logistic Regression (Churn Prediction) Scale P(churn) = 1 / (1 + e^(-(β₀ + β₁×x₁ + ...))) Predict user churn risk and identify at-risk segments
Outcome Matrix (LTV × Churn Risk) Scale Segment users into 4 quadrants based on value and risk Prioritize retention investments and expansion opportunities
Impact-Effort Prioritization Scale Plot opportunities on impact vs. effort 2×2 matrix Identify quick wins and allocate engineering resources
Cohort Analysis (LTV by Cohort) Scale LTV = Average Revenue per User × Customer Lifetime Understand how different user cohorts (acquisition date, source) perform
Calibration & Brier Score Scale Brier Score = (1/n) × Σ (predicted_i - actual_i)² Monitor whether model predictions match reality in production
Exponential Decay (Retention Fitting) Scale Retention(t) = R₀ × e^(-λt) where λ is decay constant Model churn as exponential decay and project long-term retention
