The Behavioral Health Treatment Gap

More than half of American adults with a mental illness do not receive treatment. This treatment gap — estimated at 55% nationally by SAMHSA's National Survey on Drug Use and Health (2022-2023) — varies significantly by state, ranging from roughly 45% in states with robust provider networks to over 60% in underserved regions.

For behavioral health companies considering geographic expansion, this gap represents both the core problem they aim to solve and a key signal for where demand is greatest. But the treatment gap alone doesn't determine where to expand. Provider availability, insurance coverage, competitive dynamics, regulatory frameworks, and reimbursement rates all vary state by state — creating a complex, multi-dimensional decision space that resists simple answers.

This analysis builds a systematic framework for evaluating that decision space using publicly available data, applied to a real company as a case study.

The Current Behavioral Health Provider Landscape

The United States has approximately 623,000 licensed behavioral health providers across six major categories: psychiatrists, psychologists, mental health counselors, marriage and family therapists, social workers, and substance abuse counselors. Their distribution is highly uneven — northeastern and western states have 2-3x the per-capita provider density of southern and rural states.

This matters for expansion because a company entering a new state needs to recruit providers locally (or through interstate compacts). States with higher provider density offer a larger recruitment pool, but may also face more competition from established platforms.

Four major telehealth behavioral health competitors — Rula, Grow Therapy, Headway, and Alma — already operate nationwide. Competition is measured here through a composite of competitor provider density, brick-and-mortar behavioral health facility density (~18,000 SAMHSA-listed facilities nationally), and broadband access (a proxy for telehealth viability).

Octave Health Group — A Case Study in Expansion Analysis

Octave Health Group is a behavioral health company operating in 23 states plus Washington D.C. Founded in 2018, Octave offers therapy and psychiatric services through a hybrid model of virtual telehealth and in-person clinics (San Francisco, Oakland, Los Angeles, New York City). The company has raised approximately $88M in funding (Series C) and employs a curated network of hundreds of therapists.

Octave partners with 14 commercial insurance plans — including UnitedHealthcare/Optum, Aetna, Cigna/Evernorth, and multiple Blue Cross Blue Shield affiliates — covering an estimated 40 million lives nationally.

The question driving this analysis: if Octave were to expand from 23 to all 50 states plus D.C., which of the 28 remaining states represent the strongest opportunities, and what factors should inform the sequencing?

Key assumptions about covered lives:

The payer model estimates covered lives by applying each insurer's state-level commercial market share to the state's commercially insured population, then summing across Octave's payer partners. This produces a "raw" estimate of ~82M lives in the current 23 states. Since Octave reports ~40M covered lives, we apply a conservative 40% network access rate — reflecting the reality that not all commercially insured lives in a payer's book are actively in-network with Octave. Every covered-lives figure in this analysis uses this adjusted number.

Four smaller payers (Centivo, Health Net, MHN, UMR for Mt. Sinai) are not modeled individually, likely undercounting by 2-5%. This makes our estimates conservative.

A Six-Dimension Scoring Model

Each of the 28 expansion states is scored across six dimensions, each normalized to a 0-100 scale using min-max scaling across only the expansion states (not the full 51). The dimensions are combined using weighted averaging:

Provider Pool (20%) — Licensed behavioral health providers per 100K adults. More providers = larger recruitment pool. Source: BLS OES 2023, HRSA NPPES.

Payer Coverage (25%) — Accessible covered lives as a percentage of the adult population (after 40% network access rate). Higher penetration = more patients reachable through existing payer partnerships. Source: KFF, insurer market share reports.

Unmet Demand (20%) — Treatment gap percentage: the share of adults with any mental illness (AMI) who did not receive treatment. Higher gap = greater unmet need. Source: SAMHSA NSDUH 2022-2023.

Competitive Advantage (10%) — Inverse of competitive intensity. States with fewer existing telehealth competitors and BH facilities score higher. Source: SAMHSA treatment locator, FCC broadband.

Regulatory Ease (15%) — Interstate compact membership (PSYPACT, Counseling Compact, Social Work Compact) and telehealth parity laws. States belonging to all 3 compacts enable provider credentialing in 2-4 weeks vs. 12-20 weeks for non-compact states. Source: Compact rosters.

Reimbursement Favorability (10%) — Medicare fee schedule rates for common therapy codes (CPT 90834, 90837). Used as a floor benchmark since commercial plans typically reimburse at 130-196% of Medicare (Milliman 2025). Source: CMS Physician Fee Schedule 2024.

Why these weights? Payer coverage receives the highest weight (25%) because without insurance coverage, there are no patients. Provider pool and unmet demand each get 20% — they represent supply and demand sides of the same equation. Regulatory ease (15%) reflects the operational bottleneck of provider credentialing. Competition and reimbursement receive lower weights (10% each) because they are less directly controllable.

What the Model Reveals

The scoring model identifies 7 Tier 1 (High Priority), 7 Tier 2 (Medium Priority), and 14 Tier 3 (Long-term) expansion states. Tier assignments use percentile-based thresholds: Tier 1 = above 75th percentile, Tier 2 = 50th-75th, Tier 3 = below 50th.

Tier 1 states (scores 52-60): High Priority Maine, Kentucky, Indiana, Delaware, Missouri, New Hampshire, West Virginia. These score well not because of any single factor but because provider availability, payer coverage, unmet demand, and regulatory ease all converge favorably.

Key observation: The highest-scoring expansion states are mid-sized, not the largest. Large states like Florida and Texas are already in Octave's footprint. The remaining large states tend to score lower on expansion opportunity because they often have higher competition or lower per-capita payer penetration.

26 of 28 expansion states have lower competitive intensity than the average of Octave's current 23 states. This suggests that if Octave can operate successfully in its existing markets, the competitive environment in expansion states is generally more favorable.

Scenario Modeling — Hypothetical Expansion Projections

Important disclaimer: The expansion waves and revenue projections presented here are a hypothetical analytical exercise. They demonstrate how data could inform sequencing decisions but do not reflect Octave's actual expansion plans, internal priorities, or proprietary business strategy.

Revenue assumptions (conservative scenario):
  • Market penetration: 0.5% of accessible covered lives become Octave patients
  • Session frequency: 20 sessions per patient per year
  • Average reimbursement: $130 per session (CPT 90834)
  • Provider pay: $115/hour (0.75 hours per session = $86.25/session)
  • Gross margin: ~$15 per session after provider pay

Under these conservative assumptions, the 28 expansion states yield approximately $95M in incremental annual revenue. A moderate scenario (1% penetration) doubles this to ~$190M; an optimistic scenario (2%) yields ~$380M.

Limitations and Conclusions

Limitations

This analysis has several important limitations that should inform how the results are interpreted:

1. Payer-by-state activation. The model assumes Octave's existing payer partnerships extend to all states. In practice, insurance contracts may need to be activated state-by-state, and the payer mix in expansion states may differ from current states.

2. Provider willingness. Provider density measures availability, not willingness to join a specific platform. Octave's above-market pay ($107-123/hr vs. industry average ~$86-100/hr) and no-show compensation are competitive advantages, but the model doesn't estimate a provider recruitment conversion rate.

3. In-person clinic decisions. Octave operates physical clinics in 4 cities. The model doesn't address where to locate new clinics — a decision influenced by real estate, local referral networks, and hybrid care demand that requires proprietary data.

4. Static snapshot. All data reflects 2022-2024 sources. Provider counts, payer market shares, compact memberships, and competitive dynamics change over time. The rankings should be treated as a starting framework, not a permanent answer.

5. Scoring weight sensitivity. Different strategic priorities produce different rankings. The weights used (25% payer, 20% provider, 20% demand, 15% regulatory, 10% competition, 10% reimbursement) reflect reasonable priors but are not empirically optimized. A sensitivity analysis varying these weights would strengthen confidence in the tier assignments.

6. Revenue projections are illustrative. The $95M conservative estimate depends entirely on the 0.5% penetration assumption and $130/session average. Actual revenue would depend on provider recruitment velocity, payer contracting timelines, patient acquisition costs, and local market execution.

Conclusions

The contribution of this analysis is the framework, not any single number. By normalizing heterogeneous data sources to a common scoring scale and weighting them by strategic relevance, we can systematically compare 28 states that would otherwise be evaluated ad hoc.

The model reveals that the strongest expansion opportunities are not the largest remaining states but mid-sized states where provider density, treatment gaps, and regulatory ease create favorable conditions simultaneously. This is a finding that intuition alone — which tends to favor population size — would likely miss.

For any behavioral health company evaluating geographic expansion, this type of multi-dimensional scoring approach can serve as a starting point for deeper analysis using proprietary internal data.
