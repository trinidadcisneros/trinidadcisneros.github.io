Executive Summary

More than half of American adults with a mental illness do not receive treatment — and the gap varies dramatically by state. For Octave Health Group, a behavioral health platform operating in 23 states with approximately 40 million covered lives, the remaining 28 states represent a significant untapped market. But expanding into all of them simultaneously would require recruiting and credentialing providers in each new state, activating payer contracts, and navigating state-specific regulations — all at once. A phased approach, prioritizing states by strategic fit, allows the company to concentrate resources where the return is highest and scale without overwhelming the provider network. This analysis scores each state across six publicly available indicators to answer a practical question:

Which states should come first, and what does the data say about how to enter them successfully?

Key Findings

  • Where to expand first: Maine, Kentucky, Indiana, Delaware, Missouri, New Hampshire, and West Virginia rank highest across all 28 states Octave does not currently operate in. These mid-sized states separate clearly from the rest because they combine strong demand, fast credentialing paths, and existing payer coverage — conditions that rarely converge in the larger states still available.
  • Why these states over others: When benchmarked on the same scoring framework, these top 7 outscore 21 of Octave's 23 current states across five of six indicators. Current states lead only on per-session reimbursement — a result of Octave entering high-paying coastal markets first. The expansion states offer lower rates per visit but higher unmet demand and faster regulatory pathways, meaning revenue growth comes from reaching more patients efficiently rather than from higher reimbursement per session.
  • What the competitive landscape looks like: National telehealth platforms like Rula, Grow Therapy, Headway, and Alma already operate in all 50 states, so Octave would not be entering uncontested markets. However, these states have lower density of local behavioral health facilities, which means fewer established referral networks and more patients underserved by in-person options. Octave's differentiation in this landscape would depend on its measurement-based care model (mandatory PHQ-9/GAD-7 outcome tracking), existing payer contracts with 14 commercial insurers, and value-based arrangements that reward clinical outcomes — advantages that are harder for marketplace-model competitors to replicate. How effectively Octave leverages these differentiators is an open question, and one that provider analytics would need to track market-by-market.

How to Enter Successfully

  • Expand payer partnerships before or alongside geographic expansion. Insurance coverage is the single largest driver of addressable market size. Adding 2–3 partners (e.g., Humana, regional Blue Cross affiliates) would increase the revenue opportunity in every expansion state without requiring new state-level operations.
  • Adapt provider recruitment for different practice environments. Octave's current states have a higher share of private-practice providers (20.7%), while expansion states lean toward outpatient centers (22.8%). Recruitment strategies need to target where the providers actually work in each new market.

How to Scale Provider Analytics Across 50 States

Identifying where to expand is the first problem. The harder problem is maintaining provider performance, clinical quality, and operational efficiency as the network scales from 23 to 50 states. Each new state introduces different practice backgrounds, patient populations, and payer mixes. At that scale, analytics must shift from reactive reporting to proactive performance management:

  • Provider performance: Caseload utilization, session completion rates, outcome trajectories (PHQ-9/GAD-7), and no-show rates — by state and provider type — to identify which profiles succeed in which markets.
  • Recruitment pipeline: Credentialing-to-first-session timelines, conversion rates, and retention — visible in dashboards so leadership can adjust resource allocation before scarcity delays market entry.
  • Model validation: Actual penetration vs. projections, actual reimbursement vs. estimates, and payer activation rates — feeding back into the scoring framework to recalibrate later expansion groups.
  • Clinical quality: Measurement-based care metrics at the cohort level — by state, provider tenure, and patient acuity — to ensure expansion maintains care quality, not just volume.

The Behavioral Health Treatment Gap

More than half of American adults with any mental illness (AMI) do not receive treatment. According to the SAMHSA National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) 2022-2023, the national treatment gap stands at roughly 55% — but this average obscures wide state-level variation, from 48% in Washington, D.C. to 64% in Mississippi. Closing this gap is the central reason companies like Octave, Rula, Alma, Headway, and Grow Therapy exist — they aim to make behavioral health care more accessible by connecting patients with licensed providers through insurance-based telehealth platforms.

This analysis uses Octave Health Group as a case study. Founded in 2018, Octave offers therapy and psychiatric services through a hybrid model of virtual telehealth and in-person clinics (San Francisco, Oakland, Los Angeles, and New York City). The company operates in 23 states plus Washington D.C., has raised approximately $88 million in total funding (Series C, 2023), and partners with 14 commercial insurance plans including UnitedHealthcare/Optum, Aetna, Cigna/Evernorth, and multiple Blue Cross Blue Shield affiliates. The question driving this analysis: if Octave were to expand to all 50 states, which of the 28 remaining states represent the strongest opportunities, and what factors should inform the sequencing? But demand alone does not determine where to expand. On the supply side, per-capita provider availability varies dramatically by state. The United States has approximately 623,000 licensed behavioral health providers across six major categories — psychiatrists, psychologists, mental health counselors, marriage and family therapists, clinical social workers, and substance abuse counselors (BLS OES 2023). Some states have both high unmet need and thin provider networks, a combination that represents both the greatest market opportunity and the greatest recruitment challenge for companies entering new markets.

Beyond demand and supply, the behavioral health workforce itself varies in composition across states, and competitive dynamics, regulatory frameworks, insurance coverage, and reimbursement rates add further complexity. The sections below explore these dimensions.

The Provider Landscape

Not all behavioral health providers are the same. The workforce includes psychiatrists, psychologists, mental health counselors, marriage and family therapists, clinical social workers, and substance abuse counselors — and the mix varies by state. Understanding this composition matters beyond recruitment strategy: different provider types bill under different CPT codes, have different licensure and credentialing requirements, and are reimbursed at different rates by payers. A state's provider mix directly affects the types of payer contracts a company can negotiate, the credentialing timelines it will face, and the reimbursement rates it can expect — all of which are key inputs for operations and analytics management.

Where Do Providers Work?

Beyond the mix of provider types, it is useful to understand where these providers are currently employed. According to the BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (May 2024), behavioral health providers work across a wide range of settings. Nationally, approximately 20% work in private practice or small group offices (NAICS 621330), another 21% in outpatient mental health and substance abuse centers (NAICS 621420), and about 5% in psychiatric hospitals. The remaining 54% are spread across general hospitals, schools, government agencies, residential facilities, individual and family service organizations, and other settings.

The distribution varies significantly by provider type. Psychologists are the most concentrated in private practice settings (39%), while counselors and social workers are more evenly distributed — roughly 13–16% in private practice, 18–19% in outpatient centers, and the majority in the broad "other" category that includes schools, government, and community organizations. Psychiatrists have the highest share in hospital settings (12%) and the lowest share in private practice (5%).

An important nuance: where a provider is employed does not determine whether they can also work on a telehealth platform. Providers in any setting — hospital-employed, facility-based, or private practice — can supplement their caseload through platforms like Octave via 1099 contractor arrangements. The BLS data also undercounts private practice because it excludes self-employed sole proprietors who are not on an employer's payroll. The true share of providers in independent practice is likely higher than the 20% reported here. This practice-setting data is useful as descriptive context for understanding the workforce, but it should not be interpreted as a measure of how many providers are recruitable to a telehealth platform.

Interstate Licensure Compacts

One factor that shapes both provider recruitment and speed to market is interstate licensure compacts — agreements between states that allow licensed mental health professionals to practice across state lines without obtaining a separate license in each state. There are three relevant compacts: PSYPACT (psychologists), the Counseling Compact (licensed professional counselors), and the Social Work Compact (licensed clinical social workers). States belonging to all three compacts enable provider credentialing in 2-4 weeks versus 12-20 weeks for non-compact states. Additionally, telehealth parity laws require insurers to reimburse telehealth visits at the same rate as in-person visits — without parity, the economics of telehealth expansion are undermined.

Knowing the provider landscape and regulatory environment tells a company who it could recruit and how quickly. But it does not answer how crowded those markets already are. The next dimension to consider is competitive intensity.

The Competitive Landscape

The telehealth behavioral health market currently has five major insurance-based platforms: Octave, Rula, Grow Therapy, Headway, and Alma. Of these, four — Rula, Grow Therapy, Headway, and Alma — already operate in all 50 states. Octave's expansion into new states means entering markets where these competitors are already established, making competitive positioning a key consideration.

However, competition in behavioral health is more nuanced than counting platforms. A company entering a new state is competing on three fronts simultaneously: competing for patients against the four nationwide telehealth platforms already serving those markets and against brick-and-mortar clinics and community mental health centers; competing for providers against those same platforms, which are also contracting with local therapists and counselors; and competing for providers who currently work in brick-and-mortar facilities. The nuance is that these dynamics are not purely adversarial — providers at existing facilities and on competing platforms often maintain multiple affiliations and can be recruited into an additional telehealth network to supplement their caseload. Even in the most competitive markets, at least 48% of adults with mental illness go untreated — though not all have commercial coverage that an in-network platform could bill (state-level commercial insurance rates range from 58–74%), so the addressable untreated market is a meaningful but smaller subset of the total gap.

To quantify competitive intensity by state, I calculated a composite score (0-100) combining three inputs: provider density per 100k (BLS OES, NPPES), brick-and-mortar behavioral health facility density from approximately 18,000 SAMHSA-listed facilities, and FCC broadband data as a proxy for telehealth viability. This score is my analytical creation, not a published metric. One notable limitation is that payer mix is not included in the competitive intensity score. Each platform has different payer partnerships — for example, Octave contracts with 14 commercial insurers, while competitors like Rula report 27+ payer relationships and Alma reports 20+. A platform's competitive position in a given state depends heavily on which payers it has contracted with relative to the state's insured population mix. This payer advantage is modeled separately in the scoring framework (the payer coverage dimension, weighted at 25%) rather than embedded in the competitive intensity score, because it is company-specific rather than a general market characteristic.

Methods

The Background tab established the landscape: a persistent treatment gap, uneven provider supply, a competitive market with five major platforms, and regulatory variation across states. The question is not whether to expand, but how to systematically prioritize which states to enter first. This section describes the scoring framework used to answer that question.

Approach

Rather than relying on intuition or population size alone, I built a multi-dimensional scoring model that evaluates each of the 28 expansion states across six dimensions drawn from publicly available data. Each dimension captures a distinct factor that influences expansion viability — from how many patients are reachable through existing payer contracts to how quickly a company can begin operating in a new state. The dimensions are normalized to a common 0-100 scale and combined using weighted averaging to produce a single composite score per state.

Estimating Payer Coverage

Payer Coverage carries the highest weight in the model because it directly determines billable patient volume — but Octave does not publicly disclose covered lives by state. To estimate this dimension, I built a two-stage pipeline using publicly available data. First, I collected state-level commercial market share figures from four sources: the AMA 2024 Competition in Health Insurance: A Comprehensive Study of U.S. Markets (state- and MSA-level shares), NAIC 2024 Market Share Reports (Accident & Health, top 125 groups by state), KFF State Health Facts for the individual, small-group, and large-group market shares, and Anthem/Elevance and other payer 10-K filings for operating-state disclosures. I then multiplied each payer's market share by the state's commercially insured adult population from Census Bureau ACS 1-Year 2023 estimates to calculate raw covered lives.

Of Octave's 14 listed insurance partners, 9 payer entities were explicitly modeled: UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, and Cigna operate nationally and were assigned market share in all 50 states and D.C.. Six regional payers were modeled in their home territories — Anthem BCBS (14 states), Florida Blue (FL), Blue Shield of California (CA), Highmark (PA, DE, WV), Horizon BCBS of New Jersey (NJ), and Health Net (CA). Managed Health Network (MHN) was folded into Health Net's California footprint because its independent market share is too small to isolate at the state level. The remaining partners — primarily smaller behavioral health carve-outs and regional plans — could not be reliably assigned state-level market share from public sources and were excluded, making this estimate conservative.

A key structural gap in the model is Blue Cross Blue Shield coverage: 32 of the 50 states and D.C. have no Octave-affiliated Blue Cross Blue Shield partner, leaving those states reliant on only UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, and Cigna — which together average roughly 27% combined market share, compared to approximately 57% in states where an Octave Blue Cross Blue Shield partner is present. This is the single largest driver of variation in the Payer Coverage dimension.

Finally, I applied a 40% network access rate to convert raw covered lives to accessible covered lives. This rate was derived by comparing the model's raw national estimate (~82 million lives across Octave's 23 current states) against Octave's self-reported figure of approximately 40 million accessible lives (per their website and recruiter materials as of March 2026), yielding a correction factor that accounts for plan-tier exclusions, narrow-network products, and behavioral health carve-out limitations.

Normalization: How Raw Values Become Scores

The six dimensions use different units — providers per 100k, percentages, dollar amounts, compact counts — so they cannot be compared or combined directly. I use min-max scaling to normalize each dimension to a common 0-100 scale, where 0 represents the lowest value among the 28 expansion states and 100 represents the highest. The formula is:

Score = (Value − Min) ÷ (Max − Min) × 100

I chose min-max over alternatives like z-score standardization (which produces unbounded scores centered on zero, harder to interpret) or percentile ranking (which preserves order but discards magnitude — two states with nearly identical values would appear as different as two states with a large gap). Min-max preserves the proportional distance between values while mapping everything to an intuitive 0-100 scale. The tradeoff is sensitivity to outliers, but the 28 expansion states have reasonably distributed values without extreme outliers across these dimensions.

Weighted Averaging: From Six Scores to One

Once each state has a normalized 0-100 score for all six dimensions, the composite score is a weighted average: multiply each dimension's score by its weight, then sum. Here is the calculation for Maine, the top-scoring expansion state:

Dimension Weight Maine's Score Weighted Contribution
Payer Coverage 0.25 80.2 0.25 × 80.2 = 20.1
Provider Pool 0.20 20.3 0.20 × 20.3 = 4.1
Unmet Demand 0.20 60.5 0.20 × 60.5 = 12.1
Regulatory Ease 0.15 97.4 0.15 × 97.4 = 14.6
Competitive Advantage 0.10 61.9 0.10 × 61.9 = 6.2
Reimbursement Favorability 0.10 25.0 0.10 × 25.0 = 2.5
Composite Score (sum of weighted contributions) 59.5

Maine scores 59.5 out of 100 — not because it excels on every dimension, but because it has strong payer coverage (80.2), excellent regulatory ease (97.4, member of all three interstate compacts), and moderate competitive advantage (61.9). Its provider pool score is low (20.3), which drags down the composite but is partially offset by the other dimensions. This illustrates a key feature of the weighted model: no single dimension determines the outcome. Kentucky scores nearly as high (59.0) through a different profile — lower payer coverage but much higher unmet demand (84.0). Alabama scores only 27.4, with near-zero payer coverage (0.6) and provider pool (0.0) that the other dimensions cannot compensate for.

From Scores to Tiers and Expansion Waves

The composite scores are used to assign each state to one of three priority tiers based on percentile cutoffs: High Priority (above 75th percentile, 7 states), Medium Priority (50th-75th percentile, 7 states), and Long-term (below 50th percentile, 14 states).

Within each tier, states are further sequenced into expansion waves using interstate compact membership as a secondary sort. The logic: compact states within a tier are prioritized first because providers can begin seeing patients in 2-4 weeks (versus 12-20 weeks for non-compact states), which means faster time to revenue. Specifically, Wave 1 consists of Tier 1 states with all three compacts (Maine, Kentucky, Delaware, Missouri, New Hampshire); Wave 2 adds the remaining Tier 1 states with two compacts (Indiana, West Virginia); Wave 3 picks up Tier 2 states with two or more compacts; and Wave 4 covers the remaining Tier 2 and all Tier 3 states. The specific state assignments and results are presented in the Results tab.

Limitations and Caveats

The following limitations should inform how results from this model are interpreted. They are documented here alongside the methodology so readers can evaluate the analytical choices and their implications before reviewing the results.

Payer-by-state activation. The model assumes Octave's existing payer partnerships extend to all states. In practice, insurance contracts may need to be activated state-by-state, and the payer mix in expansion states may differ from current states. A state with high payer coverage on paper may require significant contract renegotiation or new partnerships to activate.

Provider willingness is not modeled. Provider density measures availability, not willingness to join a specific platform. The practice-setting analysis (NB02b) provides partial insight — providers in private practice or outpatient centers are structurally more likely to have the autonomy to take on 1099 telehealth work — but the model does not estimate a provider recruitment conversion rate or account for individual willingness, compensation expectations, or geographic preference patterns. The BLS data also excludes self-employed sole proprietors not on an employer payroll, meaning the true private-practice share is likely higher than the ~20% reported.

In-person clinic decisions are not addressed. Octave operates physical clinics in four cities. The model does not inform where to locate new clinics, a decision influenced by real estate costs, local referral networks, and hybrid care demand that requires proprietary internal data.

Cost of living is not integrated. Medicare reimbursement rates and commercial rates vary by state but do not account for state-level cost of living. A provider in a lower-reimbursement state with substantially lower cost of living may be equally or more attractive than a provider in a high-reimbursement, high-cost state.

Static data snapshot. All data sources reflect 2022-2024 information. Provider counts, payer market shares, interstate compact memberships, and competitive dynamics change over time. These rankings should be treated as a starting framework for ongoing analysis, not a permanent answer.

Scoring weights reflect informed priors, not empirical optimization. The weights used (25% payer coverage, 20% provider pool, 20% unmet demand, 15% regulatory ease, 10% competition, 10% reimbursement) are not derived from historical expansion data or optimization algorithms. A sensitivity analysis varying these weights would strengthen confidence in the tier assignments.

Revenue projections are illustrative. The $95 million conservative estimate depends on the 0.5% penetration assumption and $130 per session reimbursement. Actual revenue depends on provider recruitment velocity, payer contracting timelines, patient acquisition costs, session frequency patterns, and local market execution quality. These are modeling exercises, not forecasts.

Top Opportunities in Detail

The tier table above shows that seven states rank highest, but composite scores alone don't reveal how each state gets there. Two states with nearly identical composites can have very different strengths and weaknesses — and those differences matter for operational planning. As described in the Methods tab, each state's composite score is a weighted average of six dimensions:

  • Payer Coverage (25%) — how many patients can Octave actually bill? Estimates the number of commercially insured adults in each state covered by Octave's 14 existing payer partners, based on KFF market share data and adjusted by a 40% network access rate.
  • Provider Pool (20%) — how many therapists and prescribers are available to recruit? Measures the density of licensed behavioral health professionals per 100,000 adults in each state, using BLS OES employment data.
  • Unmet Demand (20%) — how large is the treatment gap? Measures the share of adults with any mental illness (AMI) who are not currently receiving treatment, from SAMHSA NSDUH survey data. Higher gaps mean more potential patients.
  • Regulatory Ease (15%) — how quickly can Octave start operating? Scores each state on membership in interstate licensure compacts (PSYPACT, Counseling Compact, Social Work Compact) and telehealth parity laws. More compacts mean providers in neighboring states can serve patients across state lines without obtaining a separate license.
  • Competitive Advantage (10%) — how crowded is the market? Scores states based on having fewer existing behavioral health clinics and facilities per capita (from the SAMHSA Treatment Locator). Fewer existing facilities means less competition for patients — a telehealth platform can fill gaps that brick-and-mortar clinics have not.
  • Reimbursement Favorability (10%) — how much does each therapy session pay? Compares Medicare psychotherapy reimbursement rates (CPT 90837, 60-minute session) across states using CMS Physician Fee Schedule data. Higher-reimbursement states generate more revenue per session.

A state that scores high primarily on unmet demand but low on provider supply presents a different launch challenge than one with strong payer coverage but weaker regulatory ease. The radar chart below decomposes the top 5 Tier 1 states across all six dimensions to surface these differences.

Regulatory Landscape: Where Can Octave Credential Providers Fastest?

The heatmap reveals that Regulatory Ease is one of the more variable dimensions across expansion states — some score near 100 while others fall below 20. This variation matters because it directly controls how quickly providers on Octave's platform can begin scheduling patients in a new state.

An important nuance: every state already has licensed behavioral health providers living and working there. Octave could recruit those locally licensed providers and, in theory, skip the licensure question entirely. But that limits the recruitable pool to providers who already hold that state's license, which is a much smaller and more competitive talent pool — especially in underserved states where the Provider Pool dimension already scores low. The real scaling advantage of a telehealth platform is the ability to deploy providers across state lines: a therapist licensed in Virginia could see patients in Maine, Kentucky, and Missouri without relocating or obtaining three separate licenses. That cross-state flexibility is what interstate compacts unlock.

The map below visualizes each state's membership in three interstate licensure compacts — agreements between states that let licensed providers practice across state lines without obtaining a separate license in each new state:

  • PSYPACT (Psychology Interjurisdictional Compact) — allows licensed psychologists to provide telehealth services and conduct temporary in-person practice in other member states without a new license
  • Counseling Compact — does the same for licensed professional counselors (LPCs), one of the largest segments of the behavioral health workforce
  • Social Work Compact — applies to licensed clinical social workers (LCSWs), another core provider type for therapy platforms like Octave

In compact states, Octave can credential a provider from its existing network in roughly 2 to 4 weeks and begin scheduling patients almost immediately. In non-compact states, each provider must apply for a full state license — a process that typically takes 12 to 20 weeks. This means compact membership doesn't just affect regulatory convenience; it determines how fast Octave can build supply in a new market and start generating revenue.

Interstate compact membership across all 50 states and D.C. States in all three compacts (PSYPACT, Counseling, Social Work) enable 2-4 week credentialing versus 12-20 weeks for non-compact states. Source: PSYPACT, Counseling Compact, Social Work Compact rosters.

Reimbursement Geography: Where Does Each Session Generate More Revenue?

While regulatory ease determines how fast providers can start scheduling patients in a new state, reimbursement rates determine how much revenue each session generates once operations are live. Medicare does not pay a single national rate — it adjusts its base fee schedule using Geographic Practice Cost Indices (GPCI), which account for three local cost factors: physician work (labor costs), practice expense (rent, staff, equipment), and malpractice insurance premiums. This means the same 60-minute therapy session (CPT 90837) pays $173 in New York but only $130 in Mississippi.

The map below shows these state-adjusted Medicare rates for CPT 90837. Although Octave's providers bill commercial insurers rather than Medicare, Medicare rates serve as a floor benchmark because commercial plans typically reimburse at 130 to 196% of Medicare (per Milliman 2025 benchmarks). So the geographic variation carries through: a state with higher Medicare rates generally means higher commercial reimbursement as well. The pattern is clear — coastal and urban-corridor states (New York, Massachusetts, New Jersey, California, D.C.) command the highest rates, while rural and southern states fall toward the lower end. An important caveat: this does not account for cost of living. A provider in a lower-reimbursement state with substantially lower living costs may find the effective compensation equally attractive.

Recommendations

The Results tab identifies where to expand and in what order. This tab translates those findings into actionable recommendations across three areas: strategic priorities for market entry, operational levers that determine whether expansions succeed, and the analytics infrastructure needed to track execution and refine the model over time. Each recommendation is grounded in data from this analysis.

Strategic Priorities

1. Expand Payer Partnerships Before or Alongside Geographic Expansion

Payer coverage is the single largest determinant of addressable market size in each state, which is why it carries the highest weight (25%) in the scoring model. Octave's 14 commercial payer partners represent a narrower network than key competitors — Rula accepts 27+ insurance plans and Alma partners with 20+ plans across all 50 states. Even adding 2-3 major plans (e.g., Humana, Aetna Medicaid, regional Blue Cross affiliates) could significantly increase accessible lives without requiring new state-level expansion. The data shows this clearly: expansion states average a payer coverage score of only 34.1 (vs. 55.5 in current states), meaning new payer partnerships would have an outsized impact in these markets.

2. Differentiate Recruitment Strategy by State Archetype

Not all 28 expansion states need the same playbook. The scoring data reveals four distinct archetypes across the full set, each requiring a different recruitment and market-entry approach:

  • High demand + fast entry (Indiana, Kentucky, Maine, Missouri, Delaware, New Hampshire — 6 states): These top-ranked states combine strong unmet demand with favorable regulatory access and existing payer coverage. Recruitment should focus on speed — leveraging interstate compacts and above-market compensation ($107-123/hr vs. industry average ~$86-100) to onboard providers quickly. These are Wave 1-2 candidates.
  • High demand + access barriers (West Virginia, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Montana, Louisiana, Mississippi, Idaho, Arkansas, Alaska, Wyoming — 10 states): Unmet demand scores above 60, but weak payer coverage, limited regulatory access, or both. West Virginia is the outlier — its demand score (96.9) and low competition (87.3) pulled it into the top 7 despite weak regulatory access (40.8). These states require new payer contracts or state-specific licensing before entry generates meaningful revenue.
  • Mixed profiles with selective strengths (Nevada, Rhode Island, Nebraska, Iowa, Utah, Kansas, North Dakota, South Dakota, Michigan, Vermont — 10 states): No single dimension stands out as strong or weak. The entry strategy depends on which specific barrier is weakest — some need payer partnership expansion (RI, NE), others need regulatory groundwork (NV, MI). These are Wave 2-3 candidates where targeted investment in one or two dimensions can unlock the market.
  • Weak overall profile (Hawaii, Alabama — 2 states): Low scores across most dimensions with limited near-term upside. These belong at the end of the expansion sequence unless a new payer partnership or regulatory change materially shifts their profile.

Operational Levers

3. Treat Provider Recruitment as a Strategic Bottleneck

Only about 41% of behavioral health providers nationally work in private practice or outpatient centers — settings with the autonomy and scheduling flexibility for 1099 telehealth work. The rest are in hospitals, schools, and government agencies where employer policies may restrict outside contracting. And the recruitable mix varies by market: current Octave states skew toward private practice (20.7%), while expansion states lean toward outpatient centers (22.8%). Three strategies address this:

  • Cross-border compact recruitment: A therapist in Virginia can serve patients in West Virginia without relocating or obtaining a separate license, effectively expanding the recruitment radius beyond state borders.
  • Above-market compensation: Octave's $107-123/hr rates can attract outpatient-center and private-practice providers to supplement their in-person caseloads with telehealth hours.
  • Pipeline-style recruitment tracking: Treat provider recruitment like a sales pipeline with state-level metrics: outreach volume, response rates, credentialing conversion, and time-to-first-session. States with thin provider markets need visible dashboards so leadership can adjust before scarcity delays market entry.

4. Sequence Payer Activation Ahead of Provider Recruitment

Recruiting providers into a state where payer contracts are not yet activated wastes credentialing effort and provider goodwill. The data supports a deliberate sequencing: activate payer contracts first (especially in states with low payer coverage scores), then begin provider recruitment once the addressable patient population is confirmed. The revenue projections assume existing payer partnerships extend to all states, but the Limitations section in the Methods tab documents that this assumption may not hold — payer contracts may need to be activated state-by-state, and the actual accessible lives in expansion states could be materially different from the model's estimates until contracts are in place.

Analytics Infrastructure for Expansion Tracking

The scoring model in this analysis is a pre-expansion prioritization tool built on public data. Once expansion begins, the analytics team needs to track execution data that validates (or invalidates) the model's assumptions and enables real-time decision-making. The following metrics and dashboards represent the minimum analytics infrastructure needed to support a multi-state, multi-wave expansion.

State-Level Expansion Dashboard

Each expansion state should have a single dashboard view tracking the full lifecycle from market entry to steady-state operations. The key metrics to track, organized by phase:

  • Pre-launch (payer activation): Number of payer contracts activated vs. planned, accessible covered lives confirmed vs. modeled, payer activation lead time by state. This directly validates the payer coverage dimension — if actual activation rates diverge significantly from the 40% network access rate assumed in the model, tier assignments may need to be revised for subsequent waves.
  • Launch (provider recruitment): Provider outreach volume, response rate, credentialing conversion rate, time-to-first-session, and provider practice setting (private practice vs. outpatient center vs. other). Track by SOC code (MH & SA Counselors, MH & SA Social Workers, Psychologists, Psychiatrists) to understand which provider types are easiest to recruit in each market. The BLS data provides the baseline denominator — for example, Indiana has 6,713 estimated annual patients under conservative assumptions, requiring roughly 34 full-time-equivalent providers at 200 sessions/year each.
  • Ramp (patient acquisition): New patient intake volume by state and week, actual penetration rate vs. the 0.5% conservative assumption, patient acquisition cost, average sessions per patient per month, and no-show/cancellation rates. If actual penetration in Wave 1 states consistently exceeds 0.5%, the revenue projections for subsequent waves should be revised upward.
  • Steady-state (revenue and retention): Gross session revenue per state, actual reimbursement per session vs. the $130 estimate, provider utilization rate (sessions per provider per week), provider retention rate, and patient retention beyond initial treatment episode. These metrics directly feed the revenue model assumptions and should be used to recalibrate projections for later waves.

Model Validation and Recalibration

The scoring model should be treated as a living framework, not a static deliverable. As execution data accumulates from Wave 1 and Wave 2 states, four specific feedback loops should be established:

  • Payer activation rates: What percentage of theoretical covered lives actually convert to billable patients in each state? If the 40% network access rate assumption consistently over- or under-estimates reality, adjust the payer coverage dimension for remaining states.
  • Provider recruitment conversion rates: What share of licensed providers in a target state actually join the platform? The model uses raw BLS provider counts as a proxy for availability, but recruitment yield will vary significantly by state, practice setting, and provider type. Build a state-level conversion rate dataset that can replace the raw headcount proxy.
  • Competitive response patterns: How quickly do competitors (Rula, Grow Therapy, Headway, Alma) react to Octave's entry in each state? If competitors aggressively recruit providers or expand payer networks in response to Octave's market entry, the competitive advantage scores for subsequent waves may need adjustment.
  • Reimbursement realization rates: How closely do actual commercial reimbursements track the Medicare-based estimates used in the model? If commercial rates systematically exceed or fall short of the $130/session assumption, the revenue projections and reimbursement dimension scores should be recalibrated.

Wave Go/No-Go Decision Framework

Before launching each subsequent wave, the analytics team should produce a data-driven readiness assessment that answers three questions:

  • Are the current wave states on track? Compare actual revenue ramp, provider counts, and patient acquisition against plan. If Wave 1 states are materially underperforming, understand why before committing resources to Wave 2 — the issue may be systemic (e.g., the penetration rate assumption is too high industry-wide) rather than state-specific.
  • Have the model assumptions held? Surface any dimension scores that were materially wrong in practice. For example, if a state scored high on regulatory ease but credentialing took 12 weeks instead of 3, flag this for recalibrating the compact membership weighting for future waves.
  • Should the wave composition change? Execution data may reveal that a Wave 3 or Wave 4 state should be pulled forward (e.g., a new payer partnership dramatically improves a state's accessible lives) or pushed back (e.g., provider recruitment in a market archetype is proving harder than expected). The tier assignments should be dynamic, not permanent.

Conclusion

The contribution of this analysis is the framework itself. By normalizing heterogeneous public data sources to a common scoring scale and weighting them by strategic relevance, 28 potential expansion states can be systematically compared rather than evaluated ad hoc or by intuition. The model reveals a non-obvious finding: the strongest expansion opportunities are not the largest remaining states but mid-sized states where provider density, treatment gaps, regulatory ease, and payer coverage create favorable conditions simultaneously. Intuition alone, which tends to favor population size, would likely miss this insight.

For any behavioral health company evaluating geographic expansion, this type of multi-dimensional scoring approach serves as a starting point for deeper analysis using proprietary internal data. The methodology is transparent, data sources are publicly available and documented, and all assumptions are exposed for scrutiny and refinement. The complete Jupyter notebook pipeline (11 notebooks covering data collection, state analysis, expansion modeling, and visualizations) is available on GitHub for full reproducibility.

This analysis demonstrates how connecting operational challenges — provider credentialing, payer coverage, competitive dynamics — to market-level data can transform strategic expansion from a question of intuition into a question of evidence. For the analytics and data science teams supporting this expansion, the work does not end with a scoring model. The real value emerges when execution data flows back into the framework, sharpening predictions for each subsequent wave and surfacing operational bottlenecks before they delay revenue. The model is the starting point; the feedback loop is the competitive advantage.
