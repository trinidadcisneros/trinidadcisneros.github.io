Introduction

The last post priced a member: the capitation rate the state pays a health plan for each person, each month. This one follows that money to the bottom line. The rate times the members is the revenue; what remains after medical care and administration is the profit or the loss; and what has accumulated from past profits is the reserve that carries a plan through its losing quarters. Sixteen years of quarterly regulatory filings show the whole cycle for the two plans that have anchored Los Angeles Medi-Cal since 2010.

How a Medi-Cal plan makes, and loses, money

  • Revenue is set by the state. A Medi-Cal plan is paid by capitation: a fixed amount per member per month, negotiated by nobody, set by the Department of Health Care Services (DHCS) and its actuaries. The plan cannot raise its price.
  • Costs are mostly medical care. The plan pays the doctors, hospitals, and pharmacies for its members' care. What it spends on care, as a share of what it collects, is its medical loss ratio, the single most watched number in managed care.
  • The floor is 85%. Federal Medicaid rules expect a plan to spend at least 85% of its revenue on care and quality. A plan far above the floor is running thin; a plan below it is holding onto more of the public's money than the rules anticipate, and rebates or rate cuts can follow.
  • The remainder is margin, and margin accumulates into Tangible Net Equity (TNE): the plan's reserves. The Department of Managed Health Care (DMHC), the state's plan solvency regulator, sets a required minimum TNE for every licensed plan and publishes each plan's actual reserves every quarter. The distance between actual and required is the number of bad quarters a plan can survive.

The two plans, and a licensing trap worth knowing

  • The two subjects are the plans that have run Los Angeles Medi-Cal under the Two-Plan Model since the 1990s:
    • L.A. Care: the public plan, legally named the Local Initiative Health Authority for Los Angeles County, DMHC license 933 0355. A nonprofit public entity with no shareholders.
    • Health Net Community Solutions: the Medi-Cal subsidiary of Health Net, a for profit insurer (today part of Centene), DMHC license 933 0426.
  • The trap: L.A. Care appears twice in the DMHC's records. The regulator also licenses an entity called the L.A. Care Health Plan Joint Powers Authority (933 0504), a second, smaller license under the same brand. Only the Local Initiative license carries the giant Medi-Cal book, and this post uses that license alone. Anyone who queries the DMHC's tools by brand name and picks the wrong entry gets a different company's financials.
  • Kaiser Permanente, the third Los Angeles plan since 2024, is excluded on purpose: its DMHC license mixes its entire California business, commercial and Medicare included, so its filings cannot isolate Medi-Cal.
What this post asks about the business of Medi-Cal:
  • Which plan actually made money over sixteen years, how much, and in which quarters did each lose it?
  • How close does each plan run to the 85% medical spending floor, and who has ever dropped below it?
  • What does one member bring in and cost per month, and what margin is left?
  • How large is each plan's reserve cushion, and what did it look like when a billion dollars left one plan's balance sheet in a single quarter?

Conclusion and summary of results

Read as one story: the for profit plan ran the leaner machine and banked more than twice the profit; the public plan spent nearly everything on care, lost money more than five times as often, and leaned on a much deeper reserve cushion; and in the newest quarters the two stories converged, with both plans losing money in the same quarter for the first time since 2020.

2025 Q4: the first shared loss since 2020.Both plans lost money in the same quarter: Health Net Community Solutions posted a $18.9 million loss (its ratio spiked to 93.71%), then swung back to +$111.0 million by Q1 2026. Whatever pressed on Medi-Cal margins in late 2025 pressed on everyone at once.
The one that ties it together: a capitated plan's revenue is set by the state, so the whole game is played on the cost side. One plan played it for margin and banked $5.4 billion; the other played it for care intensity and banked its surplus into a reserve eleven times its requirement. Same county, same program, two different answers to what a health plan is for.
Two caveats that travel with every number here: the per member figures divide period totals by the end of period Medi-Cal enrollment times the months in the period, an approximation, because the filings publish a point in time count rather than true member months. And each license carries a small amount of non Medi-Cal business alongside the dominant Medi-Cal book, so per member figures are blended across each license's full membership.

Introduction and the question
The question: the numbers exist, the DMHC publishes them, and there is no download button anywhere. The Financial Summary Report is a web form that renders an HTML table on screen. How does sixteen years of quarterly data get out?

By speaking the application's own language. The report form is an ASP.NET page, and everything a browser sends when a human clicks Create Report can be sent by a script, once three undocumented rules are discovered. This sub tab is the story of finding them, because the finding is the method.

The three rules the form never states

1. The report lives at a different address than the form

  • Submitting the form back to its own page returns the same form, politely, with every checkbox remembered and no report anywhere.
  • The Create Report button's own click handler holds the secret: it retargets the form to flash.aspx and opens the result in a new window. Post the same payload there, and the report comes back as one flat HTML table.

2. Dates must be written as month name and year

  • The date boxes default to values like July 2025. Sending 01/01/2000 crashes the report engine outright, returning a bare error page.
  • The fix is to write dates the way the form itself does: January 2010, July 2026.

3. Ask for too much and the server gives up

  • A single request for all twenty six years runs about thirty seconds and then returns the error page: a server side timeout dressed as an error.
  • Windows of five years or less return in about twenty seconds each. So the pull is chunked: 2 plans × 4 non overlapping date windows = 8 requests, each saved as a raw HTML snapshot and a parsed CSV.
The debugging lesson worth keeping: every failed attempt returned an HTTP 200 with a friendly page, never an error code. Scraping a government form is not about handling errors; it is about reading the form's own JavaScript to learn what a real click actually sends, then reproducing it exactly.

Introduction and the question
The question: the server timeout forced the data out in 8 chunks, 2 plans across 4 date windows. How do 8 files become one table without a single quarter counted twice or dropped at a window boundary?

The same answer as the last post, deliberately: a wildcard union stacks files that describe the same kind of thing. What is new is that the chunk boundaries are a seam the data itself must prove clean.

The clean step: from scraped table to Tableau ready file

Each parsed file gets the same treatment, one clean CSV out per raw CSV in, no merging in Python:

  • Numbers become numbers: the 15 measure columns are parsed with commas stripped, parenthesized negatives honored, and blanks kept as nulls. Across all 162 rows and 15 columns, zero values failed to parse.
  • A display name: a Plan column carries L.A. Care and Health Net Community Solutions, so legends never show a forty character legal name.
  • Period fields Tableau will want: a real Period End Date, a Period Type (each filing is either Quarterly or Annual, and the two must never be summed together), Year, Quarter, and a Quarter Label like 2025 Q4.
The design rule carried through the series: Python cleans, Tableau combines. Derived measures (the per member fields, the margin) are deliberately not computed in Python; they are Tableau calculated fields, built on the next sub tab, so the workbook itself demonstrates the skill.

Introduction and the question
The question: a $3.6 billion quarter means nothing without knowing how many members it covered. How do dollar totals become the per member per month figures the industry actually speaks in, and where can that arithmetic go wrong?

PMPM, per member per month, is managed care's native unit: dollars divided by member months. The filings publish period totals and an end of period member count, so the conversion is two row level fields and three aggregate ones, with one classic mistake designed out.

Member months, then the division

Two building blocks run at the row level, one row per filing:

Months in Period = IF [Period Type] = "Quarterly" THEN 3 ELSE 12 END Member Months = [Medi-Cal Managed Care] * [Months in Period]

Then the money fields divide aggregates by aggregates:

Revenue PMPM = SUM([Total Revenue]) / SUM([Member Months]) Medical Cost PMPM = SUM([Total Medical Expenses]) / SUM([Member Months]) Admin Cost PMPM = SUM([Total Administrative Expenses]) / SUM([Member Months])
  • Why SUM over SUM matters: the wrong version, AVG of row level ratios, would average a big quarter and a small quarter as equals. Dividing the summed dollars by the summed member months weights every member month equally, which is what PMPM means.
  • Why Tableau refuses the wrong mix: because the PMPM fields are aggregate calculations, Tableau will not silently blend them with row level fields; the error message is the guardrail.

SQL

  • The approach: the identical arithmetic in one SELECT; for a single filing row the sums are just that row's values.
SELECT "Plan", "Quarter Label",
       "Total Revenue"              / ("Medi-Cal Managed Care" * 3.0) AS revenue_pmpm,
       "Total Medical Expenses"     / ("Medi-Cal Managed Care" * 3.0) AS medical_pmpm,
       "Total Administrative Expenses" / ("Medi-Cal Managed Care" * 3.0) AS admin_pmpm
FROM pf WHERE "Period Type" = 'Quarterly';

pandas

  • The approach: the same three divisions as vectorized columns.
q = pf[pf['Period Type'] == 'Quarterly'].copy()
mm = q['Medi-Cal Managed Care'] * 3
q['revenue_pmpm'] = q['Total Revenue'] / mm
q['medical_pmpm'] = q['Total Medical Expenses'] / mm
q['admin_pmpm']   = q['Total Administrative Expenses'] / mm

The honest footnote the approximation creates: the filings publish the member count at the end of the period, not true member months, so the divisor treats a quarter's membership as constant at its closing value. What the columns cannot rebuild: the ratio, and the waterfall that was not drawn

An obvious next step would be to recompute the DMHC's Health Expense Ratio from the columns, and an obvious chart would be a waterfall from revenue down to net income. The data quietly forbids both, and proving that is itself a parity result.

  • The recompute misses: 100 × Total Medical Expenses / Total Revenue deviates from the reported ratio by up to 25.72 points in the worst quarter. The regulator's definition adjusts both sides with components the summary report does not itemize.
  • So the waterfall would lie: a revenue to net income waterfall built from these columns would need an unlabeled everything else bar, sometimes as large as the margin itself: investment income, taxes, and other items the summary omits. A chart whose biggest bar cannot be named teaches confusion.
  • The decisions that followed: the dashboard displays only the DMHC's reported ratio, never a homemade one, and the waterfall waits for a future post built on the full quarterly reporting forms, where every line item exists.
The rule worth memorising: before decomposing a total, prove the parts sum to it. One MAX(ABS(difference)) query is all it takes, and here it changed what got built.
Introduction and the question
The question: two of the four panels examine one plan at a time. How does a single control drive both panels and their titles, and how does clicking a line in a completely different chart drive the same control?

Three mechanisms chain together: a parameter holds the selected plan, a boolean calculated field turns it into a filter, and a parameter action lets a click write into it. The last one is new ground for this series.

The Select Plan parameter and its filter

The parameter is a string with two allowed values, the two plan display names. A parameter cannot filter anything by itself; one calculated field connects it to the rows:

Plan Matches Selection = [Plan] = [Select Plan]
  • Where it sits: Plan Matches Selection = True is an ordinary filter on the two bottom sheets. The top sheets never see it, which is why they always show both plans.
  • The titles follow: both bottom panel titles embed the parameter token, so the words Revenue and Cost per Member per Month: L.A. Care rewrite themselves the moment the value changes.
  • In SQL terms: a parameter is a variable in the WHERE clause; in pandas, a variable in the mask. The dial is the reader turning that variable by hand.
-- SQL: the dial value lands in the WHERE clause
SELECT * FROM pf WHERE "Plan" = 'L.A. Care';   -- the parameter

# pandas: the same variable in the mask
selected = 'L.A. Care'
view = pf[pf['Plan'] == selected]

The 85% floor: a reference line treated as a rule

The ratio panel earns its readability from two small decisions:

  • A constant reference line at 85, labeled 85% MLR floor, formatted as a solid dark rule so it reads as regulation, not as another gridline. Tableau's own zero line convention is respected on the profit chart for the same reason in reverse: the default dashed zero line is already the right visual weight there, so no second line was added.
  • A fixed axis, 70 to 110. Left automatic, the axis would rescale to each plan's range and the floor would jump around the panel. Fixing the range pins 85 in place, so the eye learns where the floor lives and reads every crossing instantly.
