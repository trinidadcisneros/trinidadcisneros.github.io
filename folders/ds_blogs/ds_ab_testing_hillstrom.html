Comparing A/B Testing Methods: When to Use Each Approach

A practical guide to frequentist, sequential, Bayesian, CUPED, uplift, and bandit methods, how they differ, and how to choose between them.

Purpose of This Analysis

Goal

  • Apply ten different A/B testing methods to a single, publicly available email marketing experiment.
  • Show how each method frames the same business question, what assumptions it makes, and when one approach beats another.
  • Build a transferable framework for choosing a method based on the structure of the decision, not the defaults of a library.

What this is not

  • A race to declare one method the winner.
  • A replacement for a good experimental design document.
Key Terms
  • Arm (or variant, group): one of the versions being tested. An A/B test with two arms compares a control arm (no change) against a treatment arm (the new version). An A/B/C test has three arms, and so on.
  • Control: the arm that gets the existing experience, used as a baseline.
  • Treatment: an arm that gets the new experience being evaluated.
  • Randomization unit: the entity (user, session, device) that is randomly assigned to an arm.
  • Primary metric: the single measurement that decides whether the test won or lost.
  • Guardrail metric: a secondary measurement that must not regress, even if the primary metric improves.
  • Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE): the smallest lift the business would care about; drives sample size.
  • Lift: the difference between treatment and control on the primary metric.
  • Randomization check: verification that the arms look similar on pre test features, which is how we know the random assignment worked.
The Dataset

Hillstrom MineThatData Email Challenge

  • 64,000 customers who last purchased within the previous twelve months.
  • Random assignment into one of three arms, roughly equal sized.

Arms

  • Mens E Mail: promotional email featuring men's merchandise (~21,307 customers).
  • Womens E Mail: promotional email featuring women's merchandise (~21,387 customers).
  • No E Mail: holdout control (~21,306 customers).

Outcomes measured in the two weeks after send

  • visit: 1 if the customer visited the website.
  • conversion: 1 if the customer made a purchase.
  • spend: dollar amount spent, 0 for non converters.

Pre period covariates

  • recency: months since last purchase.
  • history: dollars spent in the prior year.
  • mens, womens: binary indicators of past category purchase.
  • zip_code: Urban, Suburban, or Rural.
  • newbie: 1 if new customer in the past twelve months.
  • channel: Phone, Web, or Multichannel.

Exploratory Analysis & Randomization Check
Exploratory
Introduction
In plain language: Exploratory analysis and the randomization check are the "pre-flight inspection" of an A/B test. Before trusting any result, you confirm the groups are actually comparable — similar recency, similar past spend, similar segments — because if the randomizer failed, every downstream p-value is meaningless. Use this step on every experiment, no exceptions. It's especially critical for new assignment systems, experiments that overlap with outages, or results that look suspiciously large. Skip it only when you've already run the exact same assignment pipeline many times and have separate monitoring that would catch a failure.

What is an A/B test

  • An A/B test is a controlled experiment that compares two or more versions of a product, message, or experience to see which one actually performs better.
  • Its purpose is to isolate cause and effect: by randomly placing each user into one of the groups (called arms), any difference in the outcome can be attributed to the version they received, not to who they are.
  • Before running the test, the first step is to explore the data: understand the outcome's shape, look for extreme values, and verify that the split between groups is truly balanced on pre test features like recency, past spend, or customer segment.
  • Random assignment is what makes the comparison fair, and the exploratory check is what proves the random assignment actually worked.

Why exploratory analysis matters

  • The job of this tab is to verify the arms are balanced before any statistical test is run.
  • If random placement worked, treatment and control should look alike on every pre period feature (recency, spend, segment).
  • Any imbalance large enough to move the outcome can bias the final estimate, even if the test itself is run perfectly.

What a randomization check catches

  • Bugs in the assignment service (a flag flipped, a cache leaked).
  • Sample ratio mismatch, where the observed split drifts from the planned split.
  • Outage artifacts, where one arm lost traffic during an incident.
  • Filtering bugs, where a downstream join drops users unevenly across arms.

Why it belongs before any hypothesis test

  • A failed balance check is a stop signal, not an adjustment knob.
  • Eyeballing outcome distributions also tells you which downstream method fits the data shape.
  • Skipping this step means every downstream p value, CI, and posterior rests on a cracked foundation.
The Math

The metric: Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)

  • SMD is the standard covariate balance check in randomized experiments.
  • It answers: "are treatment and control similar on pre period features?"
SMD formula
$$\text{SMD} = \frac{\bar X_T - \bar X_C}{\sqrt{(s_T^2 + s_C^2)/2}}$$
  • Numerator: the raw mean difference between treatment and control on a single covariate.
  • Denominator: the pooled standard deviation, which rescales the difference by how noisy the covariate is.
  • Result: a unitless score that can be compared across covariates with very different scales.

Why SMD instead of a t test p value

  • P values shrink as sample size grows.
  • At n = 60,000, a trivially small difference between arms can look "statistically significant" even though it is meaningless.
  • SMD is scale free and sample size independent, so it reflects the practical magnitude of the imbalance, not just the presence of one.
  • Use p values to test an effect on the outcome. Use SMD to check the randomization.

$$\text{SMD} = \frac{142.10 - 141.25}{\sqrt{(98.40^2 + 97.80^2)/2}} = \frac{0.85}{98.10} \approx 0.0087$$
  • |SMD| = 0.0087 is well below 0.1, so pre spend is balanced.
  • A naive t test on the same data gives p ≈ 0.37, also fine, but that p value would flip to "significant" if we doubled the sample while keeping the 85 cent difference. SMD does not.

How to interpret the number

  • |SMD| < 0.1 is the conventional threshold for acceptable balance.
  • |SMD| between 0.1 and 0.2 is a yellow flag; document it and consider stratification.
  • |SMD| > 0.2 signals the need to adjust, stratify, or investigate the assignment mechanism before trusting downstream results.
  • These thresholds come from causal inference and clinical trial literature (Cohen 1988, Austin 2009).
Assumptions & Requirements
  • Random assignment between arms.
  • Full population observed at assignment time (no post assignment filtering).
  • Pretreatment covariates measured before the email was sent.

Z Test & Welch T Test
Frequentist
Introduction
In plain language: The z-test and Welch t-test are the default A/B comparison — they check whether the gap between two groups' averages is bigger than you'd expect from random chance alone. Use the z-test for a yes/no outcome like "converted or not", and the Welch t-test for a dollar or count outcome like revenue or sessions. Classic case: did the email drive more conversions than no email? They fall short when the data is very skewed (a few whales dominate the average), the sample is tiny, or you plan to peek at results repeatedly before the test ends — in those situations reach for bootstrap CIs, Bayesian tests, or sequential methods.

What each test does

  • The two proportion z test asks whether two conversion rates could plausibly share the same underlying rate.
  • The Welch t test answers the same question for a continuous outcome like spend, without assuming equal variances.
  • Both are the default starting point for any fixed sample experiment.
The Math

Test statistics

Z statistic, two proportions
$$Z = \frac{\hat p_T - \hat p_C}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)\left(\tfrac{1}{n_T} + \tfrac{1}{n_C}\right)}}$$
Welch t statistic
$$t = \frac{\bar X_T - \bar X_C}{\sqrt{s_T^2/n_T + s_C^2/n_C}}$$

Effect sizes

Cohen's h, for proportions
$$h = 2\arcsin\sqrt{\hat p_T} - 2\arcsin\sqrt{\hat p_C}$$
Cohen's d, for means
$$d = \frac{\bar X_T - \bar X_C}{s_{\text{pooled}}}$$

  • |Z| = 7.44 is far beyond the 1.96 threshold for α = 0.05.
  • The two sided p value is essentially zero (p < 1e-13).
  • The Mens email drives a statistically significant lift in conversion.
Assumptions & Requirements
  • Independent observations under random assignment.
  • Z test: large np so the normal approximation holds.
  • Welch t test: finite variance; robust to non normality when n is large.
  • No repeated peeking without a sequential correction.

Chi-Square Test of Independence
Frequentist
Introduction
In plain language: The chi-square test checks whether two categorical things are actually related, by comparing the counts you observed to the counts you'd expect if they had nothing to do with each other. Use it when both the input and the outcome are categories — for example, comparing conversion rates across three email variants at once, or verifying that segment mix is similar across arms. It's the right tool when a z-test would force you into many noisy pairwise comparisons. It breaks down when any expected cell count drops below about five, when outcomes are ordered (use an ordinal test), or when observations aren't independent (e.g., the same user appears multiple times).

What the test compares

  • Observed counts in a contingency table are compared to counts expected under independence.
  • Large discrepancies suggest the row and column variables are related.
  • It is the right tool whenever both the exposure and the outcome are categorical.
The Math

Test statistic

Chi-square statistic
$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i,j} \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}, \quad E_{ij} = \frac{R_i C_j}{N}$$

Effect size

Cramér's V
$$V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2 / N}{\min(r-1,\ c-1)}}$$
  • V ≈ 0.1 is a small effect, 0.3 is moderate, 0.5 and above is large.

Chi-square, summing all six cells
$$\chi^2 = \frac{(268-193.76)^2}{193.76} + \cdots \approx 69.4$$
  • Degrees of freedom = (r-1)(c-1) = 1 × 2 = 2.
  • p value < 0.001, so arm assignment is clearly related to conversion.
  • Cramér's V = sqrt(69.4 / 64,000 / 1) ≈ 0.033, a small effect in magnitude.
Assumptions & Requirements
  • Observations are independent.
  • Every expected cell count is 5 or greater.
  • Variables are genuinely categorical, not collapsed continuous variables.

Confidence Intervals & Bootstrap
Frequentist
Introduction
In plain language: A confidence interval tells you the plausible range of the true effect — not just whether it's zero — which is usually what a business decision actually needs. The bootstrap builds that range by resampling your data thousands of times, making it robust when your outcome is skewed (revenue, time-on-site) or your sample is small. Use it whenever a stakeholder asks "how big is the effect, really?" instead of just "is there one?". It's a poor fit when your sample is so small the resamples can't capture the tails, when observations are correlated (bootstrap assumes they're independent), or when you need a fast, low-compute answer for a simple proportion comparison.

Why CIs matter

  • A p value tells you whether an effect is distinguishable from zero.
  • A confidence interval tells you the plausible range of the effect itself.
  • That range is what a stakeholder actually wants for the business decision.

Why bootstrap

  • Analytic CIs (Wald) assume a normal shape; they can be wrong with skewed outcomes or small n.
  • Bootstrap reconstructs the sampling distribution directly by resampling with replacement.
The Math

Analytic intervals

Wald CI for a proportion
$$\hat p \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}$$
Wilson score CI
$$\frac{\hat p + \tfrac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\tfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \tfrac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + z^2/n}$$

Resampling interval

Bootstrap percentile CI
$$\bigl[\hat\theta^*_{(\alpha/2)},\ \hat\theta^*_{(1-\alpha/2)}\bigr]$$
Example Calculation (click to expand)

Wald 95% CI for Mens conversion rate (268 of 21,307):

Point estimate
$$\hat p = 268 / 21{,}307 = 0.01258$$
Standard error
$$\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.01258 (1 - 0.01258)}{21{,}307}} = 0.000764$$
95% Wald CI
$$0.01258 \pm 1.96 \times 0.000764 = [0.01108,\ 0.01408]$$

Bootstrap procedure on the same data:

  • Resample 21,307 customers with replacement 10,000 times.
  • Compute the conversion rate on each resample.
  • Take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those 10,000 rates.
  • Result: [0.01112, 0.01411], essentially identical to the Wald CI because n is large.

Wilson interval handles rare events better and is slightly tighter on the left side: [0.01115, 0.01418].

Assumptions & Requirements
  • Wald CI: np and n(1−p) both at least 10.
  • Wilson CI: robust down to small np; preferred default for proportions.
  • Bootstrap: independent, identically distributed observations; 10,000 or more resamples for stable tail quantiles.

Sequential Testing
Sequential
Introduction
In plain language: Sequential testing lets you check results while the test is still running without inflating the false-positive rate. You pre-plan the number of peeks and use a stricter significance threshold at each one. Use it when stopping early has real value — killing a bad launch sooner, shipping a clear winner faster, or freeing up traffic for the next test. Classic case: monitoring a new checkout flow weekly so you can pull the plug if it starts hurting revenue. Skip it when you can't commit to a peek schedule in advance, when outcomes arrive with long delays that make interim looks meaningless, or when the experiment is short enough that one final analysis is sufficient.

Why peeking breaks the test

  • Each interim look gives the data a new chance to cross the significance threshold.
  • Five unplanned peeks at α = 0.05 roughly triples the overall type I error.
  • Sequential designs spend α across looks so the overall rate stays at 0.05.
The Math

Boundaries

O'Brien-Fleming boundary at look k of K
$$c_k = c_K\sqrt{K/k}$$
Pocock boundary, constant across looks
$$c_k = c_P \text{ solved to preserve overall } \alpha$$

Alpha spending

Lan DeMets function, O'Brien-Fleming family
$$\alpha^*(t) = 2\bigl[1 - \Phi\bigl(z_{1-\alpha/2}/\sqrt{t}\bigr)\bigr]$$

Assumptions & Requirements
  • Number of interim looks is planned up front.
  • Data at each look is independent of prior looks given the test statistic.
  • A clear rule exists for stopping for efficacy, futility, or safety.

Bayesian A/B Testing
Bayesian
Introduction
In plain language: A Bayesian A/B test gives you the direct probability that B beats A, plus the expected dollar cost of picking the wrong arm — both easy to translate into a business decision. Use it when stakeholders want an intuitive answer like "there's a 92% chance B wins" rather than a p-value, or when you have real historical data to seed a sensible prior. It's particularly useful for small samples where frequentist tests feel underpowered. It's a poor fit when you can't defend your prior (a strong prior can dominate a small sample), when peer reviewers or regulators expect classical hypothesis testing, or when Monte Carlo compute is too expensive for the setting.

Different question, different answer

  • A frequentist test asks "how unlikely is this data if the null is true?".
  • A Bayesian test answers the question stakeholders actually ask: "what is the probability that B beats A?".
  • It also produces an expected loss, which is the natural input to a business decision.
The Math

Conjugate update

Beta-Binomial posterior
$$p \mid \text{data} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_0 + k,\ \beta_0 + n - k)$$

Decision quantities

Probability of superiority
$$P(p_B > p_A) = \tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M \mathbb{1}\{p_B^{(m)} > p_A^{(m)}\}$$
Expected loss of choosing A
$$\mathbb{E}[L(A)] = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\max(0,\ p_B - p_A)\bigr]$$

  • Expected loss of shipping A is about 0.685 percentage points of conversion, a direct business cost.
  • No p value, no null hypothesis — the output is the probability a stakeholder actually wants.
Assumptions & Requirements
  • Prior must be defensible; Beta(1,1) is a weakly informative default.
  • Outcomes are Bernoulli per customer, independent across customers.
  • Monte Carlo draws must be large enough to stabilize tail estimates.

CUPED Variance Reduction
Advanced
Introduction
In plain language: CUPED is a variance-reduction trick. It uses a pre-experiment variable (like a customer's past spend) that's correlated with the outcome to strip out the predictable part of person-to-person noise. The result is tighter confidence intervals and a smaller sample size needed for the same power, without changing what you're measuring. Use it when you have a good pre-period covariate and need to run tests faster or with less traffic — common at Microsoft, Netflix, and Booking for mature experimentation programs. Not worth the effort when you have no pre-period data, when the covariate is only weakly correlated with the outcome (small variance gain), or when you already have so much traffic that standard tests converge quickly anyway.

The variance problem

  • Most of the noise in the treatment effect estimator comes from customer level variation, not the treatment.
  • If a covariate measured before the experiment is correlated with the outcome, it explains part of that noise.
  • CUPED subtracts out that explained variance and shrinks the standard error.
The Math

Adjustment

Optimal θ
$$\theta = \frac{\text{Cov}(Y, X)}{\text{Var}(X)}$$
CUPED adjusted outcome
$$Y^{\text{cuped}} = Y - \theta(X - \bar X)$$

Variance reduction

Variance ratio
$$\frac{\text{Var}(Y^{\text{cuped}})}{\text{Var}(Y)} = 1 - \rho_{XY}^2$$
Example Calculation (click to expand)

Use pre-period spend (X) as the covariate for post-period spend (Y). On the Hillstrom data these two are moderately correlated.

QuantityValue
Cov(Y, X)62.41
Var(X)201.38
Var(Y)462.97
Correlation ρXY0.204
Solve for θ
$$\theta = \frac{62.41}{201.38} \approx 0.310$$
Variance ratio
$$\frac{\text{Var}(Y^{\text{cuped}})}{\text{Var}(Y)} = 1 - 0.204^2 \approx 0.958$$
  • A ρ of 0.204 buys about 4.2% variance reduction, shrinking the standard error by roughly 2.1%.
  • Equivalently: the sample size needed for the same power drops by about 4%.
  • Modest here — CUPED shines when a pre-period metric tracks the outcome tightly (ρ ≈ 0.5 or higher).
Assumptions & Requirements
  • X is measured before treatment assignment; using a post treatment covariate biases the estimator.
  • X is correlated with Y; variance reduction equals ρ² between them.
  • θ is estimated on pretreatment data or via cross fitting.

Uplift Modeling
Advanced
Introduction
In plain language: Uplift modeling estimates the treatment effect for each individual customer rather than an overall average, so you can target only the "persuadables" — people who'd convert because of the email but not without it. Use it when you have customer features, enough sample to split training and evaluation, and a marketing action with real per-customer cost. Classic case: deciding who gets a discount code, so you don't hand money to customers who'd have bought anyway. It's a poor fit when the average treatment effect is tiny (per-customer estimates drown in noise), when your features don't capture what drives response, or when you only have a few hundred customers per arm.

Beyond the average effect

  • The ATE is an average over customers who may respond very differently.
  • Some customers convert regardless (sure things); some never convert (lost causes); some convert only because of the email (persuadables).
  • Uplift modeling estimates a per customer treatment effect τ(x) so the marketer can target the persuadables.
The Math

Meta learners

T Learner
$$\hat\tau(x) = \hat\mu_T(x) - \hat\mu_C(x)$$
S Learner
$$\hat\tau(x) = \hat\mu(x, T=1) - \hat\mu(x, T=0)$$
Transformed outcome (IPW style)
$$Y^* = \frac{T-e(x)}{e(x)\bigl(1-e(x)\bigr)} Y$$

Evaluation

Qini and AUUC
$$\text{AUUC} = \int_0^1 \bigl[Q(f) - f\,Q(1)\bigr]\,df$$
Example Calculation (click to expand)

A T-learner fits one model per arm and differences the predictions. Consider a single customer with pre-period spend 45 and a "mens buyer" flag.

ModelPredicted P(convert)
μ̂T(x) — fit on Mens arm0.0184
μ̂C(x) — fit on Control0.0061
Individual uplift
$$\hat\tau(x) = 0.0184 - 0.0061 = 0.0123$$

Score every customer this way, sort by τ̂(x) descending, and compute the Qini curve. The top decile's cumulative incremental conversions, versus a random targeting baseline, give the area under the Qini (AUUC).

Illustrative AUUC
$$\text{AUUC} \approx 0.0042$$
  • This customer is a persuadable: +1.23 percentage points of incremental conversion from the email.
  • The ATE across all customers is only 0.68 pp — targeting the top scorers concentrates lift.
  • AUUC > 0 means the model beats random targeting; compare to a benchmark of 0 for no signal.
Assumptions & Requirements
  • Random assignment so that τ(x) is identified.
  • Overlap: every feature combination has both treated and control observations.
  • Enough treatment signal relative to outcome noise; otherwise τ̂(x) is dominated by noise.

  • τ(x) is a difference of two noisy estimators, so variance compounds.
  • Do not evaluate uplift with per customer residuals; use Qini or AUUC on held out data.
  • If the ATE itself is small, heterogeneity estimates will be dominated by noise.
Multi-Armed Bandits
Advanced
Introduction
In plain language: A multi-armed bandit replaces the fixed 50/50 split of a classic A/B test with a policy that continuously shifts traffic toward whichever arm is currently winning. Use it when every customer served a losing variant has a real cost — lost revenue, bad user experience — and you care more about minimizing that cost than producing clean post-hoc statistics. Classic cases: news headline optimization, homepage promo selection, ad creative rotation. Avoid bandits when you need rigorous p-values for regulators or stakeholders, when conversions take days or weeks to materialize (breaks the fast-feedback assumption), or when arm performance drifts significantly over time.

Experimentation cost

  • A fixed A/B split wastes every customer shown the losing arm for the full duration.
  • A bandit reallocates traffic toward the arm that is currently winning.
  • The tradeoff: less statistical rigor for smaller regret (fewer lost conversions during the test).
The Math

Policies

ε-greedy
$$a_t = \begin{cases}\text{random arm} & \text{w.p. }\varepsilon\\ \arg\max_i \hat\mu_i & \text{w.p. }1-\varepsilon\end{cases}$$
Thompson Sampling
$$\theta_i \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_i, \beta_i),\ \ a_t = \arg\max_i \theta_i$$
UCB1
$$a_t = \arg\max_i \hat\mu_i + \sqrt{\frac{2\ln t}{n_i}}$$

Performance measure

Cumulative regret
$$R_T = \sum_{t=1}^T \bigl(\mu^* - \mu_{a_t}\bigr)$$
Example Calculation (click to expand)

Three arms (Control, Mens, Womens) with current Beta posteriors after 5,000 customers each:

Armα (successes + 1)β (failures + 1)Posterior mean
Control314,9710.0062
Mens634,9390.0126
Womens474,9550.0094

At the next step, Thompson Sampling draws one θ per arm and plays the argmax:

Thompson draw
$$\theta_C = 0.0059,\ \ \theta_M = 0.0141,\ \ \theta_W = 0.0088 \ \Rightarrow\ a_t = \text{Mens}$$

If the true best mean is μ* = 0.0126 (Mens) and the policy had played Control with μ = 0.0062 for 200 of the first 5,000 steps, regret from those plays is:

Regret contribution
$$R = 200 \times (0.0126 - 0.0062) = 1.28\ \text{expected lost conversions}$$
  • Thompson Sampling naturally shifts traffic toward Mens as its posterior tightens on the highest mean.
  • A fixed 50/50 A/B split would have accumulated ~32 lost conversions over the same horizon — orders of magnitude more regret.
  • Tradeoff: the bandit's adaptive data violates the i.i.d. assumption behind standard CIs, so post-hoc inference needs care.
Assumptions & Requirements
  • Rewards are stationary across the horizon.
  • Feedback arrives quickly enough for the policy to adapt.
  • Random tie breaking for arms with equal posterior mass.

  • Standard inference (p values, CIs) does not apply directly to adaptively allocated data.

Method Comparison & Diagnostics
Synthesis
Introduction
In plain language: Running every method on the same dataset and comparing them side-by-side is a stress test for your conclusion. If a z-test, a Bayesian model, and a bootstrap CI all land in the same neighborhood, you can trust the result more; if they disagree, that's a signal to dig into assumptions. Use this pattern whenever the stakes are high enough to warrant cross-validation — flagship launches, pricing changes, anything with large downstream impact. It's less useful when you've pre-committed to one method (regulatory setting, formal experimentation platform with a single supported test) or when running all methods requires so much custom setup that the overhead outweighs the confidence gain.

Why a synthesis

  • Every method has been run on the same Hillstrom data.
  • Side by side, their point estimates, CIs, and decisions rarely disagree, but their standard errors and decision speed do.
  • This tab makes that tradeoff visible.

One Line Summaries
Z / t tests. Right default when the design is fixed and the sample is large.
Chi-square. Right tool for cross tabs; always report Cramér's V alongside.
Bootstrap. Distribution free CIs for skewed or bounded outcomes.
Sequential. Buys early stopping at the cost of up front design work.
Bayesian. Answers the stakeholder question directly; be honest about priors.
CUPED. The cheapest single variance reduction lever when pre period data exists.
Uplift. A targeting tool, not a hypothesis test; evaluate with Qini.
Bandits. Operational decisions, not scientific ones; minimize regret, not p values.
What I Would Reach For First

Default stack

  • Z test or Welch t test for the headline result.
  • Wilson CI for the effect size.
  • CUPED when a good pre period covariate exists.
  • Bayesian summary for executive communication.

Only if warranted

  • Sequential design for long running experiments.
  • Bandits for production allocation rather than science.
  • Uplift modeling for targeting, once the average effect is confirmed.
Common Pitfalls
Peeking without a sequential correction.
Interpreting a p value as the probability the treatment works.
Applying chi-square to collapsed continuous data.
Choosing a covariate for CUPED based on in sample correlation with Y.
Evaluating uplift with per customer residuals instead of Qini.
Running a bandit when conversions land days after impression.
