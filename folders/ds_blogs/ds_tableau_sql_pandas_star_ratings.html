Introduction

What the CMS star rating is, in four lines:

  • Every year, CMS grades every Medicare Advantage plan in the country from 1 to 5 stars.
  • The rating is printed beside each plan on Medicare Plan Finder, where the roughly 34 million people in Medicare Advantage choose their coverage.1
  • It is not cosmetic: 4.0 or above earns a quality bonus payment, and a contract stuck below 3.0 for three straight years can be terminated.2
  • A half star is worth real money and real enrollment.

The catch: the star looks like one grade, but it is not one.

  • It rolls up roughly 40 separate quality measures, drawn from six unrelated data pipelines.
  • CMS does not average them. It weights them, from 1 to 5.3
  • An outcome measure counts three times what a process measure does; an improvement measure counts five times.
  • So a plan's worst measures do not all cost the same, and the heaviest ones quietly decide the score.
Project objective: how does CMS turn dozens of separate quality measures into one star rating, which measures move the number the most, and what does that mean for where a plan's real problems are?

To answer that, this project uses the public CMS Part C and Part D Star Ratings data for 2024 through 2026, and follows one contract through the scoring machinery: H1224, L.A. Care Medicare Plus, the Medicare Advantage plan run by L.A. Care Health Plan. That plan, and why it is a useful lens on the whole system, is the subject of the next section. Every chart also places it against all 516 rated plans in the country and the parent companies behind them, so the case study never sits on its own.

Specifically, this post answers five questions, using L.A. Care to make each one concrete:

  • How far is a plan from the national average, and is the gap closing?
  • Where does it sit in the distribution of every rated plan in the country?
  • Which of the 41 quality measures is it losing stars on, and is the biggest gap actually the biggest problem once the weights are applied?
  • Can a plan improve underneath a rating that never moves?
  • Which parent companies run the best and worst Medicare plans, and which are climbing?

The case study: L.A. Care Health Plan

Every finding in this post is anchored to one contract, so it is worth knowing whose contract it is. L.A. Care is not a typical Medicare Advantage insurer. It is a public agency, it exists because the State of California created it, and its Medicare plan serves the population most other plans are structured to avoid.

Why this plan makes a useful lens on the whole system:

  • It is the largest publicly operated health plan in the country.
  • Its Medicare members are dual eligible for Medicare and Medi-Cal, the poorest and often the sickest, exactly the group CMS built its hardship adjustment for.
  • It has scored a flat 3.0 stars both years it has been rated, against a national average of 3.65.4
  • Following one contract through the scoring machinery shows what the machinery does to every contract.

Why Medicare, and why this contract

L.A. Care runs four lines of business, but this project follows only the Medicare one, contract H1224 (marketed as L.A. Care Medicare Plus, an HMO Dual Eligible Special Needs Plan).

A point worth being precise about. L.A. Care's Medicare history goes back to 2008, when it launched its first dual eligible Special Needs Plan.5 But contract H1224 itself is recent. It is new enough that CMS declined to rate it in the 2024 Star Ratings, marking every measure “plan too new to be measured,” and it has a rating only for 2025 and 2026. That is why the data has three years nationally but only two rated years for L.A. Care, and why its line on every trend chart starts in 2025.

Why this line, and not one of the larger three:

  • It is the hardest line to score well on. Dual eligible members are poorer, sicker, and more likely to be disabled than the Medicare average, which is exactly why CMS built the adjustment covered later in this post.
  • It is the line with a public, national scorecard. Medi-Cal quality is measured by the state; Medicare quality is the CMS star rating, published for every plan in the country, which makes L.A. Care directly comparable to all 516 rated contracts.
  • It is small enough to read and large enough to matter. One contract, about 19,000 members, two rated years, a flat 3.0. A single thread that the whole scoring system can be pulled through.

How CMS scores a plan

This section is the subject of the study: what a Medicare star rating is, what goes into it, and how CMS turns dozens of measures into one number. The Methods section that follows is about how that system was analyzed.

What the CMS Star Ratings actually are
  • Medicare Advantage is the private alternative to Original Medicare. A private insurer signs a contract with CMS (identified by a code like H1224) and runs Medicare benefits under it. One contract can carry many individual plans.
  • Star Ratings are CMS's annual report card on each contract, from 1.0 to 5.0 in half star steps. They are published every October for the following plan year, so the "2026" ratings were released in October 2025.
  • They are not cosmetic. Contracts at 4.0 or above earn quality bonus payments, and a contract stuck below 3.0 for three consecutive years can be terminated. A half star is worth real money.
  • The rating is a rollup. Roughly 40 individual measures are each scored 1 to 5, grouped into domains, then rolled into a Part C summary (the health plan side), a Part D summary (the drug plan side), and an Overall star for contracts that offer both.
The quirk worth holding onto: the Overall star is a weighted rollup that is then rounded to the nearest half star. That rounding can swallow real movement. A plan can improve on both the Part C and Part D halves of its business in the same year and still show a flat Overall report card, because both gains landed inside the same rounding band. What that looks like for a real plan is in the results section.
Every quality measure, its weight, and why CMS weights it that way

The 2026 rating is built from these 45 measures: 33 on the health plan side (Part C) and 12 on the drug plan side (Part D). The weight is the number of times each measure counts toward the summary rating.3

The rationale, in CMS's own logic

CMS assigns weights by category, not by measure, and the categories follow a stated principle: the rating should reward what matters most to a member's health and be hardest to game.3

WeightCategoryWhy CMS counts it this muchIn 2026 (of 45)
5ImprovementHighest weight. Rewards a plan for getting better year over year, which is the behaviour the whole rating system exists to encourage.2 measures
3Outcome and intermediate outcomeDid the patient actually get healthier: blood pressure controlled, blood sugar in range, no readmission. The end that care is a means to.6 measures
2Patients' experience, complaints, and accessWhat members report and whether they can get care. Weighted down from 4 to 2 beginning in 2026, a deliberate policy change.16 measures

The 2026 change worth flagging. Patients' experience and access measures dropped from weight 4 to weight 2 this year.3 That single policy decision cut the influence of the survey based measures in half, and it is the kind of change that moves a plan's rating without the plan doing anything differently.

A worked example: what one weight does to the math

Weighting is a weighted average, nothing more exotic. Two plans, each scoring 5 stars on one measure and 1 star on another, end up in different places depending on which measure they aced:

Plan A: 5 stars on an OUTCOME measure (weight 3), 1 star on a PROCESS measure (weight 1) weighted average = (5×3 + 1×1) ÷ (3 + 1) = 16 ÷ 4 = 4.00 Plan B: 1 star on an OUTCOME measure (weight 3), 5 stars on a PROCESS measure (weight 1) weighted average = (1×3 + 5×1) ÷ (3 + 1) = 8 ÷ 4 = 2.00

Same two scores, 5 and 1, on the same two measures. Two full stars apart, purely because of which one carried the weight. That is the entire mechanism this post is about, scaled up to 45 measures.

Where each measure's number comes from

The other thing the weight table above does not show: a star rating is not one measurement. The 45 measures are produced by six unrelated pipelines, and a plan does not run most of them. Which pipeline a measure comes from decides how fast, if at all, a plan can move it.

The surprising part: a plan does not report most of what it is graded on. Only the HEDIS and plan submitted measures, roughly 17 of the 41 that a dual eligible contract is scored on, are numbers the plan produces itself. The rest come from surveys the plan does not administer, from CMS's own complaint and appeal records, or from arithmetic on the plan's prior year. A plan cannot simply decide to score better; it can change how it delivers care and then wait, sometimes two years, for a survey to notice.

Worked example: rebuilding a star from its measures, using L.A. Care (contract H1224)

This section is an example calculation, not a result. It uses one real contract, L.A. Care, to show the scoring machinery end to end: start from the individual measure stars, apply the weights and the adjustment, and land back on the summary star CMS assigned. It doubles as a check that the hand keyed weight table is right, because if a weight were wrong the rebuilt star would drift off the assigned one.

CMS builds a summary rating in four steps:

  1. Weighted mean of the measure stars
  2. Reward factor, a bonus for contracts that are both high scoring and consistent. CMS does not publish this per contract.
  3. Categorical Adjustment Index (CAI), an adjustment for the share of the contract's members who are low income subsidy or dual eligible, and the share who are disabled
  4. Round to the nearest half star
Step 3 is the one worth knowing about, and it is deliberately hard to find. The raw CMS archive does ship a CAI file, but it does not contain the adjustment. It contains only a Final Adjustment Category, a number from 1 to 9. The actual values live in a completely separate PDF. A plan's star rating therefore cannot be reproduced from the published data tables alone, which is a strange property for a rating that determines billions in bonus payments.

L.A. Care, rebuilt from its measures

All three rebuild exactly. Applied to the whole country, the same arithmetic reproduces the assigned Part C star for 83% of contracts and lands within half a star for 100% of them. The residual is the reward factor, which cannot be reproduced because CMS does not publish it.

The finding hiding in the fifth column.

L.A. Care's weighted mean on Part C is 2.712. Rounded on its own, that is a 2.5. The CAI adds 0.060, which pushes it to 2.772, and 2.772 rounds up to 3.0.

L.A. Care's 3.0 on the health plan side exists because of the adjustment. Its members are disproportionately dual eligible and disabled, CMS compensates for that, and the compensation is worth half a star. Nationally the CAI moves the rounded Part C star for 8% of contracts, and the Overall star for 12%.

Which means the obvious defence is already spent. The claim that a plan's members are poorer and sicker is true here, and CMS has already paid it out. The 3.0 is the score after the adjustment for serving that population. Whatever remains has to be explained some other way, and the gap chart is where to look.

Methods

The objective above is answered by benchmarking, ranking, and rebuilding the CMS Medicare Advantage Star Ratings for one contract against the national field, entirely on public data. This section states where the data came from, how it was handled, and how every figure was checked; the vocabulary, the rating system, the data dictionaries, and the calculation translations are nested below.

Data sources. Three public releases from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS):

  • Part C and Part D Star Ratings Data Tables, rating years 2024, 2025, and 2026. Published by CMS every October for the year ahead. Provides the contract level summary ratings and the measure level stars, and is the backbone of the analysis.2
  • Star Ratings Measures and Weights and the Technical Notes, published annually alongside the ratings. Supply the weight and the data source of each measure, neither of which appears in the data tables.3
  • Categorical Adjustment Index Measure Supplement, published annually. Supplies the adjustment values used to rebuild an assigned star from its measures.

How the data was used. The raw tables were cleaned and reshaped into two analysis files, one at the contract-per-year grain and one at the measure-per-contract-per-year grain, and a small reference table of measure weights was joined onto the measure data on measure code and year. All aggregation and transformation was done in Python (pandas), so every step is on the record and reproducible from the raw archives. The interactive dashboard was built in Tableau Public on the two analysis files.

How the figures were verified. Every number on the dashboard was computed three ways, in Tableau, in DuckDB SQL, and in pandas, and asserted against what Tableau renders on screen. 75 checks, all passing; if any figure failed to reconcile across the three tools, the build stops. The process caught two real errors before publication: 137 phantom rows from a mis-parsed CMS header, and a level of detail mistake in the SQL that produced 43 bars where Tableau draws 41.

A Special Needs Plan restricts enrollment to one of three populations: people eligible for both Medicare and Medicaid (dual eligible), people with a qualifying chronic condition, or people living in an institution. L.A. Care's Medicare contract is an SNP, a dual eligible one, which matters because the members it serves are poorer and sicker than the Medicare average.

Domain — which part of the business a measure belongs to

Nine values, and the prefix carries the meaning. HD is the health plan (Part C) side, DD is the drug plan (Part D) side. The number is the domain within that side. The full list and L.A. Care's score in each is in the Background section above.

  • The prefix is a usable filter. Domain LIKE 'HD%' isolates everything on the health plan side, which is where every one of L.A. Care's deep gaps sits.
  • Domains are not evenly sized. HD2 (Managing Chronic Conditions) holds 15 of L.A. Care's 43 rated measures on its own. A simple average across domains would badly under weight it.

MeasureCode and MeasureName — and why they disagree

  • The code is the machine key (C01 through C3x for Part C, D01 onward for Part D). The name is for humans.
  • They are not one to one. Two names are used by two codes each (Call Center – Foreign Language Interpreter and TTY Availability, and Members Choosing to Leave the Plan), because both are measured separately on the health side and the drug side. This is why the gap chart has 41 bars and 43 rated measures.
  • Codes are not stable across years. C04 meant "Monitoring Physical Activity" in 2024 and 2025, then "Improving or Maintaining Physical Health" in 2026. Joining on a measure code across years silently compares two different measures. Nothing in this post trends a measure across years, for exactly this reason.

Conclusion and summary of results

Summary of results

  • L.A. Care trails the nation by 0.7 stars. It scores 3.0 against a national average of 3.65, and it has not moved since it was first rated.
  • Three in four rated plans beat it. 382 of 516 rated Medicare contracts score above 3.0 in 2026. The most common rating in the country is 3.5.
  • The deepest gaps are care transitions and member experience. Medication Reconciliation Post-Discharge sits 2.83 stars below the national average, Care Coordination 2.50 below, Customer Service 2.47 below. Twenty six of its 41 measures are below the national average.
  • It is not weak everywhere. Reducing the Risk of Falling is 2.29 stars above the field, and the entire drug plan side is at or above average.
  • Its strengths are cheap and its weaknesses are expensive. Its two best measures are both weighted 1 by CMS. Care Coordination and Customer Service, where it is more than 2.4 stars behind, are weighted 2, and Plan All-Cause Readmissions is weighted 3. Rank by gap times weight and readmissions, the sixth biggest raw gap, becomes the biggest opportunity, while Medication Reconciliation, the biggest bar on the chart, leaves the top five.

Conclusion

Read as a single grade, a 3.0 star rating says a plan is mediocre and leaves it there. Taken apart, the number tells a more specific and more useful story, and three things stand out.

  • The flat rating hides real movement. Both halves of L.A. Care's business gained half a star in a single year, and the rounded Overall number absorbed all of it. A plan can be improving and stalled on paper at the same time. Anyone reading the headline star alone would miss it.
  • The hardship defence is already spent. The 3.0 on the health plan side is not the raw score; it is the raw score after CMS has already added an adjustment for serving a poorer, sicker, more disabled population. Without it the plan would sit at 2.5. The argument that a plan's members are harder to serve is true here, and it has already been credited, so it cannot also explain what remains.
  • The biggest gap is not the biggest opportunity. Because CMS weights measures from 1 to 5, the measure a plan is furthest behind on is often not the one costing it the most stars. For L.A. Care the largest raw gap is Medication Reconciliation (weight 1), but the largest weighted loss is Plan All-Cause Readmissions (weight 3), only its sixth largest gap. Ranking by raw distance points at the wrong problem.

Recommendations

  • For reading any plan's rating: never stop at the Overall star. Split it into Part C and Part D, check whether the halves moved, and look at the measure level before concluding anything about quality.
  • For a plan trying to raise its rating: rank the gaps by gap times weight, not by gap alone. The Gap Explorer toggle on the dashboard exists for exactly this, and it moves readmissions and the triple weighted medication adherence measures to the top of the list.
  • For an analyst working with this data: the weights, the data sources, and the adjustment values are all published outside the data tables. Any honest analysis of Star Ratings has to join those reference tables in, or it will silently treat a triple weighted outcome as equal to a single weighted checkbox.
The one line to keep. CMS does not average a plan's quality measures, it weights them, so a plan's rating is decided less by where it is weakest and more by whether it is weak on the measures that count the most. The single star on Plan Finder is a summary of that, and the summary hides exactly the detail a plan would need to act on.

Introduction and the question
The question: the chart is filtered to one contract. How can it still show the average of all 516?

The problem a FIXED expression solves. Drag AVG(Overall Stars) into a view that is filtered to L.A. Care and Tableau will faithfully give the average of the rows in the view: 3.0. That is L.A. Care's own score, not a benchmark. To draw a line at 3.65 the calculation has to be computed at a level of detail the view does not have, and it has to be immune to the view's filter.

That is what FIXED means. It fixes the grouping in the expression itself, so the view cannot change it, and it runs before ordinary dimension filters, so those filters cannot shrink what it sees.

Roadmap for this tab:

  • The benchmark, one dimension: the national average star
  • The scalar trick: pull one specific plan's value onto every row so every other plan can be compared to it
  • The benchmark at two dimensions: a national average per measure, which is where the most useful trap in the project lives
The benchmark: a FIXED LOD on one dimension
National Avg Star = { FIXED [Year] : AVG([Overall Stars]) }

Read it left to right: for each Year, take the average of Overall Stars, and hand that number back to every row of that year. The sheet filter (Contract = H1224) is applied afterwards, so the line stays at 3.65 even though only one contract survives the filter.

SQL

  • The approach: a window function, AVG(...) OVER (PARTITION BY Year), computed inside a CTE that has no filter on it.
  • Why the CTE matters: the CTE boundary is what makes the window blind to the outer WHERE. Move that WHERE inside the CTE and the benchmark collapses. That move is exactly what "Add to Context" does in Tableau, and it is covered on the last tab.
  • What comes back: one row per year for L.A. Care, each carrying the national average of all 516 contracts.
WITH benchmark AS (                       -- runs BEFORE the contract filter, like FIXED
    SELECT Year, Contract, Overall_Stars,
           AVG(Overall_Stars) OVER (PARTITION BY Year) AS national_avg_star
    FROM summary
)
SELECT Year, national_avg_star
FROM benchmark
WHERE Contract = 'H1224'                -- the sheet filter, applied AFTER the window
ORDER BY Year;

pandas

  • The approach: groupby('Year').transform('mean') broadcasts the year level average back onto every row.
  • Why transform and not agg: agg collapses the frame to one row per year. transform keeps the frame's shape and glues the group's answer onto each row, which is precisely the shape a FIXED LOD produces.
  • The filter comes last, after the broadcast, so the benchmark column already holds the national number by the time L.A. Care is selected.

The scalar trick: pulling one plan's value onto every row

The distribution chart needs to color each bar by whether that group of plans beats L.A. Care. To do that, every contract's row needs L.A. Care's score sitting next to its own. A FIXED LOD with a conditional inside it does that.

LA Care Star = { FIXED [Year] : MAX(IF [Contract] = "H1224" THEN [Overall Stars] END) } Beats L.A. Care = [Overall Stars] > [LA Care Star]

The IF returns L.A. Care's star on L.A. Care's row and null everywhere else. MAX then ignores the nulls and returns the one non null value, which the FIXED broadcasts to every row of that year. The second calculation is an ordinary row level comparison, and it goes on Color.

The pattern worth memorising: { FIXED [dim] : MAX(IF [member condition] THEN [value] END) } is how you get "the value belonging to one specific member" onto all rows. It is the LOD equivalent of a lookup.

SQL

  • The approach: a windowed MAX(CASE WHEN ...) broadcasts L.A. Care's star across the year's partition, then a simple comparison decides the color and a COUNT(DISTINCT) gives the bar heights.
  • The null filter is deliberate: unrated contracts are excluded from the chart entirely, so they are excluded from the count too.

    WHERE Overall_Stars IS NOT NULL          -- unrated contracts are not in the chart

A FIXED LOD on two dimensions, and the trap inside it

The measure gap chart needs a different benchmark for every measure: L.A. Care's Medication Reconciliation score should be compared to the national average on Medication Reconciliation, not to the national average overall. So the LOD pins two dimensions.

National Avg by Measure = { FIXED [Year], [Measure Code] : AVG([Stars]) } Gap vs National = AVG([Stars]) - AVG([National Avg by Measure])

SQL

  • The approach: the same window function, now partitioned by two columns instead of one, computed over the unfiltered measure table.
  • Then the view: the outer query filters to L.A. Care and groups by measure name, averaging both the plan's stars and the benchmark. That second aggregation is not decoration, and the next box explains why.
WITH benchmark AS (                     -- the LOD: level = Year, Measure CODE
    SELECT Year, Contract, MeasureCode, MeasureName, Stars,
           AVG(Stars) OVER (PARTITION BY Year, MeasureCode) AS national_avg_by_measure
    FROM measures
    WHERE Stars IS NOT NULL
)
SELECT MeasureName,                       -- the VIEW: level = Measure NAME
       AVG(Stars)                                AS la_care_stars,
       AVG(national_avg_by_measure)              AS national_avg,
       AVG(Stars) - AVG(national_avg_by_measure) AS gap_vs_national
FROM benchmark
WHERE Contract = 'H1224' AND Year = 2026
GROUP BY MeasureName
ORDER BY gap_vs_national;

pandas

  • The approach: two steps, in this order. First groupby(['Year','MeasureCode']).transform('mean') for the benchmark, on the unfiltered frame. Then groupby('MeasureName').agg(...) for the view.
  • Both terms are averaged at the view's level, which is what AVG([Stars]) - AVG([National Avg by Measure]) literally says.
m = measures[measures.Stars.notna()].copy()

# the LOD: benchmark computed per measure CODE
m['national_avg_by_measure'] = (m.groupby(['Year', 'MeasureCode'])['Stars']
                                 .transform('mean'))

# the VIEW: aggregated per measure NAME
view = (m[(m.Contract == 'H1224') & (m.Year == 2026)]
        .groupby('MeasureName')
        .agg(la_care_stars=('Stars', 'mean'),
             national_avg=('national_avg_by_measure', 'mean')))

view['gap_vs_national'] = view.la_care_stars - view.national_avg

Difference From, Relative to First

The change table shows each company's average star relative to 2024, its first year in the data. In Tableau that is not a calculated field at all, it is a setting on an existing pill:

  • Drop AVG(Overall Stars) on Text
  • Right click it → Quick Table Calculation → Difference
  • Right click again → Edit Table Calculation
  • Compute Using: Table (across)  ·  Relative to: First

Those two settings are the whole calculation. Compute Using says which direction to walk the view, and Relative to says what to subtract.

SQL

  • The approach: aggregate to one row per company per year first, then use FIRST_VALUE(...) OVER (PARTITION BY parent_org ORDER BY Year) to put each company's 2024 baseline on every one of its rows, and subtract.
  • The mapping is exact: FIRST_VALUE is "Relative to: First". PARTITION BY parent_org is "Compute Using: Table (across)", because across means "walk the columns within a row of the table".
  • Why aggregate first: a table calculation operates on the view's numbers, and the view's numbers are already aggregated. The inner GROUP BY is the view.
WITH yearly AS (                                -- this CTE is "the view"
    SELECT ParentOrgGroup AS parent_org, Year,
           AVG(Overall_Stars) AS avg_star
    FROM summary
    WHERE Overall_Stars IS NOT NULL
    GROUP BY 1, 2
)
SELECT parent_org, Year, avg_star,
       avg_star - FIRST_VALUE(avg_star) OVER (      -- Difference From, Relative to First
           PARTITION BY parent_org                    -- Compute Using: Table (across)
           ORDER BY Year
       ) AS diff_since_first
FROM yearly
ORDER BY parent_org, Year;

pandas

  • The approach: aggregate to company and year, sort by year, then group by company and subtract the first value in each group.
  • v.iloc[0] is FIRST_VALUE, and it only means "2024" because the frame is sorted by year first. A table calculation is order dependent, and so is this.
yearly = (summary[summary.Overall_Stars.notna()]
          .groupby(['ParentOrgGroup', 'Year'], as_index=False)
          .Overall_Stars.mean()
          .sort_values(['ParentOrgGroup', 'Year']))          # order matters

yearly['diff_since_first'] = (yearly.groupby('ParentOrgGroup').Overall_Stars
                                 .transform(lambda v: v - v.iloc[0]))
The self check: every company's 2024 cell must be exactly 0.0. That is not a coincidence, it is the definition of "relative to first". If a 2024 cell is non zero, the Compute Using direction is wrong.

Parameters, and the boolean bridge they need

The problem the parameter solves. Rank parent companies by average star with no minimum, and the leaderboard is nonsense: 69 of the 137 parent organizations have exactly one rated contract, and three of the "5.0 star" leaders are single contract companies that got lucky once. But hard coding a minimum of 5 is an arbitrary choice made on the reader's behalf. A parameter hands the choice over.

Parameter: Min Rated Contracts (Integer, range 1 to 10, step 1) Rated Contracts = COUNTD(IF NOT ISNULL([Overall Stars]) THEN [Contract] END) Meets Min Contracts = [Rated Contracts] >= [Min Rated Contracts] ← the bridge
Why the bridge exists. A parameter is inert. It does nothing at all until something references it. The obvious move, dragging Rated Contracts to the Filters shelf and typing the parameter into the "at least" box, does not work: a measure filter's value box will not accept a parameter. It wants a literal number. The way through is to wrap the comparison in a calculated field that returns True or False, and filter that field to True. The boolean is the bridge between the parameter and the filter.

SQL

  • The approach: aggregate per company, then filter on the aggregate with HAVING. The parameter becomes a bind variable.
  • HAVING is the boolean bridge. It is SQL's measure filter: a condition evaluated after aggregation, on an aggregate. WHERE cannot do it, because at WHERE time the count does not exist yet.
  • COUNT(DISTINCT Contract) with a null filter reproduces Rated Contracts exactly. Counting all contracts instead of rated ones would let a company qualify on plans that were never scored.
SELECT ParentOrgGroup            AS parent_org,
       COUNT(DISTINCT Contract)  AS rated_contracts,
       AVG(Overall_Stars)        AS avg_overall_stars
FROM summary
WHERE Year = 2026 AND Overall_Stars IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY ParentOrgGroup
HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT Contract) >= ?    -- the parameter, as a bind variable
ORDER BY avg_overall_stars DESC;

pandas

  • The approach: groupby().agg() for both numbers at once, then a boolean mask on the count.
  • The mask after the aggregation is the HAVING, and it is the same thing as the Tableau boolean field. Three tools, one idea: filter the aggregate, not the rows.
MIN_CONTRACTS = 7                                        # the parameter

d = summary[(summary.Year == 2026) & summary.Overall_Stars.notna()]

ranked = (d.groupby('ParentOrgGroup')
            .agg(rated_contracts=('Contract', 'nunique'),
                 avg_overall_stars=('Overall_Stars', 'mean'))
            .reset_index())

ranked = ranked[ranked.rated_contracts >= MIN_CONTRACTS]   # HAVING
ranked.sort_values('avg_overall_stars', ascending=False)

Weighting the gap: a reference join, and a parameter that swaps the measure
The question: the gap chart ranks 41 measures by how far behind L.A. Care is. But CMS does not treat those measures as equal. So is the biggest gap actually the biggest problem?

How CMS actually builds a star rating

This is worth stating precisely, because almost every chart made from this data gets it wrong. CMS does not average the measure stars. It runs four steps:

1. Weighted mean
each measure counted 1, 2, 3, or 5 times
2. Reward factor
bonus for high and consistent performance
(not published)
3. Categorical Adjustment Index
adjustment for the share of members who are low income or disabled
4. Round
to the nearest half star

Step 1 is the one this chart cares about. The weights come from the measure's category, and they span a factor of five:

WeightCategoryWhat CMS is sayingExample
1ProcessDid the plan do the thing it was supposed to do?Breast Cancer Screening, Medication Reconciliation
2Patients' experience, complaints, and accessWhat do members say, and can they get in the door?Care Coordination, Customer Service
3Outcome and intermediate outcomeDid the patient actually get better?Plan All-Cause Readmissions, Controlling High Blood Pressure
5ImprovementDid the plan's own scores move since last year?Health Plan Quality Improvement

The logic is defensible: CMS pays most for outcomes and for movement, least for box ticking. Whether it is right is a policy question. That it is true is a data question, and it changes the chart.

None of this is in the data. The Star Ratings tables ship the measure stars and nothing else. The weights are published in a separate PDF, the data sources in a second one, and the adjustment values in a third. To weight a gap chart correctly you have to leave the dataset, key a reference table by hand, and join it back on. That is not an unusual situation, and it is the reason this section exists.

Step 1: the relationship (and why one key is not enough)

  • The approach: the measure weights reference table is added to the measure data source and related on two fields, Measure Code and Year.
  • Why both: weights change between rating years. Patients' Experience measures were weight 4 before 2026 and weight 2 from 2026 on. The reference file holds only 2026, so joining on Measure Code alone would quietly stamp 2026 weights onto 2024 rows.
  • Tableau relationship, not a join: Tableau decides the join type per sheet based on what is in the view, so unmatched years come back null instead of vanishing. In SQL, the equivalent choice is LEFT JOIN versus INNER JOIN, and here the sheet is filtered to 2026 anyway, so the distinction does not bite.

Step 2: the weighted gap

Weighted Gap = [Gap vs National] * AVG([Weight])

The gap is already an aggregate, so the weight has to be aggregated too. AVG and not SUM: the two measures that carry two codes each would otherwise come back with double their weight.

Step 3: a parameter that swaps which field the bars plot

Rank By = a String parameter, list: "Raw gap" | "Weighted gap" Gap (Rank By) = IF [Rank By] = "Raw gap" THEN [Gap vs National] ELSE [Weighted Gap] END
The pattern worth keeping. A parameter cannot be plotted. It has no data in it. It only becomes visible when a calculated field reads it, and that field is what goes on the shelf. So the chart is plotting one field whose definition changes underneath it. The same trick swaps a dimension, a date grain, or a whole metric.

One thing that will catch you: the sort has to be re pointed at Gap (Rank By) as well. Sort it by Gap vs National and the bars change length when the toggle flips but stay in the old order, which is worse than not having the toggle at all.

SQL

  • The join approach: an ordinary JOIN ... USING (MeasureCode, Year) against the reference table, inside the same CTE chain as the LOD.
  • The parameter approach: a CASE in the SELECT list, driven by a bind variable. The parameter is a query variable and nothing more exotic than that.
  • The order matters: the benchmark window runs first (over the unfiltered table), the join comes second, and the view aggregation last.
WITH benchmark AS (                     -- the FIXED LOD, per Year and Measure Code
    SELECT Year, Contract, MeasureCode, MeasureName, Stars,
           AVG(Stars) OVER (PARTITION BY Year, MeasureCode) AS national
    FROM measures
    WHERE Stars IS NOT NULL
),
joined AS (                            -- the relationship: BOTH keys, not just the code
    SELECT b.*, w.Weight
    FROM benchmark b
    JOIN weights w USING (MeasureCode, Year)
),
view AS (                              -- the view: aggregated to Measure NAME
    SELECT MeasureName,
           AVG(Stars) - AVG(national) AS raw_gap,
           AVG(Weight)                 AS weight
    FROM joined
    WHERE Contract = 'H1224' AND Year = 2026
    GROUP BY MeasureName
)
SELECT MeasureName, raw_gap, weight,
       raw_gap * weight AS weighted_gap,
       CASE WHEN ? = 'Raw gap'                -- the parameter, as a bind variable
            THEN raw_gap
            ELSE raw_gap * weight
       END AS gap_rank_by
FROM view
ORDER BY gap_rank_by;

  • The join approach: merge on both keys. Passing a list to on= is the whole difference between right and wrong here.
  • The parameter approach: a plain Python variable choosing which column to sort by. In pandas the "parameter" is so ordinary it barely has a name, which is exactly why it is a useful way to see what Tableau is doing.

Why the size filter had to be a FIXED LOD

The change table needs the same size threshold. The obvious version, a plain COUNTD([Contract]) compared against the parameter, produces a broken chart, and the reason is worth the detour.

Big Company = { FIXED [Parent Org (group)] : COUNTD(IF [Year] = 2026 AND NOT ISNULL([Overall Stars]) THEN [Contract] END) } >= [Min Rated Contracts]
  • The view has Year on Columns. A view level COUNTD therefore counts contracts per company per year, not per company.
  • The consequence: a company with 6 rated plans in 2024 and 8 in 2026 would pass the threshold in some columns and fail in others. Its 2024 cell would simply not render, its baseline would silently become 2025, and its "change since first" would be measuring the wrong thing.
  • FIXED pins the count to the company, computed once, at the company grain, regardless of what is in the view. The filter then either lets a company in for all three years or none.

The SQL says it plainly: the eligibility test is a separate GROUP BY ParentOrgGroup with no year in it, joined back to the yearly figures. It cannot accidentally acquire a year, because the year is not in the grouping.

WITH big AS (                                   -- the FIXED [Parent Org] LOD: no Year in the GROUP BY
    SELECT ParentOrgGroup
    FROM summary
    WHERE Year = 2026 AND Overall_Stars IS NOT NULL
    GROUP BY ParentOrgGroup
    HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT Contract) >= ?
)
SELECT s.ParentOrgGroup, s.Year, AVG(s.Overall_Stars) AS avg_star
FROM summary s
JOIN big USING (ParentOrgGroup)                -- eligibility decided once, applied to every year
WHERE s.Overall_Stars IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY 1, 2;
The general rule: if a filter's answer should not depend on what is in the view, it has to be FIXED. Anything else is a filter that changes its mind column by column.
Introduction and the question
The question: two things in Tableau change your numbers without ever appearing in a calculation. What are they, and where do their changes actually live?

This tab covers the two ideas that are easiest to get wrong precisely because they do not look like calculations.

  • Context filters change when a filter runs. Get it wrong and a benchmark quietly collapses onto itself.
  • Groups and aliases change what the data looks like without touching the file underneath. One of them changes numbers. The other never does. Knowing which is which decides whether the data prep and the workbook can ever agree.
Context filters: moving the WHERE into the CTE

Tableau runs things in a fixed order. The only part that matters for this post is where the two kinds of filter sit relative to a FIXED expression.

1. Extract filters
rows never loaded
2. Context filters
the grey ones
3. FIXED LODs
your benchmark is computed HERE
4. Dimension filters
ordinary pills on the Filters shelf
5. Measure filters
HAVING
6. Table calcs
on the result set

A FIXED expression sits between the two kinds of filter. Everything above it can shrink what it sees. Everything below it cannot.

So a context filter is not a performance setting. It is a decision about what a FIXED expression is allowed to see, and it is one right click away from silently changing every benchmark on the sheet.

The translation, and the only sentence worth memorising from this tab: Add to Context = move the WHERE into the CTE.

The demonstration

  • The setup: the same national benchmark, computed twice, on the same data, with the same window function. The only difference is which side of the CTE boundary the contract filter sits on.
  • Without context: the window runs over all 516 rated contracts, then the filter picks out L.A. Care. The benchmark is 3.65. This is what the dashboard shows.
  • With context: the filter runs first, so the window only ever sees one contract, and the "national average" becomes L.A. Care's own 3.0. On the chart the two lines land on top of each other and the benchmark disappears.

)
SELECT DISTINCT national FROM benchmark
WHERE Year = 2026 AND Contract = 'H1224';      -- filter runs AFTER the window

    FROM summary
    WHERE Contract = 'H1224'                     -- Add to Context
)

WHERE Year = 2026;

Data prep inside Tableau: groups and aliases

Two of the CMS quirks were fixed inside the workbook rather than the data prep, deliberately, because the exam tests them and because the difference between the two is the difference between a cosmetic change and a wrong number.

What was actually wrong with the parent company field

  • CMS truncates Parent Organization at 50 characters. "Santa Barbara San Luis Obispo Regional Health Auth" is not a typo, it is a limit.
  • Twelve names are in ALL CAPS while the rest are title case.
  • One company is spelled two ways. Molina Healthcare, Inc. and Molina Healthcare, Inc., differ by a trailing comma. Same for Samaritan Health Services and Samaritan Health Services, Inc.

Two hundred and nineteen distinct parent organizations become 217 once the duplicates are merged. Molina's rated plan count goes from a split 12 and 1 to a single 13, which is the difference between clearing a threshold of 7 and not appearing on the chart at all.

Two gotchas found the hard way

Aliases do not carry into a group. Alias the ALL CAPS names on Parent Org, then build Parent Org (group) from it, and the group comes back shouting again. The group is a separate field with its own member list, built from the underlying values, not from the labels. Group first, then relabel, and relabel from inside the Edit Group dialog.

A group field has no Aliases option at all. To rename a single member of a group, make it a group of one, then rename that group. It looks like a workaround because it is one.

Where the changes actually exist: a data flow

The most common confusion about Tableau's cleaning tools is spatial: people are not sure what they have edited. The answer is that nothing upstream is ever touched.

  • Tableau Public accepts extracts only. A live database connection cannot be published there. Whatever is on Tableau Public is a snapshot, taken at the moment of publishing.
