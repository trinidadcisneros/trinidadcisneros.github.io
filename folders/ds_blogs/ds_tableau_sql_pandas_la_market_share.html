Introduction

Health insurance is one of the largest pieces of the US health care system, and for most people it is how medical care gets paid for. Rather than pay the full cost of a doctor visit, a test, or a hospital stay yourself, you (or someone on your behalf) pay a health plan a set amount, and in return the plan covers most of the bill when you need care.

Where coverage comes from

  • Employer sponsored is the most common: your job offers a plan and the employer and worker split the cost. This is commercial insurance, coverage sold by private companies.
  • Individual market is commercial insurance you buy yourself, often through a government run marketplace, when a job does not provide it.
  • Medicare is public (government) insurance, mainly for people 65 and older, with a few exceptions such as certain disabilities.
  • Medicaid is public insurance for people with low incomes, of any age.

Why public insurance matters

  • Commercial coverage only reaches people with a job that offers it or the means to buy it.
  • Public insurance fills the gap, a way to see a doctor for people who would otherwise be uninsured.
  • Medicaid is the broadest of these programs. Unlike Medicare, which is tied to age, it is based on income and need, so it covers children, working age adults, and seniors alike.

Medicaid, in brief

  • It operates in all 50 states and the District of Columbia and covers more than 70 million people.
  • It is jointly funded: the federal government pays a large share and each state pays the rest.
  • Every state runs its own program within federal rules, which is why it goes by different names in different states.

California's Medicaid is Medi-Cal

  • Medi-Cal is the largest Medicaid program in the country, covering roughly 14 million people, about a third of the state.
  • Members get their care one of two ways. In fee for service, the state pays each doctor or hospital bill directly. In managed care, the state hires a health plan and pays it a fixed amount per member each month, called capitation (a set payment per person, whether or not they use care that month), and the plan takes responsibility for that member's care.
  • Most members are in managed care, and that is where the plans compete. This post is about the managed care side.

Where Medi-Cal is most concentrated

  • Medi-Cal reaches all 58 California counties, but it is densest in the large urban and inland ones.
  • Los Angeles County is the biggest of all: the largest county Medicaid program in the country, more than three million Medi-Cal managed care members, more than the entire population of many states.

The market inside one county

  • Within a county, Medi-Cal managed care members pick from a short list of plans. The plan with the most members holds the most market share (its slice of the county's Medi-Cal managed care members) and therefore the most capitation revenue (the per member payments it collects from the state).
  • This is a market inside one public program, not a ranking of every insurer in Los Angeles. Employer and marketplace plans cover different people and are not counted here.
What this post asks about Los Angeles Medi-Cal:
  • Who holds the market across the plans, and has that changed over 19 years?
  • Is the largest plan also the highest quality?
  • How heavily does each county lean on Medi-Cal, and where does that coverage run ahead of the supply of providers (the doctors, clinics, and specialists contracted to serve members)?

Why Health Net and Kaiser appear in a public program

  • The state does not run the health plans itself. It hires plans to run Medi-Cal for it and pays each one per member.
  • Some of the plans it hires are public agencies and some are commercial companies, but the coverage they run here is Medi-Cal either way, so they compete on equal terms for the same members.
  • Los Angeles has three: L.A. Care, Health Net, and Kaiser Permanente. How they came to serve the county is the next section.

How members choose a plan, and the three that serve Los Angeles

How someone ends up in one of these plans

  • A person qualifies for Medi-Cal on income and enrolls through the county or state.
  • Enrollment is year round. Unlike employer or marketplace coverage, which is bought during an annual open enrollment window, Medi-Cal has no such window, you can apply the moment you qualify.
  • Once enrolled, they get a choice form listing the Medi-Cal managed care plans in their county, and have 30 days to pick one.
  • If they do not pick, the state assigns one for them, keeping them with their current doctor where it can.
  • Switching plans later runs through an annual plan enrollment window, with exceptions allowed for special circumstances.
  • This choice is only about which Medi-Cal plan runs their coverage. It does not touch people with employer or marketplace insurance, who are not in Medi-Cal at all.

California's Two-Plan Model, in Los Angeles

  • The Two-Plan Model is one of the ways California organizes its Medi-Cal managed care, used in a number of counties. This is how it works in Los Angeles.
  • When California moved Los Angeles Medi-Cal into managed care in 1997, it set up two competing Medi-Cal plans so members would always have a choice between them.
  • One is a Local Initiative, a public plan the state created for the county. The other is a Commercial Plan, an existing private insurer the state selected through competition. "Commercial" describes the company, not the coverage, both run Medi-Cal.
  • A third plan, Kaiser Permanente, began contracting directly in 2024, so Los Angeles Medi-Cal members today choose among three.
  • The model governs only the Medi-Cal choice. Angelenos with other coverage were never limited to these plans.

Those plans sort into two Medi-Cal plan models, by who runs them:

Model 1 — L.A. Care, the public Local Initiative

  • The state created L.A. Care in 1997 as a new public agency, the Local Initiative Health Authority for Los Angeles County, to be the county's public Medi-Cal plan.
  • It is now the largest publicly operated health plan in the country. Medi-Cal is essentially its whole business, and it has run continuously since 1997, which is why the data carries it from the first month.

Model 2 — Commercial companies that also contract to run Medi-Cal

  • These are private insurers best known for employer and marketplace coverage, but they also sign contracts with the state to run Medi-Cal lines. The coverage is the same public Medi-Cal; the difference is the kind of company behind it.

Health Net

  • An existing commercial insurer the state selected, through a competitive process, as the county's Commercial Plan in 1997.
  • It runs its Medi-Cal line as Health Net Community Solutions, and like L.A. Care has been in the data from the start.

Kaiser Permanente

  • Kaiser served Los Angeles Medi-Cal members for years, but underneath the larger plans as a subcontractor, not a plan members could choose directly.
  • That changed in January 2024, when Kaiser signed a direct Medi-Cal contract with the state across 32 counties. That is why Kaiser appears as its own line only from 2024, and why it does not yet have a Los Angeles quality score.
One line to keep: all three are Medi-Cal plans competing for the same members. In a capitated model members are revenue, so holding the county is the business, for the public plan and the commercial ones alike.

Conclusion and summary of results

Read as a single story, top to bottom: the Los Angeles Medi-Cal market grew, L.A. Care came to lead it on both size and quality, and the deeper a county's reliance on Medi-Cal runs, the thinner its provider network tends to be.

Introduction and the question
The question: why does the access map's size filter have to be a context filter, and not an ordinary one?

The access ratio is providers divided by members. In a county with only a few hundred members, one busy clinic can push the ratio to an absurd height and hijack the color scale, hiding the real pattern across the large counties. A minimum member filter fixes that, but only if it runs before the ratio and the color quantiles are computed. That ordering is what a context filter guarantees.

Context filters: moving the filter before the calculation

What Add to Context does. An ordinary Tableau filter runs late, after aggregates and quantiles are formed. Promoting a filter to context runs it early, so everything downstream, the ratio, the percentile coloring, sees only the surviving rows. In SQL the analog is precise: put the condition in a CTE so it runs before the outer calculation, rather than in the outer WHERE.

Without context, the small counties poison the scale

# the ratio is formed over ALL counties, then color quantiles are taken
# a 300 member county with one clinic can post 100+ providers per 1,000
# and every real county gets crushed into the bottom of the scale

With context, the filter runs first

WITH in_context AS (                              -- Add to Context: runs BEFORE the ratio
    SELECT County, "Medi-Cal MC Enrollees" AS members, Providers_All AS providers
    FROM county JOIN prov USING (County)
    WHERE "Medi-Cal MC Enrollees" >= 5000
)
SELECT County, providers / members * 1000 AS per_1k   -- quantiles now see clean rows only
FROM in_context;
The rule worth memorising: if a filter needs to change what a later calculation sees, not just what the view shows, it has to be a context filter. Everything that filters the color scale, a Top N, or an LOD denominator belongs in context.
The parallel to the last project: this is the same idea as the minimum contract threshold in the Star Ratings post, drop the unstable small denominator cases before ranking, so a tiny sample cannot win on noise.
Data prep: the three cleaning problems

The raw enrollment file does not arrive analysis ready. Three problems have to be fixed before any share is trustworthy, and a fourth is a data quality note.

1. The Plan Type label changed mid series (an era normalization)

From January 2007 to July 2018 the model was labeled Two-Plan. From August 2018 on it was split into Local Initiative (2 Plan) and Commercial Plan (2 Plan). The two eras never overlap within a month, so mapping both onto one continuous scheme produces a clean 19 year series. This is a union style reconciliation, the same value under two names across time.

2. Plan names have ALL CAPS and renamed variants (a group)

LA CARE becomes L.A. Care Health Plan/Los Angeles; Health Net / LA becomes Health Net Community Solutions/Los Angeles. Collapsing the variants into one brand is a Group, the same lesson as the parent orgs in the Star Ratings post. A group changes the numbers, not just the labels, so it has to be applied before any share is computed.

3. Six non medical product lines are mixed in (a filter)

Dental, PACE, SCAN, Cal MediConnect, PCCM, and Special Project are separate product lines. Market share is only meaningful over the medical managed care lines, so these are filtered out before any total is formed.

4. Cell suppression (a data quality note)

Small counts are suppressed with an annotation code. These become nulls, not zeros, treating a suppressed cell as zero would understate a plan.

Introduction and the question
The question: the enrollment file only knows enrollees. Where do residents, providers, and quality scores come from, and how are they attached without inflating the counts?

Three joins, all performed inside Tableau. The population, quality, and provider files are cleaned into join ready reference tables up front, but the joins themselves happen in the workbook, so this post is where they are explained. Each one attaches a new column on a shared key:

  • Penetration joins county population on County, to divide members by residents.
  • Access joins provider counts on County, to divide providers by members.
  • Quality joins HEDIS scores on Brand and Year, to set share against quality.
Why relationships, not physical joins: in Tableau these are modeled as relationships so the reference table cannot fan out the enrollment rows. A physical inner join on a one to many key would duplicate enrollment and silently double a total; a relationship keeps each table at its own grain and aggregates before combining.
Penetration: a join on County
Penetration = SUM([Medi-Cal MC Enrollees]) / SUM([Population])

County enrollment joins county population on County. The result is the share of a county's residents who are Medi-Cal managed care members. Los Angeles sits at 36.4%; reliance runs highest inland.

SQL and pandas

# SQL: a join on County
SELECT c.County,
       c."Medi-Cal MC Enrollees" / p.Population AS penetration
FROM county c JOIN pop p ON c.County = p.County;

# pandas: the same join, then a column division
m = county.merge(pop, on='County')
m['penetration'] = m['Medi-Cal MC Enrollees'] / m['Population']

A Medi-Cal MC Enrollees >= 5,000 filter is set to Add to Context so a handful of tiny denominator counties (Alpine has a few hundred members) cannot post an absurd ratio and blow out the color scale. Exactly why that filter has to be a context filter is explained under Context & Data Prep.

SQL and pandas

# SQL: the size filter lives in the CTE (context), the ratio is formed outside it
WITH in_context AS (
    SELECT c.County, c."Medi-Cal MC Enrollees" AS members, pr.Providers_All AS providers
    FROM county c JOIN prov pr ON c.County = pr.County
    WHERE c."Medi-Cal MC Enrollees" >= 5000        -- Add to Context
)
SELECT County, providers / members * 1000 AS per_1k
FROM in_context ORDER BY per_1k;

# pandas: filter the frame before forming the ratio
a = county.merge(prov, on='County')
a = a[a['Medi-Cal MC Enrollees'] >= 5000]
a['per_1k'] = a['Providers_All'] / a['Medi-Cal MC Enrollees'] * 1000

Introduction and the question
The question: the market share chart shows each plan separately, yet every plan's share divides by the same monthly total. How does Tableau compute a per month total while the view is broken out by plan?

The problem a FIXED expression solves. Market share is this plan's enrollees / everyone's enrollees this month. But the view is split by plan, so a plain SUM([Enrollees]) only ever sees one plan's rows at a time. The denominator has to be computed at a coarser level of detail than the view, the whole month, and then handed back to each plan's row.

That is what FIXED means. It fixes the grouping in the expression itself (here, by month), so the plan breakout in the view cannot shrink it. Every plan row for June 2026 receives the same June 2026 total, and the division gives a clean share.

Market share: a FIXED LOD on the month
Month Total = { FIXED [Enrollment Month] : SUM([Enrollees]) } Market Share = SUM([Enrollees]) / SUM([Month Total])

Read it left to right: for each month, take the total enrollees across all plans, and hand that number back to every plan row of that month. The plan breakout in the view divides into it, so L.A. Care's row shows its own enrollees over the whole month, which is its share.

SQL

  • The approach: a window function, SUM(...) OVER (PARTITION BY month), is the exact analog of the FIXED total. It computes the month's total once and repeats it on every plan row.
  • The share: each row's enrollees divided by that windowed total.
WITH totals AS (                                         -- the FIXED [Month] total
    SELECT "Enrollment Month" AS month, Brand, Enrollees,
           SUM(Enrollees) OVER (PARTITION BY "Enrollment Month") AS month_total
    FROM share
)
SELECT Brand, Enrollees / month_total AS market_share
FROM totals
WHERE month = '2026-06'
ORDER BY market_share DESC;

pandas

  • The approach: groupby('Enrollment Month').transform('sum') broadcasts the month total onto every plan row, then the share is a plain column division.
  • Why transform and not agg: agg collapses the frame to one row per month. transform keeps every plan row and glues the month total onto each, which is exactly the shape a FIXED LOD produces.
s = share.copy()
s['month_total']  = s.groupby('Enrollment Month')['Enrollees'].transform('sum')
s['market_share'] = s['Enrollees'] / s['month_total']

s.loc[s['Enrollment Month'] == '2026-06', ['Brand', 'market_share']]
