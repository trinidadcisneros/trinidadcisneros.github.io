Introduction

The last post in this series mapped who holds the Los Angeles Medi-Cal market. This one prices it: what the state actually pays a health plan for each member, how that price has moved over six years, and what a change in it is worth in dollars.

How a Medi-Cal health plan gets paid

  • Medi-Cal is California's Medicaid program, public health insurance for people with low incomes. Most members get their coverage through managed care: the state hires a health plan to run their care rather than paying each doctor bill itself.
  • The plan is paid by capitation: a fixed amount per member, per month, whether or not that member sees a doctor. The unit is often written PMPM, per member per month.
  • That fixed monthly amount is the capitation rate, and it is the subject of this post. For a Medi-Cal plan the rate is effectively the price of everything: multiply it by members and months, and that is the revenue.

Who sets the price

  • The plan does not set it. The Department of Health Care Services (DHCS), the state agency that runs Medi-Cal, develops the rates with actuaries (specialists who price risk) and certifies them to the federal government.
  • The plan cannot raise the price, discount it, or negotiate it the way a commercial insurer prices employer coverage. When the state moves the rate, the plan's revenue per member moves with it.
  • Rates are published as a range for each group of members: a lower bound, an upper bound, and, through 2023, a midpoint. What happened to the midpoint after 2023 becomes one of this post's methods.

The market this post prices

  • The setting is the same as the market share post: Los Angeles County, the largest county Medicaid market in the country, organized under California's Two-Plan Model, with three competing Medi-Cal plans.
  • L.A. Care is the public plan the state created in 1997; Health Net is the commercial plan it selected in 1997; Kaiser Permanente joined by direct state contract in January 2024.
  • Every kind of member carries its own price, and the dashboard prices four member groups:
    • Child: members under 21.
    • Adult: working age adults who qualify by income.
    • Maternity: members during pregnancy, delivery, and the follow up period.
    • SPD (Seniors and Persons with Disabilities): seniors, and members with qualifying disabilities.
  • A category dial switches every chart among the four groups; who is in each group, and what care their rate is meant to pay for, gets its own section below.

Who qualifies, in brief

  • Eligibility runs mostly on income, measured against the Federal Poverty Level (FPL), the federal income line the government updates every year.
  • The income cutoffs differ by group:
    • Adults 19 to 64: household income up to 138% of the FPL.
    • Children under 19: up to 266% of the FPL, the most generous cutoff.
    • Pregnancy: up to 213% of the FPL for full coverage.
    • Seniors and persons with disabilities: their own pathways, including automatic linkage for people receiving Supplemental Security Income.
  • The rules move with state budgets, and 2026 brought two changes worth knowing: an asset limit returned for the senior and disability pathways after two years without one, and new full scope enrollment closed for undocumented adults 19 and older who are not pregnant, a policy line that connects directly to the SIS and UIS rate categories in this data.
  • The exact dollar cutoffs change every year, so check the state's own pages for the current rules: the DHCS Do You Qualify page and its income eligibility chart.
What this post asks about the price of a Los Angeles member:
  • What does the state pay each plan per adult member per month, and how has it moved from 2021 to 2026?
  • Do Los Angeles plans get paid more or less than the statewide average for the same kind of member?
  • What do the rates mean in revenue when multiplied across millions of member months, and what is a rate move worth in dollars?
  • How different is the price for a child, an adult, a senior with disabilities, and a maternity member, and are the plans ever paid the same?

The rate card: how one price is written

A capitation rate is not one number per plan. Each plan's contract is a card of prices, one per kind of member, and reading the card takes three definitions.

Categories of aid: a price per kind of member

  • A category of aid is the state's label for a group of members who cost roughly the same to cover: Child, Adult, Maternity, SPD (Seniors and Persons with Disabilities), and more.
  • The differences are large. In 2021 Los Angeles, L.A. Care's adult rate was $216.59 per member per month while its maternity rate was $7,575.48, about 35 times higher, because the expected care is that much costlier.
  • Comparing plans is only fair within one category. Four families span all six years cleanly (Adult, Child, Maternity, SPD), and the dashboard's Select Category dial switches every rate chart among them.

The adult category changed its name in 2023

  • Through 2022 the card had a plain Adult row. From 2023 the state split every category by immigration status: SIS (Satisfactory Immigration Status) and UIS (Unsatisfactory Immigration Status) variants.
  • The adult series in this post is Adult through 2022 and Adult - SIS from 2023 on, the direct continuation of the same population. No year carries both labels, so the series never double counts.

Rates are published as ranges, and the midpoint vanished

  • Each rate is a range: a lower bound, an upper bound, and through 2023 a published midpoint.
  • From 2024 on, the state publishes only the bounds. Every chart in this post uses a working rate: the published midpoint when it exists, otherwise the halfway point of the bounds. The calculation is worked through under Methods.
One line to keep: a capitation rate card is a price list for people, priced by how much care each group is expected to need. Comparisons stay fair one group at a time, and the dashboard's dial makes every group's line as easy to follow as the adult one.
The four member groups, and what their rates pay for

Medicaid sorts members into groups by who they are and what care they are expected to need, and the capitation rate for a group is the monthly budget for everything an average member of that group uses. Reading the four groups side by side explains why the prices sit where they do. The dollar ranges below are the Los Angeles working rates across 2021 to 2026, all plans.

Child, roughly $73 to $189 per member per month

  • Members under 21. Medicaid gives children a special guarantee called EPSDT (Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic and Treatment): regular checkups on a set schedule, immunizations, and vision, hearing, dental, and developmental screenings, plus treatment for anything a screening finds.
  • The cheapest group because most children mostly need preventive care: well child visits, vaccines, and the occasional illness or injury.

Adult, roughly $176 to $321 per member per month

  • Working age adults who qualify by income. Their rate covers the everyday package: doctor and specialist visits, hospital and emergency care, prescriptions, lab work and imaging, mental health care, and preventive services.
  • The middle of the price card, and the largest group, which is why the revenue table uses this rate as its single stand in price.

Maternity, roughly $7,078 to $8,782 per member per month

  • Members during pregnancy, delivery, and the follow up period. The rate concentrates an expensive episode into a few months: prenatal visits and ultrasounds, labor and delivery in a hospital, cesarean surgery when needed, and postpartum checkups.
  • High but short lived, the plan collects it only during the maternity window. It is also the one price the state sets identically for every Los Angeles plan, a finding the dashboard makes visible.

SPD (Seniors and Persons with Disabilities), roughly $539 to $1,307 per member per month

  • Seniors and members with qualifying disabilities whose coverage runs through Medi-Cal. Their care runs heavier on every front: more specialist and hospital care, more prescriptions, durable medical equipment such as wheelchairs and oxygen, home health visits, and help coordinating it all.
  • Several times the adult price, because chronic conditions need continuous care rather than occasional visits.
The service list a capitation rate is meant to cover

Whatever the group, the monthly payment is the plan's budget for a defined package of services. The everyday core of that package:

  • Primary care visits, and specialist visits when referred
  • Hospital stays, surgeries, and emergency and urgent care
  • Prescription drugs the plan is responsible for
  • Lab tests, x rays, and other imaging
  • Preventive care: checkups, screenings, immunizations
  • Mental health care in outpatient settings
  • Maternity care, from prenatal visits through delivery and postpartum follow up
  • Children's EPSDT screenings: vision, hearing, dental, and developmental checks, with treatment for what they find
  • Medical equipment and supplies, from wheelchairs to oxygen
  • Home health visits after a hospital stay or for ongoing needs
  • Rides to medical appointments for members without transportation
  • Care coordination, the plan's job of connecting all of the above

The exact benefits package evolves with state policy; this list is the everyday core it always contains.

One line to keep: the four rates are four budgets for four different lives. A child's budget buys screenings and vaccines; an SPD member's buys equipment, home visits, and constant coordination. The price differences are the care differences.

Conclusion and summary of results

Read as one story: the state cut the adult price in 2022, rebuilt it sharply from 2024, has paid Los Angeles plans below the statewide average the whole time, and every point of movement is worth tens of millions of dollars to the county's plans.

The dashboard

Interactive. Hover any line or cell for the underlying numbers. Two dials drive it; here is what each one does.

The Select Category dial (top of the dashboard)

  • It picks which kind of member is being priced: Adult, Child, Maternity, or SPD (Seniors and Persons with Disabilities).
  • The rate chart, the statewide benchmark, and the year over year table all switch to that category's prices, and their titles change with the dial.
  • Expect the scale to jump: child prices run lowest (roughly $73 to $189 per member per month), adult sits in the middle, SPD runs $539 to $1,307, and maternity near $8,000.
  • On Maternity the chart shows a single purple line labeled All plans (identical rate). Nothing is missing: all three plans are paid exactly the same maternity price, so their three lines sit perfectly on top of each other, and the purple line says so.
  • Watch the growth as you switch: the cheap groups grew fastest over the six years, child rates about 75%, SPD 62% to 75%, adult 31% to 36%, and maternity only about 5%.

The Rate Adjustment % dial

  • It asks a what if question: what happens to revenue if the state moved rates up or down by that percent?
  • Slide it and the Scenario Revenue row reprices while Estimated Revenue holds still as the baseline; the gap between them is the dollars gained or lost.
  • The revenue cells shade blue for gains and orange for losses, deeper as the dollars grow; at 0% the two rows match and the cells sit pale.

Why the revenue table ignores the category dial

  • The revenue table multiplies two numbers: how many members a plan has, and one monthly price per member.
  • The member counts lump every kind of member together: children, adults, seniors, everyone. The public data does not say how many of a plan's members are children versus adults versus anything else.
  • A true revenue number would need that breakdown: each group of members times that group's own price, added up. Without the breakdown, the table has to pick a single price and apply it to everybody.
  • The adult price is the fairest single pick, because adults are the largest group and their price sits in the middle of the card.
  • Watch what would go wrong otherwise: put the dial on Maternity and the table would multiply millions of members by a roughly $8,000 price and print hundreds of billions of dollars. That number would be meaningless, because only a small share of members have a baby in any year.
  • So the revenue table stays priced at the adult rate on purpose, and its title says so. The Rate Adjustment % dial still moves it, because that dial changes the price itself, not which members are being priced.

The question: the published rate files were not built for analysis, seven files, four header spellings, dollar signs, duplicated rows, and a midpoint that stops existing in 2024. What has to happen before a single trustworthy chart can be drawn?

The cleaning step runs in Python and changes the data; Tableau then stacks, relates, and displays it. Data prep: the four cleaning problems

1. The seven files disagree on their own column names (a standardization)

  • The Geographic Managed Care file says Rate Period and Calendar where the others say Rating Period and Calendar Year; the PACE (Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly) file calls its plan column PACE Organization; the SCAN (Senior Care Action Network) file omits the plan column entirely.
  • The cleaning maps every variant onto one nine column layout so the files can stack. A union is only as good as the agreement of its columns.

2. Rates arrive as text (a type conversion)

  • Values appear as $1,234.56 with dollar signs, commas, and stray spaces, and missing rates appear as $-. All become plain numbers, and $- becomes a proper null rather than a zero, a zero would be a claim that the state pays nothing.

3. Plan names drift across years (a standardization into Brand)

  • The same plan appears as LA Care, LA Care HP, and LA Care Health Plan in different years. Forty variants collapse into one Brand per plan.
  • This matters beyond tidiness: Brand is the key the enrollment table is related on. A name that drifts is a join that silently drops years.

4. The source prints a few rows twice (a deduplication)

  • Four PACE rows for late 2021 appear twice in the state's own file, identical in every column. Exact duplicates are dropped; left in, any sum over that program would double count them.
The working rate: one comparable number per year

Through 2023 the state published each rate range with a midpoint. From 2024 it publishes only the lower and upper bounds. A chart that plots the midpoint goes blank at 2024, not because the price vanished but because the paperwork changed.

Working Rate = IFNULL([Midpoint], ([Lower Bound] + [Upper Bound]) / 2)

Read it left to right: use the published midpoint when there is one; otherwise take the halfway point of the published bounds. For 2021 to 2023 that is exactly the state's number; for 2024 on it is the natural center of the range the state still publishes.

SQL

  • The approach: COALESCE, which returns the first non null argument, is the exact analog of IFNULL.
SELECT Brand, "Calendar Year",
       COALESCE("Midpoint", ("Lower Bound" + "Upper Bound") / 2) AS working_rate
FROM rates;

pandas

  • The approach: fillna fills the missing midpoints from a second column built as the bounds' average.
rates['working_rate'] = rates['Midpoint'].fillna(
    (rates['Lower Bound'] + rates['Upper Bound']) / 2)

Introduction and the question
The question: the rates live in seven files at one row per year, and the members live in another file at one row per month. How do they become one dashboard without any number getting counted twice?

Two mechanisms, and the difference between them is the difference between right and wrong totals: a union stacks tables that describe the same kind of thing, and a relationship connects tables that describe different kinds of things without merging their rows.

The wildcard union: seven files, one stack

The seven model files share one layout after cleaning, so they stack vertically, the same rows, more of them. In Tableau this is a wildcard union: point the connection at clean_*.csv and every matching file, present or future, joins the stack, with a Path field recording which file each row came from.

Relationships vs joins: the fan out pitfall

The rates are yearly, about 23 category rows per plan per year. The members are monthly, 12 rows per plan per year. Gluing the tables together row by row, a hard join, multiplies them.

The wrong way: a row level merge

  • The approach (do not use it): a pandas merge on plan and year matches every rate row to every month row.
merged = rates.merge(enroll,
    left_on=['Brand', 'Calendar Year'], right_on=['Brand', 'Year'])
# every rate row now appears 12 times (once per month)
# every monthly member row now appears ~23 times (once per rate category)
What a sum returnsVerdict
Wrong: sum the merged tableRates inflate 12 fold, member months inflate about 23 fold, every total is fiction.✗

The right way, which is what a Tableau relationship does

  • The approach: aggregate each side to plan and year, then combine the two small results. A relationship stores no merged rows at all; it matches the tables at whatever grain each chart asks for.
# pandas: aggregate first, then merge the aggregates
rate_y = (rates.groupby(['Brand', 'Calendar Year'], as_index=False)
          ['working_rate'].sum())
mm_y   = (enroll.groupby(['Brand', 'Year'], as_index=False)
          ['Enrollees'].sum())          # member months: the 12 monthly counts, added
out = rate_y.merge(mm_y, left_on=['Brand', 'Calendar Year'],
                         right_on=['Brand', 'Year'])
-- SQL: the same shape, two aggregate CTEs joined on the keys
WITH r AS (
    SELECT Brand, "Calendar Year" AS yr, SUM(working_rate) AS rate
    FROM rates WHERE County = 'Los Angeles' GROUP BY 1, 2
), m AS (
    SELECT Brand, Year AS yr, SUM(Enrollees) AS member_months
    FROM enroll GROUP BY 1, 2
)
SELECT r.Brand, r.yr, m.member_months * r.rate AS revenue
FROM r JOIN m ON r.Brand = m.Brand AND r.yr = m.yr;

Introduction and the question
The question: the top chart is filtered to Los Angeles, yet its gray benchmark line is a statewide average. How does one chart hold both, and why did the benchmark's first draft come out ten times too high?

The tool is a FIXED Level of Detail (LOD) expression, a calculation that declares its own grouping and deliberately ignores the sheet's ordinary filters. That independence is exactly what a benchmark needs, and exactly what makes it dangerous until the filters it should respect are promoted to context.

FIXED: an average that ignores the county filter
Statewide Average Rate = { FIXED [Calendar Year] : AVG([Working Rate]) }

Read it left to right: for each year, average the working rate over every row the calculation can see, regardless of what the sheet filters to. The sheet's County filter holds the plan lines to Los Angeles; the FIXED average sails past it and spans the state. One chart, two scopes.

SQL

  • The approach: compute the yearly average in its own aggregate, without the county condition, and join it back by year, the FIXED expression is precisely a subquery with fewer WHERE clauses than the outer query.
WITH statewide AS (                        -- no County condition here: that is the point
    SELECT "Calendar Year" AS yr, AVG(working_rate) AS benchmark
    FROM rates
    WHERE Model = 'Two-Plan' AND "Category of Aid" IN ('Adult', 'Adult - SIS')
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT la.Brand, la.yr, la.rate, s.benchmark
FROM la_rates la JOIN statewide s ON la.yr = s.yr;

pandas

  • The approach: compute the benchmark on the unfiltered frame, then map it onto the Los Angeles rows by year, computing on one scope and attaching to another is the whole trick.
two_plan_adult = rates[(rates['Model'] == 'Two-Plan')
    & (rates['Category of Aid'].isin(['Adult', 'Adult - SIS']))]
benchmark = two_plan_adult.groupby('Calendar Year')['working_rate'].mean()

la = two_plan_adult[two_plan_adult['County'] == 'Los Angeles'].copy()
la['benchmark'] = la['Calendar Year'].map(benchmark)

Context filters: the WHERE clause that runs before the benchmark

FIXED ignores ordinary filters, all of them. The sheet also filters Model to Two-Plan and the category to the adult rows, and the benchmark must respect those two, otherwise it averages every program in the union, including elderly care programs priced in the thousands per month.

What Add to Context does. A context filter runs before FIXED expressions are computed, an ordinary filter runs after. Promoting Model and Category of Aid to context feeds the benchmark clean rows; leaving County ordinary keeps the benchmark statewide. The filter placement is the calculation.

The order of operations, in one line each

  • Context filters run first: Model = Two-Plan, Category of Aid = Adult and Adult - SIS.
  • FIXED expressions run next, seeing only what context allowed: the statewide benchmark.
  • Ordinary filters run last, shaping the view without touching the benchmark: County = Los Angeles.

SQL

  • The approach: context filters live in the inner CTE (before the average), the ordinary county filter lives in the outer query (after it), the SQL from the previous section is already written this way.
The rule worth memorising: a filter that must change what a FIXED calculation sees goes to context; a filter that must not, stays ordinary. Here both directions are used at once, and the gray line is only correct because of it.
The parallel to the last post: the market share post promoted a minimum member filter to context so tiny counties could not hijack a color scale. Same lever, different reason: there it protected quantiles, here it protects an LOD benchmark.
Introduction and the question
The question: one dial repaints every chart for a different kind of member, another claims a 5% rate move is worth $375 million, and the year over year table says the price moved 25% in 2024. Where do those numbers come from?

Two mechanisms finish the workbook: a table calculation turns the rate series into period over period change without touching the data, and a parameter puts a reader controlled number into a calculation, the what if dial.

Percent difference: the LAG window

The middle dashboard panel is the same rate series as the top chart, displayed as percent change from the prior year. In Tableau that is a quick table calculation, Percent Difference, computed per plan across the years.

SQL

  • The approach: LAG, a window function, fetches the prior year's rate on each row; the change is the difference over the prior value, partitioned by plan so each brand compares only with itself.
SELECT Brand, yr,
       (rate - LAG(rate) OVER (PARTITION BY Brand ORDER BY yr))
       / LAG(rate) OVER (PARTITION BY Brand ORDER BY yr) * 100 AS pct_change
FROM la_rates;

pandas

  • The approach: pct_change down each plan's column does the lag, divide, and subtract in one call.
wide = la.pivot_table(index='Calendar Year', columns='Brand', values='working_rate')
yoy  = wide.pct_change() * 100

The what if parameter: one dial, every cell

A parameter is a single value the reader controls, here Rate Adjustment %, a dial from -10% to +10%. It feeds a scenario calculation that reprices every cell of the revenue table as the dial turns.

Estimated Revenue = SUM([Enrollees]) * SUM([Working Rate]) Scenario Revenue = SUM([Enrollees]) * SUM([Working Rate]) * (1 + [Rate Adjustment %]) Revenue Change = [Scenario Revenue] - [Estimated Revenue]

SQL and pandas

  • The approach: a parameter is just a scalar in the arithmetic; SQL and pandas each multiply the same aggregate by (1 + the dial).
-- SQL: the dial as a scalar
SELECT Brand, yr, member_months * rate * (1 + 0.05) AS scenario_revenue
FROM revenue_base;

# pandas: the same scalar
out['scenario'] = out['revenue'] * (1 + 0.05)
out['change']   = out['scenario'] - out['revenue']

The category parameter: one dial rewriting every chart

The second parameter is a string with four values: Adult, Child, Maternity, SPD (Seniors and Persons with Disabilities). It cannot filter anything by itself; two calculated fields connect it to the rows.

Category Family = IF CONTAINS([Category of Aid], "UIS") THEN NULL ELSE REPLACE([Category of Aid], " - SIS", "") END Category Matches = [Category Family] = [Select Category]
  • Why the mapping exists: the state renamed every category in 2023 by immigration status, so Adult became Adult - SIS (Satisfactory Immigration Status). Stripping the suffix rebuilds four stable families; the UIS (Unsatisfactory Immigration Status) variants become null so no family ever counts two populations.
  • Where the filter sits: Category Matches = True is a context filter on every rate sheet, so the FIXED statewide benchmark obeys the dial too. The revenue sheet is deliberately left off the dial, as explained on The Dashboard tab.
  • The titles follow: each panel title embeds the parameter, so a Child chart is labeled Child.

SQL and pandas

  • The approach: a parameter is a variable substituted into the filter, a WHERE clause in SQL, a boolean mask in pandas.

FROM rates
WHERE REPLACE("Category of Aid", ' - SIS', '') = 'Child'   -- the parameter

When plans tie, the chart says so

Maternity exposed a display trap: all three plans are paid the identical rate, so three lines drew in the same place and only the top one showed, reading as a single plan's line. The fix makes the tie itself visible.

Same Rate All Plans = { FIXED [County], [Calendar Year] : MIN([Working Rate]) } = { FIXED [County], [Calendar Year] : MAX([Working Rate]) } Plan Label = IF [Same Rate All Plans] THEN "All plans (identical rate)" ELSE [Brand] END
  • The flag: if the smallest and largest rate in the county that year are equal, every plan is being paid the same number.
  • The label: color runs on Plan Label instead of Brand, so tied years draw one purple line that announces the tie, while Brand stays on Detail to keep each plan's line and tooltip intact.
  • In SQL terms: the flag is MIN(rate) = MAX(rate) over a county and year group, the same trick used to test any column for uniformity.

The highlight table: color as a measure

The revenue table doubles as the dial's display. Each cell's background is driven by Revenue Change on a diverging scale, blue for gains, orange for losses, centered on a fixed zero with fixed ends at plus and minus $750 million.

  • Why the ends are fixed: left automatic, the scale would rescale to whatever the dial produces, so 1% and 10% would look identical and 0% would render dark. Pinned ends make the shading proportional to the dollars: pale near zero, deeper as the dial grows, always deepest for the biggest book.
  • Why the center is zero: zero change is the neutral state regardless of where the dial sits, so the midpoint of the color scale belongs to it.
