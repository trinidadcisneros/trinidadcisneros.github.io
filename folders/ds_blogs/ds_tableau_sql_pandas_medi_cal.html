Introduction

If you have used Tableau or another visualization tool, you may have wondered what data preparation and transformation are actually occurring under the hood when you build a chart, table, or dashboard.

The purpose of this post is to demystify how Tableau converts your actions into queries through its internal engine, VizQL, using a select set of query concepts as the lens, and to show that what happens is no different from everyday SQL and pandas.

Specifically, this post covers:
  • Row level vs aggregate calculations, how the grain of a question decides the right aggregation, and the trap when it does not
  • Level of Detail expressions (FIXED, INCLUDE, EXCLUDE), group level calculations whose answers attach back to every row
  • Table calculations, running totals and year over year change, computed on the view's results rather than the raw data
  • Parameters, viewer controlled variables that drive Top N filters and metric swaps
  • A published interactive dashboard and a one page Tableau to SQL to pandas cheat sheet

Finally, this post uses the Medi-Cal Managed Care Enrollment Report, monthly enrollment for every Medi-Cal managed care plan, by county, January 2007 through June 2026, for every comparison, and the accompanying dashboard answers:

  • How has Medi-Cal enrollment trended, statewide and in any county you click?
  • Which plans dominate each county, and what share of the market do they hold?
  • How is enrollment distributed geographically across California's 58 counties?
  • Who are the top N plans, ranked by total enrollment or by county share?

Introduction & The Question
  • Every calculation, in Tableau or any analytics tool (SQL, pandas, Excel, R), is one of two kinds: row level or aggregate
  • Knowing which one you are writing is the difference between a right number and a plausible but wrong one
  • Why it matters: aggregates power every KPI, summary table, and chart; row level calculations build the derived fields that feed them
  • Mixing them up fails silently: the wrong choice still returns a clean looking number, with no error to warn you
  • Below we demonstrate how row level and aggregate calculations operate, the common pitfall, and how to tell which one a question needs
  • We then use the Medi-Cal plan data to answer the project question:

When to use which: the correct and the incorrect approach

Step 1: check the grain of the data

The choice of aggregation starts with the data itself. A sample of the cleaned data:

MonthCountyPlan TypePlan CategoryPlanEnrollees
2026-06-01Los AngelesLocal Initiative (2 Plan)MedicalL.A. Care Health Plan2,102,545
2026-06-01Los AngelesCommercial Plan (2 Plan)MedicalHealth Net Community Solutions1,079,978
2026-06-01Los AngelesSCANMedicalSCAN Health Plan14,680

Look at the grain: one row is one plan's enrollment, in one county, in one month. June 2026 in LA County is spread across seven rows, one per plan. "A month of LA County enrollment" does not exist as a row; it has to be assembled by summing the plan rows. That is exactly where the trap lives.

Step 2: choose the calculation kind

Knowing the grain, here is how to choose, and what happens to the project question when you choose right and when you choose wrong:

SituationRight choiceHow it goes wrong
The answer lives inside one row (a rate from two columns on the same row)Row level: [A] / [B]Aggregating first when the question was per row
The answer spans rows (a total, a count, an average)Aggregate: SUM([A]) / SUM([B])Averaging a row level ratio, every row counts equally no matter its size
The rows are slices of the thing you care about (months split across plans, sales split across items)Reassemble first: SUM(x) / COUNTD(unit)The classic trap: AVG(x) quietly averages the slices, not the thing

The rule in one line: match the aggregation to the grain of the question, never average something that is already a slice of what you actually care about.

Applied to the project question, in Tableau

// INCORRECT: averages plan rows, not months AVG([Enrollees]) → 437,233 // CORRECT: reassemble the months, then divide by how many there are SUM([Enrollees]) / COUNTD([Month]) → 2,546,791

Why they differ by 6x: both formulas are valid Tableau; they answer different questions. AVG([Enrollees]) treats every plan row as an equal unit, so it answers "how big is a typical plan's month?" (437K). The question asked about a typical month, and a month is seven plan rows glued together, so the total must be reassembled first, then divided by the number of distinct months (2.5M).

  • Build: Filters shelf: County = Los Angeles, Plan Category = Medical; both calculated fields on Text via one Measure Values pill, Measure Names on Rows

The same thing in SQL and pandas

-- SQL (DuckDB)
SELECT AVG(Enrollees)                          AS incorrect_avg,   -- 437,233
       SUM(Enrollees) / COUNT(DISTINCT Month)  AS correct_avg      -- 2,546,791
FROM enr
WHERE County = 'Los Angeles' AND "Plan Category" = 'Medical';
# pandas
la = df[(df.County == 'Los Angeles') & (df['Plan Category'] == 'Medical')]

incorrect = la.Enrollees.mean()                          # 437,233
correct   = la.Enrollees.sum() / la.Month.nunique()      # 2,546,791

Notice the code makes the choice visible: mean() vs sum() / nunique() are obviously different operations. In Tableau both hide behind the innocent word "average", which is why the grain check matters more in a GUI.

Introduction & The Questions
  • Level of Detail (LOD), in lay language: a calculation done for a group (a county's total, a plan's total) whose answer gets attached back to every row inside that group, so you can use it in row math like a share or a benchmark
  • You already know this idea by other names:
    • in SQL it is a window function, SUM(x) OVER (PARTITION BY g), or a grouped subquery joined back
    • in pandas it is groupby(g)['x'].transform('sum')
    • out loud it is "the group total, broadcast back to the rows" or "GROUP BY without collapsing"
  • Why it matters: shares, benchmarks, and cohort math all need a denominator computed at a different grain than the view, and a row cannot see other rows in any tool; LODs are Tableau's only way to reach across rows
  • There are three types, and they differ only in which grouping the calculation uses: FIXED = the grouping written inside the formula, ignoring whatever the chart shows · INCLUDE = the chart's grouping plus a finer one added for the math · EXCLUDE = the chart's grouping with one level removed

The three types sit on a spectrum of grouping detail, relative to how the data is currently grouped:

← INCLUDE
moves the math one step toward MORE detail

math runs per Plan

the level your data is summarized to (the GROUP BY your shelves created)

FIXED is the exception: it does not slide. You pin the grouping in the formula ({ FIXED [County] : ... }), and the math stays at that spot no matter how the chart is grouped, filtered, or sliced. Inside all three, any aggregate works: SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX, COUNTD, MEDIAN.

The same thing in SQL and pandas

-- SQL: the window function IS the FIXED
WITH la AS (
    SELECT "Plan", SUM(Enrollees) AS members,
           SUM(SUM(Enrollees)) OVER ()  AS county_total   -- the FIXED denominator
    FROM enr
    WHERE County = 'Los Angeles' AND "Plan Category" = 'Medical'
      AND Month = DATE '2026-06-01'             -- WHERE runs first = Add to Context
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT "Plan", ROUND(100.0 * members / county_total, 1) AS pct
FROM la ORDER BY members DESC;
# pandas: filter first, then share of the group sum
la6 = df[(df.County == 'Los Angeles') & (df['Plan Category'] == 'Medical')
         & (df.Month == '2026-06-01')]

p = la6.groupby('Plan', as_index=False)['Enrollees'].sum()
p['pct'] = (100 * p.Enrollees / p.Enrollees.sum()).round(1)

INCLUDE: prepares an intermediate aggregate for each group, so those results can be reaggregated downstream
  • FIXED (above) completely ignored how the chart groups things
  • INCLUDE is gentler, it works with the chart's grouping:
    • the chart's rows stay exactly as they are (plan types)
    • behind the scenes, each row is split into smaller pieces (the individual plans inside each type)
    • each piece gets totaled first
    • then those totals are averaged to give one number back per row of the chart

Introduction & The Question
  • Table calculations perform some operation on values ALREADY in the view
    • each cell compared to its neighbors:
      • running totals
      • differences
      • ranks
      • moving averages
  • The key distinction from LODs: an LOD reaches into the raw data but a table calc transforms the small result table AFTER the query returns
  • You already know this idea:
    • in SQL it is a window function over an ordered result set, SUM(x) OVER (ORDER BY t) or LAG(x, 12)
    • in pandas it is .cumsum() and .pct_change(12) on an ordered frame
  • Why it matters: every growth story, every "compared to last year," every cumulative chart is a table calc; and Tableau's Compute Using menu is just choosing the PARTITION BY and ORDER BY
  • Scope note: this tab demonstrates only two of the methods; Tableau's Quick Table Calculation menu also offers difference, percent difference, percent of total, rank, percentile, moving average, YTD total, compound growth rate, and YTD growth, all built from the same window logic shown here

Example: building a running total and year over year growth in all three tools

In Tableau

  • Columns: discrete YEAR(Month) + discrete MONTH(Month); the year hierarchy is REQUIRED (see the gotcha below)
  • Rows: SUM(Enrollees) → right click → Quick Table Calculation → Running Total
  • Second SUM(Enrollees) → Quick Table Calculation → Year over Year Growth

The same thing in SQL and pandas

-- SQL: window functions over the ordered result
WITH monthly AS (
    SELECT Month, SUM(Enrollees) AS members
    FROM enr WHERE "Plan Category" = 'Medical' GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT Month, members,
       SUM(members) OVER (ORDER BY Month)                 AS running_total,
       100.0 * (members - LAG(members, 12) OVER (ORDER BY Month))
             / LAG(members, 12) OVER (ORDER BY Month)    AS yoy_pct
FROM monthly ORDER BY Month;
# pandas: cumsum and pct_change on the ordered frame
mo = df[df['Plan Category'] == 'Medical'].groupby('Month', as_index=False)['Enrollees'].sum()
mo['running_total'] = mo.Enrollees.cumsum()
mo['yoy_pct'] = 100 * mo.Enrollees.pct_change(12)

Abstract

Purpose

  • Build one interactive dashboard where any viewer can explore California Medi-Cal managed care enrollment by county and plan
  • Serve as the capstone that puts every calculation method in this post to work on one screen

Methods

  • CalHHS enrollment data (January 2007 to June 2026), cleaned in pandas (see the Overview tab's Methods)
  • Sheets built with the FIXED and EXCLUDE LODs, table calculations, and parameters covered in the other tabs
  • Assembled with a KPI strip, county responsive trend, county map, and Top N chart, wired by one county click filter action; published to Tableau Public
  • Every displayed number verified identical in DuckDB SQL and pandas

The questions we asked, and the answers

  • How has enrollment trended? Statewide Medical enrollment roughly quadrupled from about 3.2M in 2007 to a peak near 14M in 2023, then declined to 12.9M members by June 2026 (about 8% down year over year). The decline is real policy, not a data artifact:
    • 2023 to 2025: the post pandemic "unwinding": states resumed eligibility redeterminations after COVID era continuous coverage ended (DHCS)
    • July 2025: H.R. 1 cut roughly 1 trillion dollars from federal Medicaid over ten years, the largest change since the ACA (CHCF)
    • January 2026: California froze new comprehensive Medi-Cal enrollment for undocumented adults, with work requirements arriving late 2026 and six month redeterminations in January 2027 (LAO, KFF)
  • Which plans dominate each county, and by how much? In LA County: L.A. Care 59.4%, Health Net 30.5%, Kaiser 9.6% (June 2026); clicking any county re ranks its own top plans
  • How is enrollment distributed geographically? LA County alone holds 27.5% of the state, and the five biggest Southern California counties together hold about 54%
  • Who are the top N plans? Statewide, L.A. Care leads at 2.1M members, followed by Health Net (1.4M), Inland Empire Health Plan (1.4M), and Kaiser Permanente (1.2M)

Takeaway

  • A public plan built for a single county, L.A. Care, is the largest Medi-Cal plan in the state, and enrollment geography is heavily Southern Californian
  • Every interactive element on the dashboard is one of the calculation methods from this post doing its job in public
Putting it together: KPI strip, county responsive trend, choropleth, and the parameterized Top N chart, wired with one filter action, click any county.
Design decisions worth stealing
  • One visual hierarchy: the dashboard title carries the shared context (Medical, June 2026, how to interact); sheet titles are short labels, context is stated once, not four times
  • Actions are configured at the dashboard level: the filter action's source and target dropdowns must point at the dashboard, with sheets checked in the list below
  • The county click was deliberately added to context on the Top N sheet so a click answers "who leads in this county" rather than "which statewide giants operate here"
  • The KPI strip stays an action target on purpose: numbers update with the selection, layout never moves
