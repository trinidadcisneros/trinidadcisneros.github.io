What HEDIS and the quality score actually are (the learning goal of this post)
  • HEDIS (Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set) is a standardized set of quality measures maintained by NCQA, the National Committee for Quality Assurance. Most US health plans report it, which is what makes plans comparable.
  • A single measure is a rate, numerator over denominator. For example, Breast Cancer Screening is the share of women 50 to 74 who got a mammogram out of those who should have. Higher is better for most measures.
  • DHCS holds Medi-Cal plans to two bars: a Minimum Performance Level (roughly the national Medicaid 50th percentile) that triggers corrective action if missed, and a High Performance Level (the 90th percentile, the national top tier).

This dataset does not give individual measures. It gives one composite score per plan per region per year, the Aggregated Quality Factor Score. Since acronyms mean nothing on their own, here is the plain version, the same explanation used across this project:

  • The score is a report card grade for a health plan, from 0 to 100
  • It answers one question: how good is this plan at keeping its members healthy (did kids get checkups, did people get their shots, did sick people get the care they needed)
  • Higher is better; 100 means performing at the level of the best plans in the country
  • The "national top tier" is the bar only the best 10% of US plans clear on a measure. It is NOT the same as scoring 90 to 100; it is the cutoff the top decile reaches.

How one plan's score is built, worked example on dummy data:

MeasureThis plan's rateNational top tier barMeasure score = (rate ÷ bar) × 100
Shots given709078
Checkups done808594
Diabetes control607086
Average of the measure scores(78 + 94 + 86) ÷ 3 = 86

Why divide the rate by the top tier bar: the bar is the goal, so rate over goal answers "how close did the plan get." Hitting the goal is 100, halfway is 50, beating it is over 100. Doing this puts every measure on the same 0 to 100 scale, so shots and checkups can be averaged together fairly. A plan can score high without ever reaching a single bar, if it gets close on all of them.

The key point for this post: the data already contains the finished score, one per plan per region per year. It is never recomputed here. Everything below only averages, compares, and ranks the scores that are already there.

Methods

Data source

  • Managed Care Performance Monitoring Dashboard Report, DHCS Managed Care Quality and Monitoring Division, via the CalHHS Open Data Portal (accessed July 2026)
  • The core file is the composite quality score, one row per plan, per region, per year, 2016 through 2023 (436 rows after cleaning); companion files add primary care provider ratios and member grievances
  • Obtained programmatically through the portal's CKAN API so the pipeline survives the state's file renames

Preparation

  • The one text field "Reporting Unit" split into Plan and Region; scores stored as text with percent signs converted to numbers; grievance and provider counts stripped of commas
  • A derived "Statewide Average" row added per year, the mean of that year's reporting unit scores, so the benchmark is a real series a chart can draw (see the Benchmarks tab)
  • Quality checks: no missing scores, score range 26.0 to 98.1, and a recorded panel note that the number of reporting units grows from 53 (2016 to 2019) to 56 (2020 on)

Reproducibility and verification

  • Two notebooks: one extracts the raw files and logs what was pulled; one cleans them into the CSVs used here. Re running the cleaner from the raw files reproduces the exact files byte for byte, so the pipeline is fully reproducible.
  • Every number in this post was computed in Tableau, then checked in DuckDB SQL and pandas; all three agree to the digit

Introduction & The Question
  • A single plan's quality score means little on its own. 59 out of 100, is that good? The only way to answer is to put a benchmark next to it, and the natural benchmark here is the statewide average for the same year.
  • A benchmark is a calculation done for a group (all plans in one year) whose answer gets attached back to every plan, so each plan can be compared against it. That is exactly a Level of Detail expression.
  • You already know this idea by other names:
    • in SQL it is a window function, AVG(x) OVER (PARTITION BY Year), or a grouped subquery joined back
    • in pandas it is groupby('Year')['x'].transform('mean')
    • out loud it is "the group average, broadcast back to the rows"
  • Why FIXED specifically: the dashboard lets a viewer filter to one year. A plain benchmark can quietly break when that filter runs. FIXED pins the grouping in the formula so the benchmark stays correct no matter how the chart is filtered.

The three LOD types sit on a spectrum of grouping detail. This post uses FIXED, the one that does not slide:

FIXED is the exception: it does not slide. You pin the grouping in the formula ({ FIXED [Year] : ... }), and the math stays at that spot no matter how the chart is grouped or filtered. (Part 1 covers INCLUDE and EXCLUDE in full.)

In Tableau

Two ways, both used in this project. The dashboard's trend line uses a derived data row (built once in cleaning) so the benchmark is a drawable series; the ranking's reference line uses a FIXED LOD (next section).

// The benchmark as an LOD, one average per year on every row { FIXED [Year] : AVG([Aqfs]) }

The same thing in SQL and pandas

  • SQL approach: a window function, AVG(...) OVER (PARTITION BY Year), computes each year's average and attaches it to every row without collapsing the table
  • pandas approach: groupby('Year').transform('mean') does the same, broadcasting the year's mean back onto every row so the gap to the benchmark is a single subtraction
-- SQL: the window function IS the FIXED benchmark
SELECT "Reporting Unit", Year, Aqfs,
       AVG(Aqfs) OVER (PARTITION BY Year)      AS statewide_avg,   -- broadcast to every row
       Aqfs - AVG(Aqfs) OVER (PARTITION BY Year) AS vs_avg
FROM plans
WHERE Year = 2023 ORDER BY Aqfs DESC;
# pandas: transform keeps the row shape while broadcasting the group mean
plans['statewide_avg'] = plans.groupby('Year')['Aqfs'].transform('mean')
plans['vs_avg']        = plans.Aqfs - plans.statewide_avg

FIXED for the reference line: pinning the benchmark so a year filter cannot blank it

The ranking sheet is filtered to one year by the viewer's parameter (see the Parameters tab). The ranking needs a red dashed line at that year's statewide average. The subtlety: the benchmark must survive the year filter, and land at the right value for whichever year is showing.

Question: draw a reference line at the selected year's statewide average, on a sheet filtered to that one year.

In this project the statewide average is stored as its own row (a "Statewide Average" reporting unit per year). The reference line reads it with a FIXED LOD keyed on Year:

// Statewide value for the reference line, one per year { FIXED [Year] : MAX(IF [Is Statewide] THEN [Aqfs] END) }

Why FIXED [Year] and not a plain average: FIXED computes per year before the view's own sort and layout, so each year keeps its own benchmark. When the year filter narrows the sheet to 2023, every remaining row already carries 57.41, and the line lands there. Key it wrong (or leave it un pinned) and the line either blanks out or shows a value mixed across years.

  • Analytics tab → Reference Line → Table; Value = the FIXED field, Aggregation = Average; format red dashed
  • The one gotcha: a reference line only offers fields already in the view, so drop the FIXED field on Detail first

The same thing in SQL and pandas

  • SQL approach: filter to the chosen year in WHERE, then take one AVG, the single benchmark value the reference line draws
  • pandas approach: mask to the year and call .mean() for that same scalar; the ranking is just a sort of those filtered rows
-- SQL: filter to the chosen year, the benchmark is just that year's average
SELECT "Reporting Unit", Aqfs,
       (SELECT AVG(Aqfs) FROM plans WHERE Year = 2023) AS ref_line
FROM plans
WHERE Year = 2023 ORDER BY Aqfs DESC;
# pandas: the reference value is a scalar for the filtered year
year   = 2023
ref    = plans.loc[plans.Year == year, 'Aqfs'].mean()   # 57.41
ranked = plans[plans.Year == year].sort_values('Aqfs', ascending=False)

The same 57.41 the trend line shows for 2023; the FIXED LOD and the plain filtered average land on the identical number.

Pitfall: a benchmark that ignores the year. If the reference used one average across all years instead of FIXED [Year], every year's sheet would show the same flat line (around 59.8, the eight year mean), making 2023's plans look worse and 2018's look better than they are. Pinning to [Year] is what makes the line move with the selection. SQL and pandas cannot make this mistake silently: the year is written into the WHERE clause or the mask, so the benchmark is always for the year on screen.
Introduction & The Question
  • A table calculation transforms the numbers ALREADY in the view, each cell compared to its neighbors: running totals, differences, ranks, moving averages
  • The key distinction from a benchmark or LOD: an LOD reaches back into the raw data; a table calc only touches the small result table AFTER the query returns. Here the view already holds one score per plan per year, and the table calc subtracts each plan's 2016 score from its later years.
  • You already know this idea:
    • in SQL it is a window function over an ordered result, x - FIRST_VALUE(x) OVER (ORDER BY Year)
    • in pandas it is x - x.iloc[0] on an ordered frame
  • Why it matters: "how much has this plan improved or slipped" is the question every quality team asks, and it is a table calc, not a raw number in the data

The view holds each plan's score for each year. The table calc walks along the years, per plan, and subtracts the first year's value from every year, so 2016 is always zero and later years read as the gain or loss since then.

In Tableau

  • Columns: Year (discrete); Rows: AVG(Aqfs)
  • Right click the AVG(Aqfs) pill → Quick Table Calculation → Difference From; then edit it to relative to First
  • Compute Using: Year (so the walk runs along the year axis, restarting per plan)

The same thing in SQL and pandas

  • SQL approach: an ordered window function, FIRST_VALUE(...) OVER (PARTITION BY unit ORDER BY Year), grabs each plan's 2016 score and subtracts it from every year
  • pandas approach: groupby(unit).transform(s - s.iloc[0]) walks each plan's year sorted scores and subtracts the first, the same baseline math
-- SQL: the ordered window is the table calc; FIRST_VALUE is "relative to First"
SELECT "Reporting Unit", Year, Aqfs,
       Aqfs - FIRST_VALUE(Aqfs) OVER (
              PARTITION BY "Reporting Unit" ORDER BY Year)  AS change_since_2016
FROM plans
ORDER BY "Reporting Unit", Year;
# pandas: subtract each group's first value, on a year sorted frame
plans = plans.sort_values(['Reporting Unit', 'Year'])
plans['change_since_2016'] = (plans.groupby('Reporting Unit')['Aqfs']
                               .transform(lambda s: s - s.iloc[0]))

The same thing in SQL and pandas

The same Difference From First calc as the Table Calculations tab, read out for the two Los Angeles plans (2016 is each plan's own baseline):

  • SQL approach: the same FIRST_VALUE window builds change since 2016 for every year, then MAX(CASE WHEN Year = 2023 ...) pulls out just each plan's 2023 net
  • pandas approach: the same per group subtraction builds the change column, then reads off the 2023 row for each plan
-- SQL: change since each plan's first year, then pull the 2023 net
WITH t AS (
    SELECT Year, "Reporting Unit" AS unit, Aqfs,
           Aqfs - FIRST_VALUE(Aqfs) OVER (
                  PARTITION BY "Reporting Unit" ORDER BY Year) AS change_since_2016
    FROM plans
)
SELECT unit,
       ROUND(MAX(CASE WHEN Year = 2023 THEN change_since_2016 END), 2) AS net_2016_to_2023
FROM t
WHERE unit IN ('LA Care - Los Angeles', 'Health Net - Los Angeles')
GROUP BY unit;
# pandas: subtract each plan's first year, then read the 2023 row
plans = plans.sort_values(['Reporting Unit', 'Year'])
plans['change_since_2016'] = (plans.groupby('Reporting Unit')['Aqfs']
                               .transform(lambda s: s - s.iloc[0]))

net = plans[plans.Year == 2023].set_index('Reporting Unit')['change_since_2016'].round(2)
# net['LA Care - Los Angeles']    -> -1.58
# net['Health Net - Los Angeles'] -> -9.36

Example: a year selector driving the ranking in all three tools

The parameter holds a year. A boolean bridge field compares each row's year to it, and that boolean goes on the Filters shelf set to True. Nothing about the chart changes except which year passes the filter.

In Tableau

// Select Year: integer parameter, values 2016 through 2023 // Year Matches: the bridge between parameter and data [Year] = [Select Year]
  • Create the integer parameter Select Year (list of the eight years), then Show Parameter
  • Drop the boolean Year Matches on Filters, keep True; the ranking now shows only the chosen year
  • The reference line reads the same year through its FIXED LOD, so the benchmark moves with the selection

The same thing in SQL and pandas

  • SQL approach: a WHERE clause picks the year (the parameter), then a RANK() OVER (ORDER BY Aqfs DESC) window numbers the plans best to worst
  • pandas approach: a boolean mask picks the year, then .rank(ascending=False) numbers them; filter first, then rank, is the order that matters
-- SQL: the parameter is a query variable; the filter runs first, then the rank
SELECT "Reporting Unit", Aqfs,
       RANK() OVER (ORDER BY Aqfs DESC) AS rnk
FROM plans
WHERE Year = :year                -- :year is the parameter
ORDER BY Aqfs DESC;
# pandas: the parameter is a plain variable
YEAR = 2023

ranked = (plans[plans.Year == YEAR]
          .sort_values('Aqfs', ascending=False)
          .assign(rnk=lambda d: d.Aqfs.rank(ascending=False, method='min')))

Abstract

Purpose

  • Build one scorecard where any viewer can see how California's Medi-Cal managed care plans compare on quality, with L.A. Care and its Los Angeles rival Health Net in focus
  • Serve as the capstone that puts the benchmark, table calc, and parameter methods from this post to work on one screen

Methods

  • DHCS composite quality scores (2016 to 2023) for the trend and ranking, with primary care provider ratios and member grievances as companions
  • Built with the FIXED benchmark, the Difference From First table calc, and the year parameter covered in the other tabs
  • Every displayed number verified identical in DuckDB SQL and pandas

The questions asked, and the answers

  • How has plan quality trended? L.A. Care finished above the statewide average in all eight years. It peaked in 2020 (72.2), dipped in the 2021 pandemic year, and settled at 59.3 by 2023, just below its own 2016 level. Its Los Angeles rival Health Net fell much further over the same span.
  • How does L.A. Care rank in 2023? 26th of 56 reporting units. Above the 57.41 statewide average, but mid pack, because small single region units hold the top spots.
  • How much has each plan changed since 2016? L.A. Care is down 1.58, Health Net down 9.36, while the statewide average is up 1.96; L.A. Care gave back its pandemic era gains, Health Net slid well past its starting point.
  • How do plans compare on primary care access? Measured as primary care doctors per 2,000 members, every plan clears California's minimum of 1; L.A. Care sits at 3.75, comfortably above the floor.
  • What do members complain about? Grievances are published statewide only, not by plan. Across all plans in 2023, Quality of Service is the largest category (216,256 of 595,072 total grievances).

Takeaway

  • L.A. Care is a steady, above average performer that slipped from a 2020 peak rather than a top ranked plan; the quality leaders are smaller regional units
  • Every interactive element on the dashboard is one of the methods from this post doing its job in public: the benchmark line, the year selector, and the change calculation
One honesty note. The "Statewide Average" line is a derived benchmark, the mean of each year's reporting unit scores, not an official DHCS published figure. And the grievance panel is statewide, so it shows what members complain about across the whole program, not a plan by plan comparison.
Putting it together: a quality trend with a statewide benchmark, a year selectable ranking with a benchmark reference line, and access and grievance companions, all on one scorecard. Use the Select Year control to re rank every plan.
Design decisions worth stealing
  • One color, everywhere: L.A. Care is green on every sheet, Health Net blue, the statewide average gray. A plan that changed color between sheets would quietly mislead.
  • The benchmark appears twice, on purpose: as a gray line in the trend and as a red dashed reference line in the ranking, so "above or below average" reads instantly in both.
  • One knob drives the ranking: the Select Year parameter sits right above the chart it controls, so the interaction is obvious.
  • Outliers handled honestly: the access chart drops one plan with a tiny Medi-Cal membership whose ratio would otherwise flatten every other bar, and says so, rather than letting it distort the scale.
