Beyond Recall: How Business Loss Functions Change Which Model Wins

A follow up to The Recall Precision Spectrum — retraining 15 classifiers under cost sensitive and profit based optimization

🔗 Companion post: This analysis builds on The Recall Precision Spectrum, which compares the same 15 classifiers optimized for recall alone. Start there if you want the model by model breakdown before reading this business lens.
Why This Analysis Exists

In the companion post, we optimized all 15 classifiers using recall as the primary metric. The goal was to catch as many defaults as possible. That is a reasonable starting point, but it ignores two realities:

  1. Not all errors cost the same. Missing a USD 50,000 default is far more expensive than rejecting a USD 5,000 good loan.
  2. The bank's goal is profit, not perfect classification. A model that catches 95% of defaults but rejects 40% of good borrowers may destroy more value than it saves.

This post runs the same 15 classifiers through two additional optimization frameworks and compares the results against the recall baseline. The central question: does the "best" model change when you optimize for business outcomes instead of statistical metrics?

Three Optimization Frameworks
Framework Objective Scorer What It Captures
Recall Optimized (baseline) Maximize default detection recall Catches defaults regardless of cost
Cost Sensitive Minimize expected misclassification cost Custom cost scorer Weighs FN vs FP by dollar impact

Each tunable model is trained three times — once per framework. Models without hyperparameters (Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, LDA, QDA) are trained once and evaluated from all three perspectives. This produces 45 total model evaluations across the 15 classifiers.

How the Custom Scorers Work

Cost Sensitive Scorer
\[\text{Total Cost} = (\text{FN} \times 9{,}304) + (\text{FP} \times 4{,}466)\]

sklearn maximizes, so the scorer returns −cost / n. Lower cost = better.

Profit Based Scorer
\[\text{Total Profit} = (\text{TN} \times 3{,}966) - (\text{FN} \times 9{,}804)\]

Approved good borrowers generate revenue. Approved defaulters destroy it. Rejections earn USD 0.

What Changes (and What Does Not) Across Frameworks

Boosted ensembles dominate every framework

CatBoost, LightGBM, and XGBoost hold the top 3 positions under both cost and profit optimization. Under recall optimization, CatBoost still leads, but SVM and Decision Tree climb into the top 5 because they sacrifice precision to catch more defaults.

Many models are insensitive to the scorer

Nine of the 15 models produced identical results across all three frameworks: Logistic Regression, Gaussian Naive Bayes, LDA, QDA, Bagging, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, SVM, and MLP. For these models, the hyperparameter grid did not contain configurations that differentiate recall from cost/profit optimization. The three custom scorers all chose the same best hyperparameters.

Implication for practitioners: If your model is insensitive to the scorer, it may mean the hyperparameter grid is too narrow. Alternatively, the model family may lack the capacity to exploit the cost asymmetry. Either way, you should also tune the decision threshold (see the Threshold tab).

The models that do change

CatBoost, KNN, Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM all found different optimal hyperparameters under at least one scorer. CatBoost showed the most dramatic shift, selecting a deeper tree with a faster learning rate when optimizing for business metrics.

Why Threshold Optimization Matters

The default classification threshold of 0.50 is arbitrary. It assumes that the cost of a false positive equals the cost of a false negative, which is almost never true in business applications. By sweeping the threshold from 0.01 to 0.99, we find the operating point that maximizes profit (or minimizes cost) for each model — without retraining.

Threshold optimization is free uplift. It requires only a holdout set with predicted probabilities. No additional training, no new features, no hyperparameter search. Just move the decision boundary.

Threshold Sweep: Top 3 Profit Models

CatBoost

At the default threshold of 0.50, CatBoost (profit optimized) earns USD 3,950,859 in portfolio profit. Moving the threshold to 0.24 increases profit to USD 4,061,117 — a USD 110,258 uplift (2.8%) with zero retraining. The cost optimized threshold also lands at 0.24, confirming that cost and profit align at this operating point.

LightGBM

LightGBM shows the most dramatic threshold effect. At 0.50, it earns USD 3,921,890. At its optimal threshold of 0.10, profit jumps to USD 4,073,558 — a USD 151,668 uplift (3.9%). This is the single highest profit figure across all models and all thresholds. However, the aggressive 0.10 threshold means the model classifies any loan with >10% default probability as risky.

XGBoost

XGBoost moves from USD 3,862,180 at threshold 0.50 to USD 3,984,115 at threshold 0.26, a USD 121,935 uplift (3.2%). Both cost and profit optimization converge on the same threshold.

A subtlety worth noting: After threshold optimization, LightGBM (USD 4,073,558) slightly outperforms CatBoost (USD 4,061,117). The model that wins at the default threshold is not necessarily the model that wins after threshold tuning. This is why threshold optimization should always be the final step.
Recovery Rate Sensitivity

The recovery rate is the single most influential business parameter. It determines how much money the bank gets back when a borrower defaults and the property is foreclosed. We hold all other parameters constant and sweep recovery from 30% to 70%.

What Drives the Crossover?

As recovery rate increases, the cost of a false negative drops while the cost of a false positive stays constant. The cost ratio falls from 2.92x at 30% recovery to 1.25x at 70%. Once the cost ratio approaches 1:1, the model that rejects fewer good loans (higher precision) wins over the model that catches more defaults (higher recall).

Practical guidance: If your institution has strong collateral recovery processes (recovery rate above 50%), favor LightGBM. If recovery is uncertain or weak, favor CatBoost. Either way, both models outperform the other 13 at every recovery rate tested.
Implications for Production

Sensitivity analysis is not an academic exercise. These parameters change over time:

  • Recovery rates shift with housing market conditions. In a downturn, foreclosure recovery drops; in a boom, it climbs.
  • Interest rates change with monetary policy. Higher rates increase the opportunity cost of rejecting good borrowers (higher FP cost).
  • Loan terms vary by product. A 15 year mortgage has different economics than a 5 year home equity line.

Any production system should recalibrate these parameters quarterly and re-evaluate model rankings. A model that was optimal last quarter may not be optimal this quarter if the macro environment has shifted.

Key Findings

1. The optimization objective changes the "best" model

QuestionFrameworkWhat Matters Most
Which model catches the most defaults? Recall True positive rate regardless of false positives
Which model minimizes total dollar losses? Cost Sensitive Weighted error cost penalizes missed defaults more
Which model makes the bank the most money? Profit Based Net portfolio value balances revenue against losses

2. Recall optimization is a starting point, not a destination

A model that catches 95% of defaults but rejects 40% of profitable borrowers may destroy value rather than create it. Recall tells you how many defaults you caught; it says nothing about how many good customers you turned away.

3. Cost sensitive and profit based training converge

When the cost asymmetry is moderate (2.08x in this dataset), minimizing cost and maximizing profit select the same hyperparameters and the same model. They diverge more when the asymmetry is extreme.

4. Threshold optimization is free uplift

Even without retraining, moving the decision threshold from 0.50 to the profit optimal point unlocked USD 110,000 to USD 150,000 in additional portfolio value for the top models. After threshold optimization, LightGBM narrowly overtakes CatBoost.

5. Sensitivity to assumptions matters

The recovery rate assumption alone changes which model ranks first. CatBoost wins at 30% to 40% recovery; LightGBM wins at 50% to 70%. Any production deployment should include regular recalibration of these parameters.

Recommended Two Stage Framework for Production
Stage 1: Train to maximize recall or minimize cost. These objectives are more stable during cross validation and produce models that reliably catch defaults. Use the full hyperparameter grid with a cost sensitive scorer to find the right balance.
Stage 2: Set the decision threshold using profit optimization. On a holdout calibration set, sweep thresholds from 0.01 to 0.99 and select the threshold that maximizes portfolio profit. This decouples the training objective (catch defaults) from the deployment objective (make money).
Stage 3 (monitoring): Track both recall and profit metrics in production. If recall drops below a safety floor (for example, 0.70), override the threshold regardless of profit impact. This gives you the safety of catching defaults while maximizing business value within that constraint.

Summary

This analysis demonstrates a fundamental truth in applied machine learning: the "best" model depends entirely on what you are optimizing for. Statistical metrics like recall and F1 are useful starting points, but they are not business metrics. The model that catches the most defaults is not necessarily the model that makes the most money. Any serious production system should evaluate candidates through the lens of the business problem it is solving.
