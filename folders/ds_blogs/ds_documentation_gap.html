Executive Summary

Every year, U.S. acute care hospitals lose billions of dollars not because they deliver less care, but because their clinical documentation does not fully capture the severity of the patients they treat. Under the Medicare Severity Diagnosis Related Group system, incomplete documentation means cases get classified into lower-paying tiers even when the clinical reality warranted more. A growing market of AI-powered Clinical Documentation Improvement (CDI) technology—including prebill review engines, autonomous coding platforms, and denial management systems—now offers hospitals a path to recover this missed revenue at scale, but doing so requires first understanding where the gaps are, how large they are, and which hospitals stand to benefit most.

This analysis sets out to:

  • Quantify the documentation gap across approximately 3,280 U.S. acute care hospitals (those reimbursed under Medicare’s Inpatient Prospective Payment System) using publicly available CMS provider utilization and payment data.
  • Estimate the revenue at stake by modeling how many cases at each hospital would shift to a higher-severity DRG if documentation fully reflected clinical complexity.
  • Identify what hospital characteristics predict larger gaps, so that hospitals and AI-CDI vendors can prioritize where documentation improvement technology would deliver the greatest return.

Key Findings

  • The documentation gap is real and measurable. Using peer-group benchmarking across 42 hospital cohorts (by bed size, teaching status, and ownership), 8% of hospitals score in the "High" documentation gap tier (score ≥ 65), with another 39% in the "Medium" tier. These hospitals have Case Mix Index values, severity mixes, and payment patterns that fall significantly below structurally similar peers.
  • Revenue impact is concentrated but widespread. The top 20 hospitals by estimated opportunity account for hundreds of millions, but the median hospital still has a ~$272K annual opportunity. The shift model estimates that even modest improvements in documentation could recover $5,791 per shifted case (blended Complication or Comorbidity / Major Complication or Comorbidity uplift), consistent with published CC/MCC capture rate benchmarks.
  • Diagnosis Related Group Diversity is the strongest predictor. Hospitals treating a broader mix of conditions have substantially higher gap scores (+5.01 per standard deviation). A wider case mix means more documentation complexity and more opportunities for severity codes to be missed. Wage index (−2.77) and CMS quality scores (−1.94) are the next strongest drivers, suggesting that hospitals in lower-wage markets with fewer resources and weaker quality performance face compounding documentation challenges.
  • H1 (Ownership): Partially supported. For-profit hospitals have slightly lower gap scores than government hospitals (47.5 vs 48.2), but the difference is not statistically significant (p = 0.233). Ownership alone is a weak predictor of documentation quality, suggesting that organizational structure matters less than operational factors like staffing and case mix.
  • H2 (Staffing): Mixed support. Higher Registered Nurse skill mix was significantly associated with lower gap scores (−0.72, p < 0.001), supporting the idea that a more skilled clinical workforce contributes to better documentation. However, the resident-to-bed ratio showed the opposite pattern, likely reflecting the confounding effect of case complexity in teaching hospitals. Staffing composition matters more than staffing volume.
  • The model explains ~21% of variance — and that is informative. The regression captures what publicly available structural data can predict. The remaining ~80% is driven by factors not in public datasets: Clinical Documentation Improvement program maturity, EHR system quality, coder expertise, and physician engagement. The significant predictors identified here serve as observable risk signals that can flag which hospitals are most likely to benefit from documentation improvement — even without seeing inside their operations.
Implication for hospital leaders and Clinical Documentation Improvement vendors:

The hospitals most likely to have large documentation gaps are identifiable from public data: they treat a wide variety of conditions, operate in lower-wage markets, and have a lower share of registered nurses. The data suggests three priorities:
  • Invest in AI-powered Clinical Documentation Improvement technology that scales across a broad case mix.
  • Focus coding resources on high-diversity Diagnosis Related Group families where severity capture is weakest.
  • Strengthen the Registered Nurse workforce — one of the few modifiable factors significantly associated with better documentation (AHIMA Clinical Documentation Improvement Toolkit, 2024).

Background: How Medicare Payment and Severity Tiers Work

Medicare pays hospitals for inpatient stays through the Inpatient Prospective Payment System. Each admission is assigned a Medicare Severity Diagnosis Related Group, which determines the base payment amount. Many clinical conditions have three severity tiers that map to different Diagnosis Related Groups within the same family. For example, a heart failure admission might be classified as Diagnosis Related Group 291 (Heart Failure with Major Complication or Comorbidity), Diagnosis Related Group 292 (with Complication or Comorbidity), or Diagnosis Related Group 293 (without Complication or Comorbidity / Major Complication or Comorbidity). The payment difference between these tiers is substantial.

From Patient Intake to Medicare Payment

Understanding how a hospital stay becomes a Medicare payment helps explain why documentation matters so much. Hospitals do not bill Medicare a dollar amount for each service the way a restaurant presents a check. Instead, the Inpatient Prospective Payment System pays a single, predetermined amount for each admission based on the patient’s assigned Diagnosis Related Group. The entire pipeline—from bedside care to bank deposit—runs through five stages.

Two things follow from this pipeline:

  • The DRG weight is the single largest driver of payment. All the policy adjustments—wage index, teaching status, safety-net add-ons—multiply against the DRG weight. A case that groups to a higher-severity DRG generates a proportionally larger payment at every hospital in the country.
  • The hospital’s only lever is the quality of the clinical codes it submits in Step 1. If a physician treats a patient for heart failure with respiratory failure but the documentation does not specify the respiratory failure clearly enough for coders to capture it, the Grouper will assign a lower-severity DRG, the payment will be lower, and the hospital’s year-end Case Mix Index will reflect that gap.

How CMS Measures Hospital Complexity: The Case Mix Index

Every Medicare inpatient case is assigned a Diagnosis Related Group with a relative weight—a number that reflects how costly that type of case is compared to the national average. A weight of 1.0 is average; a weight of 2.0 means the case is roughly twice as resource-intensive. A hospital’s Case Mix Index is simply the weighted average of all those DRG weights across every discharge in a year. In plain terms, it is a single number that summarizes how sick a hospital’s patients are, on average.

CMS calculates and publishes each hospital’s Case Mix Index as part of the annual Inpatient Prospective Payment System Impact File. Hospitals do not self-report this number—it is derived directly from the claims data they submit to Medicare. The national average Case Mix Index in the current data is approximately 1.76. When a hospital’s Case Mix Index falls significantly below structurally similar peers, one likely explanation is that its clinical documentation is not fully capturing patient severity (Tieder et al., 2014).

Show example: How Case Mix Index is calculated

Here is how it works. When a patient is discharged, coders review the physician’s documentation and assign the case to a Diagnosis Related Group. That DRG carries a fixed relative weight set by CMS—every hospital in the country uses the same weight for the same DRG. The weight is not unique to the patient; it belongs to the DRG category the patient was assigned to. Two patients assigned to DRG 291 both carry the same weight of 1.3049.

A quick note on severity tiers, since they appear throughout this analysis: CC stands for Complication or Comorbidity—a secondary diagnosis that increases the complexity or cost of care (for example, diabetes in a heart failure patient). MCC stands for Major Complication or Comorbidity—a more severe secondary condition (for example, respiratory failure in a heart failure patient). Cases documented without CC/MCC receive the lowest-weighted DRG in the family.

To calculate the Case Mix Index, CMS takes the sum of the DRG relative weights across every individual discharge at the hospital in a fiscal year, then divides by the total number of discharges (CMS Case Mix Index File documentation). Here is a simplified example with 5 discharges:

Notice that Discharge 3 was coded as Heart Failure with CC (DRG 292, weight 0.86) rather than with MCC (DRG 291, weight 1.30). The patient had the same condition as Discharges 1 and 2, but the documentation did not support the higher severity tier. If that patient actually had a major complication that went undocumented, correcting it would shift Discharge 3 from DRG 292 to DRG 291, raising the hospital’s Case Mix Index from 1.61 to 1.70—a meaningful change from a single chart.

Where Documentation Breaks Down: The Clinical Documentation Gap

The gap between care delivered and care documented sits at the heart of hospital reimbursement. A patient might receive complex, resource-intensive care for multiple chronic conditions, but if the physician’s notes do not reflect that complexity with the right specificity, the hospital gets paid as if the case were routine. As shown in the payment pipeline above, the CMS Grouper can only work with the codes it receives—it has no way to know what the physician observed but did not write down. The result is a systematic understatement of patient severity that costs hospitals revenue on every affected claim and depresses their year-end Case Mix Index.

Clinical Documentation Improvement (CDI) programs attempt to close this gap. In a traditional CDI workflow, trained specialists review charts while the patient is still admitted, identify places where the physician’s documentation lacks the specificity needed for accurate coding, and send queries asking the physician to clarify. For example, a CDI specialist might notice that a patient is receiving IV diuretics and has a creatinine of 3.2 but the progress note says only “heart failure”—missing the acute kidney injury that would support a higher-severity DRG. The specialist queries the physician, the physician amends the note, and the coder can now capture the additional diagnosis before the claim is submitted (AHIMA Journal, “Revenue Impact of Clinical Documentation Improvement”).

AI-Powered Clinical Documentation Improvement

A new generation of AI-powered tools is automating and scaling different parts of this process. These platforms use natural language processing and large language models to read clinical notes, identify gaps, and surface recommendations—but they differ in when they intervene. They operate at three distinct points in the revenue cycle:

  • Prebill review (post-discharge, pre-submission): After the patient is discharged and the CDI and coding teams have finished their work, an AI performs a second-pass review of every chart before the claim is billed. It identifies diagnoses that the documentation supports but that coders missed, giving the hospital a chance to correct the claim before it ever reaches CMS. SmarterDx pioneered this approach with its SmarterPrebill product, which customers report uncovering millions in net new revenue per year (Becker’s Hospital Review; KLAS Emerging Solutions Spotlight, 2023). The company also offers SmarterDenials for automated denial overturn and recovery. In 2025, New Mountain Capital acquired SmarterDx and combined it with Thoughtful AI and Access Healthcare to form Smarter Technologies, adding concurrent documentation capabilities through SmarterNotes (Business Wire, Sept 2025).
  • Autonomous coding (pre-submission): Rather than assisting human coders, these platforms replace the coder entirely. After the physician finalizes the documentation, the AI reads the chart, assigns ICD-10 codes, and returns complete coded encounters ready for billing—with no human review required on most cases. Fathom leads this category, reporting 95%+ automation rates at 98%+ accuracy across outpatient specialties, with inpatient DRG coding in active development (KLAS, 2024; HIT Consultant, 2026). Aidéo Technologies and Neural Wave also compete in this space.
  • Full-lifecycle RCM automation (pre-submission + post-submission + denial management): Some platforms span the entire revenue cycle—from eligibility verification and prior authorization through claim submission, post-payment audit, and automated denial appeals. AKASA (Series B, $200M+ raised) is the most mature player here. In May 2025, SmarterDx, Thoughtful AI, and Access Healthcare merged under New Mountain Capital to form Smarter Technologies, creating a full-lifecycle platform combining prebill CDI, RCM automation agents, and outsourced coding operations.

Purpose of This Analysis and Hypotheses

This blog post sets out to do three things:

  • Quantify the clinical documentation gap across the U.S. acute care hospital landscape by building a composite Documentation Gap Score from publicly available CMS data.
  • Estimate the revenue left on the table nationally by modeling how many cases at each hospital would shift to a higher-severity DRG if documentation fully reflected clinical complexity.
  • Identify the hospital-level characteristics that predict larger or smaller documentation gaps, so that hospitals and CDI vendors can prioritize resources where they are most needed.

To address the third objective, the analysis uses an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression model with the Documentation Gap Score as the dependent variable and a set of structural hospital characteristics as independent variables. Two specific hypotheses are tested:

  • H1 (Ownership): For-profit hospitals have smaller documentation gaps than government hospitals, because they may invest more heavily in revenue cycle operations and CDI programs (cf. Tieder et al., 2014 on hospital variables and case mix).
  • H2 (Staffing): Hospitals with higher provider-to-patient ratios have smaller documentation gaps, because more clinicians per patient means more capacity to document thoroughly (cf. Pillai et al., 2014 on staffing and documentation quality).

Beyond these two hypotheses, the regression includes additional structural controls—bed count, teaching status, case-mix diversity, geographic wage index, and urbanicity—to isolate the independent effect of ownership and staffing after accounting for other factors that may drive documentation quality. Analysis Overview and Data Pipeline

This analysis draws on five publicly available CMS datasets, all linked by the CMS Certification Number (CCN)—a unique six-character identifier that CMS assigns to every Medicare-certified facility (the first two digits indicate the state; the remaining four identify the provider). The data spans Fiscal Year 2022 through Q4 2025, reflecting the most recent available releases at the time of analysis: inpatient claims and payment data from the 2022 Medicare Inpatient Public Use File, hospital-level Case Mix Index and payment parameters from the FY 2025 IPPS Impact File, DRG relative weights from the FY 2025 Final Rule, staffing data from the Q4 2025 Provider of Services file, and quality performance measures from the 2024 Hospital General Information dataset. After merging and cleaning, the final analytic dataset covers approximately 3,280 acute care hospitals (those reimbursed under Medicare’s Inpatient Prospective Payment System) with over 50 engineered features.

From the merged data, I engineered features across several categories: peer-group benchmarks, gap sub-scores, revenue impact estimates, staffing ratios, and quality composites. Hospitals were grouped into peer cohorts and all gap metrics were computed relative to peer means. The pipeline culminates in a composite Documentation Gap Score, a revenue impact model, and a suite of regression models to identify the structural and staffing characteristics most associated with documentation risk.

What Determines a Hospital’s Diagnosis Related Group Mix

Before comparing hospitals on documentation quality, it helps to understand what shapes the mix of Diagnosis Related Groups a hospital treats in the first place. Think of a hospital’s DRG profile as its menu of services—the range of conditions it is equipped to treat based on its capacity, clinical workforce, and specialized resources. A cardiac surgery program, a Level I trauma designation, or a neonatal intensive care unit each open entire families of DRGs that a hospital without those capabilities will never see. This menu is not primarily a function of patient demographics. It is driven by a combination of structural and operational factors:

  • Scope of clinical services: A hospital with cardiac surgery, a Level I trauma center, neurosurgery, and an oncology program will treat a much broader mix of DRG families than a community hospital without those service lines. Two hospitals in the same zip code serving identical populations can have very different DRG diversity if one is a full-service academic medical center and the other is a focused surgical facility.
  • Geographic isolation: A rural hospital that is the only facility within 60 miles tends to treat everything that presents—orthopedics, cardiac, respiratory, obstetric, psychiatric emergencies—driving up DRG diversity not because the surrounding population is different, but because there is no nearby facility to absorb specialty cases.
  • Referral patterns: Teaching hospitals and regional referral centers attract complex cases from a wide catchment area, pulling in rare and unusual DRG families that inflate diversity beyond what the local population would generate on its own.
  • Bed count and capacity: Larger hospitals can staff more departments and accept a wider range of admissions, mechanically increasing the number of distinct DRG families they handle.
  • Patient demographics (secondary effect): An older population generates more varied inpatient conditions than a younger one, and communities with specific occupational or environmental risks may add certain DRG families. However, this effect is smaller than the hospital capability and geography factors listed above.

This distinction matters for interpreting the results. When the analysis finds that DRG diversity is the strongest predictor of documentation gaps, it is not saying that patient demographics drive poor documentation. It is saying that hospitals with a broader scope of services face more documentation complexity—more condition types means more coding rules to follow, more severity tiers to distinguish, and more opportunities for a clinically present complication to go undocumented. The regression model includes DRG diversity as an independent variable precisely to measure this effect, alongside controls for bed count, teaching status, wage index, and urbanicity that capture the structural factors listed above.

How Hospitals Are Compared: Peer Group Benchmarking

Comparing a 20-bed critical access hospital to a 600-bed academic medical center would be misleading—they serve different patient populations, have different resources, and operate under different economic conditions. To make meaningful comparisons, hospitals are grouped into peer cohorts based on the intersection of three structural characteristics:

  • Bed size tier (6 categories): 1–24, 25–99, 100–199, 200–399, 400–599, and 600+.
  • Teaching status (2 categories): teaching vs. non-teaching, based on whether the hospital operates a graduate medical education program with a resident-to-bed ratio above zero.
  • Ownership type (4 categories): for-profit (e.g., HCA or Tenet facilities); nonprofit (including church-affiliated and other voluntary hospitals); government (state, local, and hospital-district authorities); and other (Veterans Health Administration, Department of Defense, Tribal, and physician-owned hospitals).

The theoretical maximum is 48 combinations (6 × 2 × 4). After removing combinations with fewer than 5 hospitals, 42 peer groups remain—a reasonable number given approximately 3,280 IPPS acute care hospitals across the United States, yielding an average of roughly 78 hospitals per group. All gap metrics are computed relative to peer group means, so a hospital’s Documentation Gap Score reflects how it performs against structurally similar hospitals, not the national average.

Measuring the Documentation Gap: Where Hospital Claims Fall Short of Clinical Reality

With peer groups established, the next step is to distill each hospital’s performance relative to its peers into a single, comparable number.

The Documentation Gap Score answers a straightforward question:

How much does this hospital’s documented severity fall short of what structurally similar hospitals achieve?

To understand what this means in practice, consider two nonprofit, non-teaching hospitals of similar size—both with around 150 beds in the same peer group. Hospital A documents at or above the peer average across all four signals. Hospital B treats similar patients but consistently codes cases at lower severity tiers—its heart failure patients land in DRG 292 (With CC) more often than DRG 291 (With MCC), and its sepsis cases are coded without major complications more frequently than its peers.   • As the input to the Revenue Impact Model, where higher-gap hospitals are assigned proportionally higher severity shift rates—the estimated percentage of cases that would move from a lower-paying DRG to a higher-paying one if documentation captured all clinically present conditions—to estimate how much revenue each hospital may be leaving on the table.

How the Documentation Gap Score Is Constructed

The Documentation Gap Score is a composite index scaled from 0 to 100 that combines four normalized sub-scores. Each sub-score captures a different signal of potential under-documentation, and each is normalized so that 50 represents the peer-group average and higher values indicate a larger gap:

  • Case Mix Index Gap Score — how far a hospital’s Case Mix Index falls below its peer group mean. A hospital whose patients appear “less sick on paper” than structurally similar peers scores higher on this dimension.
  • Severity Score — the share of cases coded with a Major Complication or Comorbidity (MCC) compared to peers. A hospital that captures fewer high-severity codes than expected scores higher.
  • Payment Score — whether per-case Medicare payments are lower than expected given the hospital’s peer group. Because DRG weight drives payment, lower payments relative to peers suggest that cases are landing in lower-paying severity tiers.
  • Complexity Score — the breadth of Diagnosis Related Group families the hospital treats and its bed count, reflecting how much documentation surface area exists. Larger hospitals with broader service menus have more opportunities for severity codes to be missed.

Why these methods?
Case Mix Index Gap Score uses a z-score relative to the peer group because CMI distributions vary widely across peer groups—a CMI of 1.4 may be above average for a small rural cohort but well below average for a large teaching hospital cohort. Standardizing within the peer group ensures the score reflects how far a hospital deviates from structurally similar peers, not from a national average that would penalize smaller hospitals. Because a hospital below its peer mean produces a negative z-score, the formula multiplies by −1 before scaling—this ensures that a larger shortfall translates to a higher sub-score, consistent with the convention that higher values indicate larger documentation gaps.
Severity Score uses a simple gap from the peer group mean MCC ratio rather than a z-score because MCC ratios are bounded between 0 and 1 and have relatively uniform variance across peer groups. A fixed clipping window of ±0.3 is sufficient to capture meaningful variation without the added complexity of group-specific standard deviations.
Payment Score computes the gap against the peer group average (to isolate documentation-related payment shortfalls from structural differences), but normalizes using the global standard deviation across all 3,280 hospitals. This is because peer groups with very few hospitals can produce unstable within-group standard deviations; using the national spread provides a more stable denominator.
Complexity Score uses national percentile ranks rather than peer-relative measures because it captures absolute documentation surface area—how many distinct DRG families and beds a hospital has—regardless of its peer group. A 600-bed hospital treating 120 DRG families has more documentation complexity than a 50-bed hospital treating 30 families, even if both are average within their respective peer groups. Each percentile (ranging from 0 to 1) is multiplied by 50 so that the two components contribute equally and the combined result falls on the same 0–100 scale used by the other three sub-scores.

While no single standardized documentation gap metric exists in the literature, this composite approach builds directly on established Clinical Documentation Improvement methods:

  • The American Health Information Management Association recommends using Complication/Major Complication capture rates and peer-benchmarked Case Mix Index comparisons as key performance indicators for identifying documentation opportunities (AHIMA, “Using CC/MCC Capture Rates as a Key Performance Indicator”).
  • Pillai et al. demonstrated that a normalized Case Mix Index can isolate documentation quality from patient acuity when comparing across Diagnosis Related Group families (Pillai et al., 2014, Perspectives in Health Information Management).
  • Each sub-score component is individually well-established in Clinical Documentation Improvement practice (AHIMA Clinical Documentation Improvement Toolkit).

How the Revenue Model Works: Key Terms and Definitions

The following terms define how the revenue impact model translates documentation gaps into dollar estimates. Understanding these terms is essential to reading the model’s inputs, outputs, and the worked example that follows.

  • Severity shift — when improved documentation moves a case from a lower-paying severity tier to a higher-paying one. No clinical care changes; only the documentation accuracy changes. Three shift paths are possible:
    • “Without CC/MCC” (baseline) → “With CC” (Complication/Comorbidity tier)
    • “Without CC/MCC” (baseline) → “With MCC” (Major Complication/Comorbidity tier)
    • “With CC” → “With MCC” (moderate to major severity)
  • Complication/Comorbidity (CC) uplift — the average additional payment Medicare makes when a case moves from the baseline tier to the middle “With CC” tier. Computed as the mean payment difference across all Diagnosis Related Group families in the CMS Inpatient PUF.
    Example: A heart failure case coded as DRG 293 (Without CC/MCC) pays approximately $4,900. If documentation captures a complication like chronic kidney disease, the case shifts to DRG 292 (With CC), which pays approximately $8,157.
    Uplift = $8,157 − $4,900 = $3,257
    The national average CC uplift across all DRG families is $3,257.
  • Major Complication/Comorbidity (MCC) uplift — the average additional payment when a case moves from baseline to the highest “With MCC” tier. This is much larger because MCC cases reflect the most resource-intensive patients.
    Example: The same heart failure case coded as DRG 293 (Without CC/MCC) pays approximately $4,900. If documentation captures a major complication like acute respiratory failure, the case shifts to DRG 291 (With MCC), which pays approximately $16,606.
    Uplift = $16,606 − $4,900 = $11,706
    The national average MCC uplift across all DRG families is $11,706.
  • Blended uplift ($5,791) — a weighted average assuming 70% of shifted cases land in the CC tier and 30% reach the MCC tier: ($3,257 × 0.70) + ($11,706 × 0.30) = $5,791.
  • Shift rate — the estimated percentage of a hospital’s Medicare discharges that could be reclassified to a higher severity tier through better documentation. This rate scales with the hospital’s Documentation Gap Score: a hospital with a larger gap has more cases where clinically present conditions are likely going undocumented, so a higher proportion of its discharges are candidates for reclassification.
    Example: A hospital with a Documentation Gap Score of 59 (Medium tier) receives a shift rate of 5.93%, meaning roughly 6 out of every 100 Medicare discharges are estimated to have undocumented complications that, if captured, would move the case to a higher-paying DRG.

How Shift Rates Scale with Documentation Gap Severity

The shift rate is not a single number—it increases linearly within each gap tier, reflecting the idea that hospitals with bigger documentation gaps have proportionally more cases that could be reclassified.

Important assumption: These shift rates are analyst-defined estimates, not empirically calibrated from chart-audit data. They are intentionally conservative relative to industry benchmarks—published Clinical Documentation Improvement programs typically improve 15–25% of targeted cases, but this model accounts for hospitals that may not have a full program in place. The tier boundaries are aligned with the composite Documentation Gap Score classification (Low, Medium, High) to maintain consistency across the analysis. Linear interpolation within each tier ensures smooth transitions and avoids hard jumps at tier boundaries.

Testing the Hypotheses: Statistical Models Used in This Analysis

With gap scores and revenue estimates in hand, the final step is to test whether observable hospital characteristics can predict where documentation gaps are largest. I used several statistical approaches, each serving a distinct purpose:

  • Hypothesis testing (H1 — Ownership): Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests compare gap score distributions across ownership categories (for-profit, nonprofit, government). These are non-parametric tests, meaning they do not assume the data follows a bell curve—appropriate here because gap scores are not perfectly normally distributed. The Mann-Whitney U test is run one-sided to specifically test whether for-profit hospitals have lower gap scores than government hospitals.
  • Hypothesis testing (H2 — Staffing): Spearman and Pearson correlations measure the relationship between staffing ratios (Registered Nurse skill mix, resident-to-bed ratio) and gap scores. Spearman captures any monotonic relationship (not just straight-line), while Pearson measures linear correlation. ANOVA is used to compare gap scores across staffing quartiles.
  • Primary regression model: An enriched Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression with 17 predictors serves as the main analytical model. OLS estimates how much each hospital characteristic contributes to the Documentation Gap Score while holding all other characteristics constant—in plain terms, it isolates the independent effect of each factor. I started with 10 structural features (bed size, ownership, teaching status, urban/rural, etc.) and enriched the model with staffing ratios from the Provider of Services file and quality metrics from Hospital General Information, bringing the total to 17 predictors. All coefficients are standardized to one-standard-deviation units, so the size of each coefficient can be directly compared: a coefficient of +5.01 on DRG Diversity means a one-standard-deviation increase in diversity is associated with a 5-point increase in gap score.
  • Model validation: To confirm that OLS was the right choice, I compared it against three regularized alternatives—Ridge regression (which penalizes large coefficients to reduce overfitting), Lasso regression (which can shrink irrelevant coefficients to zero, effectively removing them), and ElasticNet (a blend of Ridge and Lasso)—using 5-fold cross-validation (splitting the data into five groups, training on four and testing on the fifth, rotating through all five). All four models produced nearly identical cross-validated R² values (0.19–0.21), confirming that the 17-feature OLS specification is lean enough without needing regularization to prune or shrink coefficients.
  • Classification model: I also fit a logistic regression to predict high-gap status (score ≥ 65 vs. below 65), achieving an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.75. AUC measures how well the model distinguishes high-gap from non-high-gap hospitals on a scale from 0.5 (no better than a coin flip) to 1.0 (perfect separation)—0.75 indicates reasonably good discrimination using only publicly available structural characteristics.
  • Residual diagnostics: I reviewed standard diagnostic plots (residuals vs. fitted values, Q-Q plot, histogram of residuals, and scale-location plot) to verify that the OLS model does not violate key assumptions: that errors are approximately normally distributed, that variance is roughly constant across predicted values (homoscedasticity), and that the relationship between predictors and outcome is approximately linear. No major violations were detected.

This analysis focuses on acute care hospitals—facilities that provide short-term treatment for serious illness, injury, or surgery—because they are the hospitals reimbursed under Medicare’s Inpatient Prospective Payment System, where documentation directly determines payment through Diagnosis Related Group assignment. The dataset covers 3,280 such hospitals, ranging from small critical access facilities with fewer than 25 beds to large urban academic medical centers with 600+. The median hospital has around 185 beds, while the mean sits at 268. About one-third are teaching hospitals.

The first question is whether different types of hospitals treat systematically different patient populations, because that context matters for interpreting documentation gaps. To measure patient complexity, this analysis uses the Case Mix Index—a number that reflects how sick or resource-intensive a hospital’s average Medicare patient is. A Case Mix Index of 1.0 means average complexity; values above 1.0 mean the hospital treats more complex cases than the national norm, and values below 1.0 mean less complex. Case Mix Diversity and Wage Index Predict the Largest Gaps

To move from describing where gaps exist to understanding why, I tested two hypotheses and fit an enriched Ordinary Least Squares regression model with 17 predictors.

H1 (Ownership): Partially supported. For-profit hospitals have a slightly lower mean gap score (47.5) than government hospitals (48.2), but the difference is not statistically significant (Mann-Whitney U, one-sided p = 0.233). In the regression, nonprofit status was the only ownership category that reached significance (p = 0.017), associated with a modest increase in gap score relative to for-profit hospitals. Ownership alone is a weak predictor of documentation quality.

H2 (Staffing): Mixed support. Higher Registered Nurse skill mix (the share of nursing staff who are registered nurses) was significantly associated with lower gap scores (Spearman r, p < 0.001), supporting the idea that a more skilled clinical workforce contributes to better documentation. However, the resident-to-bed ratio showed a significant positive correlation, meaning hospitals with more residents had higher gap scores — likely reflecting the confounding effect of case complexity in teaching hospitals. Staffing composition matters more than staffing volume.

The regression model (R² = 0.21, Adj R² = 0.20) explains about one-fifth of the variance in gap scores. This is a modest fit — and intentionally so. The publicly available CMS data captures hospital structure, staffing, and quality performance, but it cannot observe the factors that most directly determine documentation quality: whether a hospital has a Clinical Documentation Improvement program, how mature its EHR system is, how skilled its coders are, or how engaged its physicians are in documentation. These unmeasured operational factors almost certainly explain the remaining 80% of the variance. What the model can tell us is which observable, structural characteristics reliably signal higher documentation risk — and those signals are strong enough to be actionable. The following predictors all reached statistical significance (p < 0.05):

1. Diagnosis Related Group Diversity (+5.01) — by far the strongest signal. Hospitals treating a broader mix of conditions have substantially higher gap scores. More condition types means more documentation complexity and more opportunities for severity codes to be missed. A one standard deviation increase in Diagnosis Related Group diversity is associated with a 5-point increase in gap score. Importantly, a hospital’s DRG diversity reflects its menu of services—whether it operates cardiac surgery, trauma, oncology, neonatal intensive care, and similar specialty programs—rather than patient demographics. Hospitals with broader service menus face more coding rules, more severity tiers to distinguish, and more opportunities for a clinically present complication to go undocumented. (See What Determines a Hospital’s Diagnosis Related Group Mix in the Methods section for a fuller discussion.)

2. Wage Index (−2.77) — hospitals in lower-wage labor markets have larger gaps. This likely reflects that these facilities have fewer resources to invest in Clinical Documentation Improvement programs, coding staff, and EHR infrastructure.

3. Quality Score (−1.94) — hospitals with worse CMS quality performance (mortality, safety, readmission) also have larger documentation gaps, suggesting that documentation quality and clinical quality share common institutional drivers.

4. Resident/Bed Ratio (−1.88) — after controlling for teaching status, more residents per bed is associated with lower gap scores, possibly because residents generate more detailed clinical notes.

5. Teaching Status (+0.97) — teaching hospitals show higher gaps overall, likely because their complex case mix creates more documentation opportunities to miss.

6. Urban (+0.87) — urban hospitals have modestly higher gaps than rural ones.

7. Readmission % Worse (−0.83) — counterintuitively, hospitals with worse readmission rates show slightly lower gap scores, possibly because readmission scrutiny drives more thorough documentation of comorbidities.

8. Registered Nurse Skill Mix (−0.72) — a higher share of registered nurses is protective. Better-trained clinical staff appear to contribute to more complete documentation.

What this means for hospital leaders and Clinical Documentation Improvement vendors:

The hospitals most likely to have large documentation gaps are identifiable from public data alone — they treat a wide variety of conditions, operate in lower-wage markets, score below peers on CMS quality measures, and have a lower share of registered nurses. These are the facilities where AI-powered Clinical Documentation Improvement tools can deliver the highest return on investment: they have the volume and complexity to generate significant revenue recovery, but lack the staffing resources to scale manual chart review.

For hospitals fitting this profile, the data suggests prioritizing three actions:
  • Invest in Clinical Documentation Improvement technology that scales across a broad case mix. Hospitals with high Diagnosis Related Group diversity have the largest gaps — manual review cannot cover the full range of conditions, but AI-powered tools can.
  • Focus coding resources on high-diversity Diagnosis Related Group families where severity capture is weakest. Not all documentation gaps are equal — targeting the Diagnosis Related Group families with the largest payment uplift potential maximizes return per coding hour.
  • Strengthen the Registered Nurse workforce. Nursing skill mix is one of the few modifiable factors significantly associated with better documentation. Hospitals with a higher share of registered nurses consistently show smaller gaps.

Limitations

Why the Model Explains Only 21% of Variance

As discussed in the Results, the regression R² of ~0.21 is a data limitation, not a model limitation. I validated the Ordinary Least Squares specification against Ridge, Lasso, and ElasticNet using 5-fold cross-validation, and none of the regularized alternatives improved fit (all cross-validated R² values fell within 0.19–0.21). Residual diagnostics confirmed no major violations of linearity, normality, or homoscedasticity. The model is well-specified for the data it has — the constraint is what publicly available CMS data can observe.

The remaining ~80% of variance is almost certainly driven by operational factors that are not available in any public dataset: Clinical Documentation Improvement program maturity, EHR system quality, coder expertise, physician engagement with documentation queries, and overall revenue cycle investment. Two structurally identical hospitals can have vastly different documentation quality depending on these unmeasured factors. This means the model's significant predictors should be interpreted as risk signals that flag which hospitals are most likely to benefit from documentation improvement — not as causal drivers that fully explain the gap.

Data and Methodology Caveats

  • Case Mix Index does not equal documentation quality. Case Mix Index differences between hospitals can reflect genuine patient population differences, not just documentation quality. The peer-group approach (42 cohorts by bed size, teaching status, and ownership) mitigates this but cannot fully eliminate it.
  • Severity shift rates are estimates. The 1–15% shift rates are based on published Clinical Documentation Improvement program benchmarks, not measured directly for each hospital. Actual capture rates vary widely by institution and specialty.
  • Medicare fee-for-service only. This analysis covers only Medicare fee-for-service claims. Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, and commercial payers are excluded, meaning the true national revenue impact is likely substantially larger than the $1.9 billion estimate.
  • Cross-sectional design. This is a snapshot, not a longitudinal analysis. Hospitals that recently implemented Clinical Documentation Improvement programs may show high gap scores that are already improving. Conversely, hospitals with declining programs may show low scores that are worsening.
  • Provider of Services staffing data quality. Staffing counts in the Provider of Services file are self-reported and may contain measurement error. Some staffing ratios showed extreme outliers that were capped at the 99th percentile but not excluded.
  • Composite score weighting. The 40/30/20/10 weighting of the four gap sub-scores (Case Mix Index gap, severity distribution, payment deviation, complexity) is based on domain knowledge, not empirically derived. Alternative weightings could shift individual hospital rankings, though the overall tier distributions are robust to moderate changes.
  • Tier classification thresholds. The current tier boundaries—Low (0–49), Medium (50–64), and High (65–100)—are asymmetric by design: the Low tier spans 50 points because 50 represents the peer-group average, and hospitals at or below average have limited actionable gap. However, this means Medium covers only 15 points while High spans 36. Sensitivity testing with alternative classification schemes could determine whether different boundaries would better segment hospitals for intervention. Candidate approaches include: equal thirds (0–33, 34–66, 67–100), which offer the simplest interpretation; anchoring at 50 with balanced upper tiers (0–49, 50–74, 75–100), which preserves the peer-average anchor while giving Medium and High equal width; or a quartile-based scheme (0–25, 26–50, 51–75, 76–100) that adds a fourth tier to separate hospitals well below average from those near the boundary. Future work could evaluate each scheme against chart-audit validation data or Clinical Documentation Improvement program outcomes to determine which thresholds most accurately identify hospitals that benefit from intervention.
  • Severity shift rate assumptions. The shift rates used in the Revenue Impact Model (1–4% for Low-gap, 4–7% for Medium-gap, 7–15% for High-gap hospitals) are analyst-defined assumptions based on domain judgment, not empirically calibrated from chart-audit data. While they are intentionally conservative relative to published Clinical Documentation Improvement benchmarks (15–25% improvement rates), the actual relationship between a hospital’s Documentation Gap Score and the proportion of cases that could be reclassified remains unvalidated. Calibrating these rates against real-world CDI program outcomes would strengthen the revenue estimates.
  • Geographic coverage. The choropleth includes all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories, but territory-based hospitals may have different payment structures and patient populations that limit direct comparability.

What Future Work Could Address

The most impactful extension of this analysis would be linking hospital-level documentation gap scores to actual Clinical Documentation Improvement program characteristics — program age, staffing levels, technology adoption, and query response rates. This would require proprietary or survey-based data, but could dramatically improve the model's explanatory power and enable more precise targeting of documentation improvement interventions.

Other valuable extensions include longitudinal tracking of gap scores over time to measure the impact of Clinical Documentation Improvement program implementation, expanding to Medicare Advantage and commercial payers to estimate the full revenue opportunity, and validating the composite gap score against chart-level audit data to confirm that high-scoring hospitals do in fact have higher rates of under-documented severity.

Case Study: AI-Powered Healthcare Product

This section applies the five-phase framework to a real product category: AI-powered Clinical Documentation Improvement (CDI) software. The examples use actual analytics from a Medicare documentation gap analysis of over 3,200 hospitals, demonstrating how each phase of the general framework translates into domain-specific practice.

Why AI Products Need an Extra Analytics Layer

AI products introduce a measurement challenge that traditional software does not have. A conventional feature either works or it does not. An AI model produces outputs that are right some percentage of the time, partially right some percentage, and wrong some percentage. That ambiguity means product analytics for AI products must track two layers simultaneously:

  • Product experience layer: Is the user adopting and engaging with the product? (Onboarding, workflow integration, session frequency)
  • Model performance layer: Is the AI itself producing accurate, useful outputs? (Precision, recall, calibration, acceptance rate)

Conflating these two layers is one of the most common mistakes in AI product analytics:

  • A model can be highly accurate but poorly surfaced in the workflow → low adoption despite good AI
  • Users may engage frequently but lose trust if suggestions are wrong too often → high usage but declining outcomes

Separating these layers allows the team to diagnose problems precisely and direct resources to the right fix.

The Product

Imagine a product that ingests hospital medical records, applies natural language processing to identify conditions and procedures that were clinically present but not fully documented, and surfaces suggestions to clinical coders and physicians. When documentation is improved, the hospital's Medicare severity tier (Diagnosis Related Group) more accurately reflects the true complexity of care, resulting in appropriate reimbursement.

Going Deeper: From Insight to Product Concept

The general CDI product described above — AI reads charts and identifies missing documentation — is a broad category. This section goes further by:

  • Showing how an insight from the original analysis leads to a more targeted product idea
  • Explaining the distinction between a new product and a new feature — and why it changes the analytics scope
  • Walking through the framework applied to the specific concept

The Insight That Shapes the Product

The regression model in the documentation gap analysis flagged Diagnosis Related Group diversity as the strongest positive predictor of documentation gaps (+5.01 per standard deviation). But DRG diversity reflects what services a hospital offers — its menu of specialties. A hospital cannot change its DRG diversity, and neither can a product. This is a targeting signal for sales, not a lever the product can pull.

The product needs to act on something every hospital controls regardless of its size, specialty mix, or geography: the quality of its documentation at the point of claim submission.

  • A 25-bed rural hospital and a 600-bed academic medical center both submit claims to Medicare
  • Both can have gaps in their documentation
  • Both lose revenue when claims are rejected or undercoded

The documentation process itself — not the hospital's characteristics — is where the product creates value.

The Product Concept: Pre-Submission Claim Benchmarking

How It Works

Before a hospital submits a claim, the product compares it against claims from peer hospitals — hospitals of similar size, teaching status, and patient mix — to determine whether the claim appears to be missing information. The comparison draws from two sources:

  1. Successful peer claims: Claims from peer hospitals that were approved and reimbursed at the expected severity tier. If a peer hospital with a similar patient consistently documents a Complication or Comorbidity that your hospital's claim is missing, that is a signal.
  2. Rejected peer claims: Claims from peer hospitals that were denied or modified by the payer. If a pattern of missing documentation led to rejections at peer hospitals, the product warns the coder before submission.

The output is a claim readiness score and a set of specific, actionable recommendations — each one tied to a concrete peer comparison — that the coder can accept or reject before submitting the claim. The recommendations are designed to be easy to interpret: not "consider adding secondary diagnoses" but "peer hospitals with this primary DRG documented [specific condition] in 78% of similar cases — your claim does not include it."

Key Distinction
This concept differs from a general chart-review AI in an important way:
  • General CDI AI asks: "What diagnoses might be present in this medical record?"
  • Peer benchmarking asks: "Compared to claims that worked at hospitals like yours, what is this claim missing?"
The intelligence comes from the peer benchmark, not just from reading the chart in isolation.

Applying the Framework: Claim Benchmarking as a Feature

If the pre-submission claim benchmarking tool is positioned as a feature within an existing CDI platform, the analytics framework focuses on Phases 2 through 5 (Validation through Scale), since the market already exists.

2 Validation — Define the Feature's Success Metrics

North Star contribution: Does the feature increase the parent product's North Star (additional net revenue captured per hospital)? Specifically, does the claim readiness score reduce the rate of claim rejections and increase the rate of appropriate severity tier coding?

Feature-specific metrics:

  • Claim readiness score accuracy — do claims with higher scores get approved at higher rates?
  • Recommendation acceptance rate — what percentage of peer-comparison recommendations do coders act on?
  • Rejection rate reduction — comparing pre-feature vs. post-feature denial rates for the same hospital
  • Revenue uplift attributable to accepted recommendations — dollar value of severity tier corrections

Baseline: Each hospital's historical rejection rate and severity tier distribution for the 12 months before the feature is activated.

Because this is a feature within an existing product, rollout uses a feature flag rather than a new contract. The analyst designs the test:

  • Treatment group: 50% of hospitals see the claim readiness score and recommendations before submission
  • Control group: 50% of hospitals continue using the existing workflow without peer benchmarking
  • Primary metric: Claim rejection rate (treatment vs. control)
  • Secondary metrics: Revenue per hospital, coder time-per-claim, recommendation acceptance rate
  • Guardrails: Coder satisfaction score does not drop; time-per-claim does not increase more than 10%; no increase in audit flags
  • Duration: 90 days minimum — long enough to capture claim adjudication cycles (payers may take 30-60 days to respond)
5 Scale — Measure Feature Impact on Retention and Expansion

At scale, the feature's value is measured by its contribution to the parent product's business metrics:

  • Retention impact: Do hospitals using the benchmarking feature renew at higher rates than those who do not?
  • Expansion signal: Does the feature justify upgrading customers to a higher pricing tier?
  • Peer network effects: As more hospitals use the product, the peer benchmark dataset grows, improving recommendation quality for everyone — track whether recommendation accuracy improves as the customer base scales
  • Segment performance: Which hospital segments get the most value? Use this to refine the targeting model for customer success outreach
Why This Product Concept Works Across All Hospitals
Unlike the regression model's finding that DRG diversity predicts documentation gaps (a hospital characteristic that cannot be changed), the peer benchmarking approach acts on the documentation process itself. A small rural hospital with 50 DRG families benefits just as much as a large academic center with 200 — because the tool compares each hospital to its own peers, not to the national average. The peer groups from the original analysis (42 cohorts by bed size, teaching status, and ownership) become the matching criteria for the benchmarking engine. Every hospital has peers, every hospital submits claims, and every hospital can improve its documentation relative to what worked at similar institutions.
