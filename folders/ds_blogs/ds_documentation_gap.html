Executive Summary

Every year, U.S. hospitals lose billions of dollars not because they deliver less care, but because their clinical documentation doesn't fully capture the severity of the patients they treat. Under the Medicare Severity Diagnosis Related Group system, incomplete documentation means cases get classified into lower-paying tiers even when the clinical reality warranted more. This analysis uses publicly available CMS provider utilization and payment data to quantify the gap across approximately 3,000 hospitals, estimate the revenue at stake, and identify what hospital characteristics predict larger documentation gaps.

Key Findings

  • The documentation gap is real and measurable. Using peer-group benchmarking across 49 hospital cohorts (by bed size, teaching status, and ownership), 8% of hospitals score in the "High" documentation gap tier (score ≥ 65), with another 39% in the "Medium" tier. These hospitals have Case Mix Index values, severity mixes, and payment patterns that fall significantly below structurally similar peers.
  • Revenue impact is concentrated but widespread. The top 20 hospitals by estimated opportunity account for hundreds of millions, but the median hospital still has a ~$272K annual opportunity. The shift model estimates that even modest improvements in documentation could recover $5,791 per shifted case (blended Complication or Comorbidity / Major Complication or Comorbidity uplift), consistent with published CC/MCC capture rate benchmarks.
  • Diagnosis Related Group Diversity is the strongest predictor. Hospitals treating a broader mix of conditions have substantially higher gap scores (+5.01 per standard deviation). A wider case mix means more documentation complexity and more opportunities for severity codes to be missed. Wage index (−2.77) and CMS quality scores (−1.94) are the next strongest drivers, suggesting that hospitals in lower-wage markets with fewer resources and weaker quality performance face compounding documentation challenges.
  • H1 (Ownership): Partially supported. For-profit hospitals have slightly lower gap scores than government hospitals (47.5 vs 48.2), but the difference is not statistically significant (p = 0.233). Ownership alone is a weak predictor of documentation quality, suggesting that organizational structure matters less than operational factors like staffing and case mix.
  • H2 (Staffing): Mixed support. Higher Registered Nurse skill mix was significantly associated with lower gap scores (−0.72, p < 0.001), supporting the idea that a more skilled clinical workforce contributes to better documentation. However, the resident-to-bed ratio showed the opposite pattern, likely reflecting the confounding effect of case complexity in teaching hospitals. Staffing composition matters more than staffing volume.
  • The model explains ~21% of variance — and that is informative. The regression captures what publicly available structural data can predict. The remaining ~80% is driven by factors not in public datasets: Clinical Documentation Improvement program maturity, EHR system quality, coder expertise, and physician engagement. The significant predictors identified here serve as observable risk signals that can flag which hospitals are most likely to benefit from documentation improvement — even without seeing inside their operations.
Implication for hospital leaders and Clinical Documentation Improvement vendors:

The hospitals most likely to have large documentation gaps are identifiable from public data: they treat a wide variety of conditions, operate in lower-wage markets, and have a lower share of registered nurses. The data suggests three priorities:
  • Invest in AI-powered Clinical Documentation Improvement technology that scales across a broad case mix.
  • Focus coding resources on high-diversity Diagnosis Related Group families where severity capture is weakest.
  • Strengthen the Registered Nurse workforce — one of the few modifiable factors significantly associated with better documentation (AHIMA Clinical Documentation Improvement Toolkit, 2024).

Background: How Medicare Payment and Severity Tiers Work

Medicare pays hospitals for inpatient stays through the Inpatient Prospective Payment System. Each admission is assigned a Medicare Severity Diagnosis Related Group, which determines the base payment amount. Many clinical conditions have three severity tiers that map to different Diagnosis Related Groups within the same family. For example, a heart failure admission might be classified as Diagnosis Related Group 291 (Heart Failure with Major Complication or Comorbidity), Diagnosis Related Group 292 (with Complication or Comorbidity), or Diagnosis Related Group 293 (without Complication or Comorbidity / Major Complication or Comorbidity). The payment difference between these tiers is substantial.

Complication or Comorbidity vs. Major Complication or Comorbidity: A Complication or Comorbidity is a secondary diagnosis that increases the complexity or cost of care. A Major Complication or Comorbidity represents an even higher level of severity. Shifting a single case from "without Complication or Comorbidity / Major Complication or Comorbidity" to "with Complication or Comorbidity" typically adds around $3,257 in Medicare reimbursement. A shift to "with Major Complication or Comorbidity" adds approximately $11,706 (based on FY 2026 IPPS payment rates).

Case Mix Index

The Case Mix Index is the average Diagnosis Related Group weight across all of a hospital's discharges. A higher Case Mix Index means the hospital's patient population is, on average, more complex and resource-intensive. The national average Case Mix Index in the current Inpatient Prospective Payment System data is approximately 1.76. Hospitals with similar patient populations should have similar Case Mix Index values. When a hospital's Case Mix Index falls significantly below its peers, one likely explanation is that its clinical documentation is not fully capturing patient severity (Tieder et al., 2014).

The Clinical Documentation Gap

The gap between care delivered and care documented sits at the heart of hospital reimbursement. A patient might receive complex, resource-intensive care for multiple chronic conditions, but if the physician's notes don't reflect that complexity with the right specificity, the hospital gets paid as if the case were routine. Clinical Documentation Improvement programs attempt to close this gap by having trained specialists review charts and query physicians for additional specificity. AI-powered Clinical Documentation Improvement tools extend this approach by analyzing clinical notes in real time at scale (AHIMA Journal, "Revenue Impact of Clinical Documentation Improvement").

Two Hypotheses

This analysis tests two hypotheses about what drives documentation quality:

  • H1 (Ownership): For-profit hospitals have smaller documentation gaps than government hospitals, as they may be better resourced for revenue cycle operations (cf. Tieder et al., 2014 on hospital variables and case mix).
  • H2 (Staffing): Hospitals with greater provider-to-patient ratios have better documentation and smaller gaps than lower-resourced hospitals (cf. Pillai et al., 2014 on staffing and documentation quality).

This analysis draws on five publicly available CMS datasets, all linked by CMS Certification Number. I collected hospital characteristics, Diagnosis Related Group and Case Mix Index data, inpatient charges and payments, staffing levels, and quality performance measures. After merging and cleaning, the final analytic dataset covers approximately 3,280 acute care hospitals with over 50 features.

From the merged data, I engineered features across several categories: peer-group benchmarks, gap sub-scores, revenue impact estimates, staffing ratios, and quality composites. Hospitals were grouped into peer cohorts and all gap metrics were computed relative to peer means. The pipeline culminates in a composite gap score, a revenue impact model, and a suite of regression models to identify the structural and staffing characteristics most associated with documentation risk.

Peer Group Benchmarking

To compare hospitals fairly, I grouped them into peer cohorts based on the intersection of bed size tier (8 categories from 1–24 to 600+), teaching status, and ownership type, producing 49 distinct peer groups (42 after filtering groups with fewer than 5 hospitals). All gap metrics are computed relative to peer group means to ensure apples-to-apples comparisons.

Documentation Gap Score

I constructed a composite Documentation Gap Score (0–100) that combines four normalized sub-scores:

  • Case Mix Index Gap Score (40% weight) — how far a hospital's Case Mix Index falls below its peer group mean
  • Severity Score (30% weight) — the proportion of cases coded at the lowest severity tier compared to peers
  • Payment Score (20% weight) — whether per-case payments are lower than expected given the hospital's peer group
  • Complexity Score (10% weight) — the diversity of Diagnosis Related Group families the hospital handles, reflecting documentation complexity

Hospitals scoring 65 or above are classified as "High" gap, 50–65 as "Medium," and below 50 as "Low."

While no single standardized documentation gap metric exists in the literature, this composite approach builds directly on established Clinical Documentation Improvement methods:

  • The American Health Information Management Association recommends using Complication/Major Complication capture rates and peer-benchmarked Case Mix Index comparisons as key performance indicators for identifying documentation opportunities (AHIMA, "Using CC/MCC Capture Rates as a Key Performance Indicator").
  • Pillai et al. demonstrated that a normalized Case Mix Index can isolate documentation quality from patient acuity when comparing across Diagnosis Related Group families (Pillai et al., 2014, Perspectives in Health Information Management).
  • Each sub-score component is individually well-established in Clinical Documentation Improvement practice (AHIMA Clinical Documentation Improvement Toolkit).
Documentation Gap Score = Case Mix Index Gap Score × 0.40 + Severity Score × 0.30 + Payment Score × 0.20 + Complexity Score × 0.10

Revenue Impact Model

The revenue model estimates how much additional Medicare reimbursement a hospital could capture if its documentation fully reflected the true severity of its patients. The core idea is simple: when a patient’s chart does not capture all relevant complications and comorbidities, Medicare assigns the case to a lower-paying Diagnosis Related Group. Better documentation “shifts” some of those cases into a higher severity tier—and a higher payment—without changing the care delivered.

Key Terms in Plain Language

  • Severity shift — when improved documentation moves a case from the baseline “Without CC/MCC” tier (lowest payment) up to either the Complication/Comorbidity tier or the Major Complication/Comorbidity tier (higher payment). No clinical care changes; only the documentation accuracy changes.
  • Complication/Comorbidity (CC) uplift ($3,257) — the average additional payment Medicare makes when a case moves from the baseline tier to the middle “With CC” tier. Computed as the mean difference across all Diagnosis Related Group families in the CMS Inpatient PUF.
  • Major Complication/Comorbidity (MCC) uplift ($11,706) — the average additional payment when a case moves from baseline to the highest “With MCC” tier. This is much larger because MCC cases reflect the most resource-intensive patients.
  • Blended uplift ($5,792) — a weighted average assuming 70% of shifted cases land in the CC tier and 30% reach the MCC tier: ($3,257 × 0.70) + ($11,706 × 0.30) = $5,792.
  • Shift rate — the estimated percentage of a hospital’s Medicare discharges that could be reclassified through better documentation. This rate scales with the hospital’s Documentation Gap Score: hospitals with larger gaps have more room for improvement.

Shift Rate Schedule

The shift rate is not a single number—it increases linearly within each gap tier, reflecting the idea that hospitals with bigger documentation gaps have proportionally more cases that could be reclassified.

Gap Score Range Tier Shift Rate Range Formula
0–40 Low 1%–3% 0.01 + (score / 40) × 0.02
40–60 Medium 3%–6% 0.03 + ((score − 40) / 20) × 0.03
60–80 High 6%–10% 0.06 + ((score − 60) / 20) × 0.04
80–100 Critical 10%–15% 0.10 + ((score − 80) / 20) × 0.05

These rates are conservative relative to industry benchmarks. Published Clinical Documentation Improvement programs typically improve 15–25% of targeted cases, but this model accounts for hospitals that may not have a full program in place.

Statistical Models

I tested the two hypotheses using Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U (one-sided for H1), ANOVA, and Spearman/Pearson correlations. The primary model presented in the Results is an enriched Ordinary Least Squares regression with 17 predictors (up from a base model with 10 structural features), adding staffing ratios from the Provider of Services file and quality metrics from Hospital General Information. All regression coefficients are standardized to one standard deviation units for comparability. To validate model selection, I compared Ordinary Least Squares against Ridge regression (L2 regularization), Lasso regression (L1 regularization with feature selection), and ElasticNet using 5-fold cross-validation. The regularized models offered no meaningful improvement over Ordinary Least Squares (all cross-validated R² values fell within 0.19–0.21), confirming the 17-feature specification is parsimonious enough without requiring shrinkage or feature elimination. I also fit a logistic regression to predict high-gap status (score ≥ 65), which achieved an Area Under the Curve of 0.75. Residual diagnostics (residuals vs. fitted, Q-Q plot, histogram, and scale-location plot) confirmed no major violations of linearity, normality, or homoscedasticity.

Results

Hospital Ownership Shapes Patient Complexity

The hospitals in this analysis span the full spectrum of U.S. acute care: small critical access hospitals with fewer than 25 beds to large urban academic medical centers with 600+. The median hospital has around 185 beds, while the mean sits at 268. About one-third are teaching hospitals. How a hospital is structured affects both its patient mix and its documentation practices. Government and nonprofit hospitals tend to have higher Case Mix Index values, reflecting their role as safety-net providers treating more complex cases.

Figure 1: Box plot of Case Mix Index across four hospital ownership categories for 3,280 U.S. acute care hospitals: Nonprofit (n = 1,944), For-Profit (n = 651), Government (n = 451), and Other (n = 234, including Veterans Health Administration, Department of Defense, and Tribal facilities). The Case Mix Index is the average Diagnosis Related Group weight across all of a hospital's Medicare discharges — a higher value means the hospital's patient population is, on average, more clinically complex and resource-intensive. The national mean Case Mix Index in this dataset is approximately 1.76. A one-way ANOVA confirms that Case Mix Index differs significantly across all four ownership categories (F(3, 3276) = 108.6, p < 0.001), and Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc pairwise t-tests show that every pair of groups is significantly different from each other (all p < 0.001). The “Other” category (primarily federal facilities such as Veterans Health Administration hospitals) stands out with the highest mean Case Mix Index (2.57), reflecting specialized patient populations such as veterans with complex, service-connected conditions. For-Profit (mean 1.79) and Nonprofit (mean 1.76) hospitals cluster near the national average, while Government hospitals have the lowest mean (1.63). The effect size is medium (η² = 0.096), indicating that ownership type explains roughly 10% of the variation in Case Mix Index.

Severity Classification Drives Massive Payment Differences

The financial stakes of severity classification are significant. Across all Diagnosis Related Group families with complete severity tiers, the average Complication or Comorbidity uplift is approximately $3,257 per case, and the average Major Complication or Comorbidity uplift is approximately $11,706 per case. Nationally, roughly 69% of Medicare inpatient cases are classified at the Major Complication or Comorbidity tier. The remaining 31% represent a massive volume of discharges being paid at lower rates.

Figure 2: National distribution of Medicare inpatient cases across three severity tiers, aggregated across all Diagnosis Related Group families. In this context, a “complication” refers to a secondary diagnosis — such as diabetes, heart failure, or sepsis — that increases the complexity and cost of a patient’s hospital stay. Medicare classifies each case as “Without Complication or Comorbidity” (the lowest severity), “With Complication or Comorbidity” (moderate), or “With Major Complication or Comorbidity” (the highest severity). Approximately 64% of cases are already classified at the highest tier. The remaining 22% at the two lower tiers are not necessarily less sick patients — they may simply have less thorough documentation. When physicians do not record the full specificity of a patient’s conditions (for example, writing “diabetes” instead of “diabetes with chronic kidney disease stage 3”), the case gets coded at a lower severity tier even though the patient’s actual clinical complexity is higher. These cases represent the primary target for clinical documentation improvement programs.
Figure 3: Total Medicare payment at each severity tier for the top 10 Diagnosis Related Group families ranked by the largest payment increase when a major complication is documented. Each bar is broken into three segments: the grey portion represents the baseline payment when no complication or comorbidity is documented, the orange segment shows the additional payment when a complication is present, and the blue segment shows the further increase when a major complication is documented. For example, an intracranial vascular procedure pays roughly $17,700 at baseline, but documenting a major complication raises the total reimbursement to over $51,500 — nearly triple the baseline amount. These large payment differences highlight why thorough clinical documentation matters: a single undocumented complication can mean tens of thousands of dollars in unrealized revenue per case.

Nearly Half of All Hospitals Fall into Medium or High Gap Tiers

To measure how well each hospital documents the severity of its patients, we created a single score from 0 to 100 called the Documentation Gap Score. It combines four signals: how much a hospital's Case Mix Index falls below similar hospitals, how often cases are coded at the lowest severity tier compared to peers, whether per-case payments are lower than expected, and how many different types of cases the hospital handles. A higher score means the hospital's records are more likely to understate how sick its patients really are. We then grouped all 3,280 hospitals into three tiers based on their score: Low (below 50), Medium (50 to 65), and High (65 and above). The treemap below breaks down each tier by hospital ownership type and shows the total number of distinct Diagnosis Related Groups they handle — giving a sense of where documentation gaps are most concentrated and how many types of cases are affected.

Figure 4: Treemap of 3,280 U.S. hospitals organized by Documentation Gap tier (Low, Medium, High) and then by ownership type (Nonprofit, For-Profit, Government, Other). Nonprofit hospitals dominate every tier, but the Medium gap tier stands out: its 783 Nonprofit hospitals collectively handle nearly 58,000 Diagnosis Related Groups — more than any other segment — suggesting a large volume of cases where documentation improvement could change severity classifications and reimbursement.

Lower Complexity Hospitals Show the Largest Documentation Gaps

If documentation gaps were random, there would be no relationship between a hospital's Case Mix Index and its gap score. Instead, the data shows a consistent negative pattern across all four ownership types: hospitals with lower Case Mix Index values tend to have larger documentation gaps. This suggests that hospitals treating less complex patient populations may be systematically under-documenting the severity that is present, possibly because their Clinical Documentation Improvement programs are less mature or because lower-acuity settings attract less coding scrutiny.

All four ownership types show a consistent negative slope: hospitals with lower Case Mix Index scores tend to have larger documentation gaps, suggesting that facilities treating less complex patient populations may be under-documenting severity. The pattern holds regardless of ownership structure, reinforcing that documentation gaps are a systemic issue rather than one confined to a particular hospital type.

Documentation Gaps Represent $1.9 Billion in National Revenue Opportunity

Translating gap scores into dollar estimates, the model finds approximately $1.9 billion in aggregate national opportunity. The mean hospital opportunity is $579K annually, while the median is $272K, reflecting the right-skewed distribution where a small number of large hospitals account for outsized impact.

Case Mix Diversity and Wage Index Predict the Largest Gaps

To move from describing where gaps exist to understanding why, I tested two hypotheses and fit an enriched Ordinary Least Squares regression model with 17 predictors.

H1 (Ownership): Partially supported. For-profit hospitals have a slightly lower mean gap score (47.5) than government hospitals (48.2), but the difference is not statistically significant (Mann-Whitney U, one-sided p = 0.233). In the regression, nonprofit status was the only ownership category that reached significance (p = 0.017), associated with a modest increase in gap score relative to for-profit hospitals. Ownership alone is a weak predictor of documentation quality.

H2 (Staffing): Mixed support. Higher Registered Nurse skill mix (the share of nursing staff who are registered nurses) was significantly associated with lower gap scores (Spearman r, p < 0.001), supporting the idea that a more skilled clinical workforce contributes to better documentation. However, the resident-to-bed ratio showed a significant positive correlation, meaning hospitals with more residents had higher gap scores — likely reflecting the confounding effect of case complexity in teaching hospitals. Staffing composition matters more than staffing volume.

The regression model (R² = 0.21, Adj R² = 0.20) explains about one-fifth of the variance in gap scores. This is a modest fit — and intentionally so. The publicly available CMS data captures hospital structure, staffing, and quality performance, but it cannot observe the factors that most directly determine documentation quality: whether a hospital has a Clinical Documentation Improvement program, how mature its EHR system is, how skilled its coders are, or how engaged its physicians are in documentation. These unmeasured operational factors almost certainly explain the remaining 80% of the variance. What the model can tell us is which observable, structural characteristics reliably signal higher documentation risk — and those signals are strong enough to be actionable. The following predictors all reached statistical significance (p < 0.05):

1. Diagnosis Related Group Diversity (+5.01) — by far the strongest signal. Hospitals treating a broader mix of conditions have substantially higher gap scores. More condition types means more documentation complexity and more opportunities for severity codes to be missed. A one standard deviation increase in Diagnosis Related Group diversity is associated with a 5-point increase in gap score.

2. Wage Index (−2.77) — hospitals in lower-wage labor markets have larger gaps. This likely reflects that these facilities have fewer resources to invest in Clinical Documentation Improvement programs, coding staff, and EHR infrastructure.

3. Quality Score (−1.94) — hospitals with worse CMS quality performance (mortality, safety, readmission) also have larger documentation gaps, suggesting that documentation quality and clinical quality share common institutional drivers.

4. Resident/Bed Ratio (−1.88) — after controlling for teaching status, more residents per bed is associated with lower gap scores, possibly because residents generate more detailed clinical notes.

5. Teaching Status (+0.97) — teaching hospitals show higher gaps overall, likely because their complex case mix creates more documentation opportunities to miss.

6. Urban (+0.87) — urban hospitals have modestly higher gaps than rural ones.

7. Readmission % Worse (−0.83) — counterintuitively, hospitals with worse readmission rates show slightly lower gap scores, possibly because readmission scrutiny drives more thorough documentation of comorbidities.

8. Registered Nurse Skill Mix (−0.72) — a higher share of registered nurses is protective. Better-trained clinical staff appear to contribute to more complete documentation.

What this means for hospital leaders and Clinical Documentation Improvement vendors:

The hospitals most likely to have large documentation gaps are identifiable from public data alone — they treat a wide variety of conditions, operate in lower-wage markets, score below peers on CMS quality measures, and have a lower share of registered nurses. These are the facilities where AI-powered Clinical Documentation Improvement tools can deliver the highest return on investment: they have the volume and complexity to generate significant revenue recovery, but lack the staffing resources to scale manual chart review.

For hospitals fitting this profile, the data suggests prioritizing three actions:
  • Invest in Clinical Documentation Improvement technology that scales across a broad case mix. Hospitals with high Diagnosis Related Group diversity have the largest gaps — manual review cannot cover the full range of conditions, but AI-powered tools can.
  • Focus coding resources on high-diversity Diagnosis Related Group families where severity capture is weakest. Not all documentation gaps are equal — targeting the Diagnosis Related Group families with the largest payment uplift potential maximizes return per coding hour.
  • Strengthen the Registered Nurse workforce. Nursing skill mix is one of the few modifiable factors significantly associated with better documentation. Hospitals with a higher share of registered nurses consistently show smaller gaps.

Limitations

Why the Model Explains Only 21% of Variance

As discussed in the Results, the regression R² of ~0.21 is a data limitation, not a model limitation. I validated the Ordinary Least Squares specification against Ridge, Lasso, and ElasticNet using 5-fold cross-validation, and none of the regularized alternatives improved fit (all cross-validated R² values fell within 0.19–0.21). Residual diagnostics confirmed no major violations of linearity, normality, or homoscedasticity. The model is well-specified for the data it has — the constraint is what publicly available CMS data can observe.

The remaining ~80% of variance is almost certainly driven by operational factors that are not available in any public dataset: Clinical Documentation Improvement program maturity, EHR system quality, coder expertise, physician engagement with documentation queries, and overall revenue cycle investment. Two structurally identical hospitals can have vastly different documentation quality depending on these unmeasured factors. This means the model's significant predictors should be interpreted as risk signals that flag which hospitals are most likely to benefit from documentation improvement — not as causal drivers that fully explain the gap.

Data and Methodology Caveats

Key Caveats

  • Case Mix Index does not equal documentation quality. Case Mix Index differences between hospitals can reflect genuine patient population differences, not just documentation quality. The peer-group approach (49 cohorts by bed size, teaching status, and ownership) mitigates this but cannot fully eliminate it.
  • Severity shift rates are estimates. The 1–15% shift rates are based on published Clinical Documentation Improvement program benchmarks, not measured directly for each hospital. Actual capture rates vary widely by institution and specialty.
  • Medicare fee-for-service only. This analysis covers only Medicare fee-for-service claims. Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, and commercial payers are excluded, meaning the true national revenue impact is likely substantially larger than the $1.9 billion estimate.
  • Cross-sectional design. This is a snapshot, not a longitudinal analysis. Hospitals that recently implemented Clinical Documentation Improvement programs may show high gap scores that are already improving. Conversely, hospitals with declining programs may show low scores that are worsening.
  • Provider of Services staffing data quality. Staffing counts in the Provider of Services file are self-reported and may contain measurement error. Some staffing ratios showed extreme outliers that were capped at the 99th percentile but not excluded.
  • Composite score weighting. The 40/30/20/10 weighting of the four gap sub-scores (Case Mix Index gap, severity distribution, payment deviation, complexity) is based on domain knowledge, not empirically derived. Alternative weightings could shift individual hospital rankings, though the overall tier distributions are robust to moderate changes.
  • Geographic coverage. The choropleth includes all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories, but territory-based hospitals may have different payment structures and patient populations that limit direct comparability.

What Future Work Could Address

The most impactful extension of this analysis would be linking hospital-level documentation gap scores to actual Clinical Documentation Improvement program characteristics — program age, staffing levels, technology adoption, and query response rates. This would require proprietary or survey-based data, but could dramatically improve the model's explanatory power and enable more precise targeting of documentation improvement interventions.

Other valuable extensions include longitudinal tracking of gap scores over time to measure the impact of Clinical Documentation Improvement program implementation, expanding to Medicare Advantage and commercial payers to estimate the full revenue opportunity, and validating the composite gap score against chart-level audit data to confirm that high-scoring hospitals do in fact have higher rates of under-documented severity.
