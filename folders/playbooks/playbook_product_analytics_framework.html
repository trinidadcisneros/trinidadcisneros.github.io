Why Product Analytics Matters

Every product starts as a bet; a hypothesis that a certain problem exists, that customers will pay to solve it, and that the team can build something that delivers value better than the alternatives.

The job of the product analyst is to test that bet at every stage of the product lifecycle.

Before Launch: Discovery + Validation

Core question: Is there a problem worth solving, and how will the team know if the product succeeds?

The analyst defines what success looks like in measurable terms. This means:

  • Supporting the Product Manager with market sizing and opportunity validation using internal data (for existing platforms) or external evidence (for net new products)
  • Segmenting the market to identify which customers have the biggest need and the highest willingness to pay
  • Proposing a North Star metric anchored on the customer's outcome, not just product usage
  • Building the metrics tree: adoption funnel, product performance, revenue/outcome metrics, and guardrail metrics
  • Setting success thresholds and kill/pivot criteria with the Product Manager before development begins

Tabs: Discovery, Validation

During Build: Instrumentation + Preparation

Core question: Can the team measure every meaningful interaction once the product is live?

The analyst instruments the product so every meaningful interaction is trackable. This means:

  • Designing the event taxonomy: every user action mapped with properties and business context
  • Specifying dashboards for two audiences: an operational dashboard for the product team and an executive dashboard for leadership
  • Building a data quality validation plan covering completeness, accuracy, and latency
  • For AI products: ensuring every AI interaction is logged (suggestion generated, displayed, accepted, rejected, modified) with the model confidence score
  • Working with engineering to define events before development, not after, so instrumentation is built in from the start

Tab: Build

At Launch: Rollout

Core question: Is the product being adopted, delivering value, and ready to expand?

The analyst monitors adoption, measures impact against baselines, and identifies where the product is succeeding or falling short. This means:

  • Tracking the adoption funnel: Awareness → Contract → Activation → Engagement → Habit
  • Measuring the Aha! moment: the first time a user experiences the product's core value
  • Running A/B tests or pilot analyses with pre calculated sample sizes and clear success criteria
  • Monitoring guardrail metrics with automated alerts for threshold breaches
  • Applying the Expand / Iterate / Pause framework: recommending next steps based on quantitative criteria, not gut instinct
  • For AI products: separating model performance (precision, recall) from product outcome (acceptance rate, value delivered)

Tab: Rollout

After Launch: Scale

Core question: Is the product retaining users, growing sustainably, and still worth investing in?

The analyst builds the feedback loops that tell the team whether the product is delivering value and where to invest next. This means:

  • Running retention analysis at multiple levels: N-day curves, cohort based analysis (time, behavioral, and segment cohorts), and survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier)
  • Building a churn prediction model that gives the customer success team early warning before users leave
  • Refining the Ideal Customer Profile with actual performance data to guide targeting
  • Identifying expansion and upsell opportunities through net revenue retention and feature adoption analytics
  • Running the product-market fit assessment: comparing actual performance against the thresholds set in Validation, and making the data driven recommendation to continue, pivot, or kill

Phase 1: Discovery

Core question: Is there a problem worth solving, and how big is it?

Primary deliverable: Market sizing, opportunity analysis, segmentation

Data source: Product Manager led. For new features on an existing platform, the analyst validates market assumptions using internal product data (conversion gaps, search failure rates, segment behavior). For net new products with no existing users, the analyst sources external evidence: industry reports, public datasets, customer interviews, and competitor analysis.

Analyst role: Sources evidence and validates sizing. The Product Manager synthesizes the go or no go recommendation.

Phase 2: Validation

Core question: Does the product actually solve the problem?

Primary deliverable: North Star metric, metrics tree, baselines, success and kill thresholds, pilot results

Data source: Pilot or minimum viable product experiment data. A controlled test on a small user segment using a prototype or minimal version of the feature, not the fully built product.

Analyst role: Designs and runs the pilot, defines success thresholds, and measures baselines before the full product is built.

Phase 3: Build

Core question: How does the team instrument and measure the product?

Primary deliverable: Metrics tree, event taxonomy, dashboards

Data source: No new data collected. The analyst defines what to track and where to source it, informed by the pilot results from Validation. Instrumentation is built here for collection during Rollout.

Analyst role: Works with engineering to define events before development so instrumentation is built in from the start, not bolted on after.

Phase 4: Rollout

Core question: Is the product working in the real world?

Primary deliverable: A/B tests, cohort analysis, adoption tracking

Data source: Production user data. Real customer activity, model predictions, and funnel events flowing through the instrumentation built in Phase 3.

Analyst role: Monitors adoption, measures impact against baselines, and applies the Expand / Iterate / Pause framework.

Phase 5: Scale

Core question: Where should the team optimize and where should it expand?

Primary deliverable: Retention analysis, expansion targeting, model monitoring

Data source: Longitudinal production data. Accumulated customer behavior over weeks or months, enabling retention curves, churn models, and lifetime value calculations.

Analyst role: Builds feedback loops that tell the team whether the product is delivering value and where to invest next, including the kill/pivot assessment.

Key Principle
The best product analysts do not wait for a product to launch to start measuring. They embed analytics into every stage of the lifecycle, starting with Discovery. Each phase builds on the data and learnings from the one before it.

Phase 1: Discovery — Size the Opportunity

Before a product team commits engineering resources, they need to know whether the problem is worth solving and how large the addressable market is. The product analyst's role in this phase depends on context:

Select a context above to see how the analyst's role changes.

The company already has a product with active users. The team wants to add a new capability. The analyst has access to real internal data that can validate whether the opportunity is real.

In practice, the analyst opens the existing platform data and checks usage patterns, conversion gaps, and engagement signals. The goal is to confirm or challenge the Product Manager's market assumptions using evidence from actual user behavior, not just industry reports.

Usage Patterns

How users actually interact with the existing product. The analyst looks for recurring behaviors that reveal what people do most, what they skip, and where they spend their time. These patterns show whether demand for a new capability already exists in the data.

Example Metrics
  • Feature adoption rate: what percentage of users try a given feature within 30 days
  • Session frequency: how often users come back per week or month
  • Time on task: how long users spend completing a specific workflow
  • Navigation paths: which pages or features users visit most, and in what order
  • Search queries: what users type into the search bar reveals unmet needs the product does not yet address
Conversion Gaps

Points in the user journey where people start an action but do not finish it. Each gap represents lost value and potential demand. A large gap at a specific step suggests users want to complete the action but something is preventing them.

Example Metrics
  • Signup to activation rate: what percentage of new signups complete their first key action
  • Cart abandonment rate: what percentage of users add items but never check out
  • Trial to paid conversion: what percentage of free trial users become paying customers
  • Funnel step drop off: the percentage of users lost between each step in a multi step workflow
  • Feature discovery to usage: what percentage of users who see a feature actually use it
Engagement Signals

Indicators that users find the product valuable enough to keep using it. Strong engagement signals suggest the product is solving a real problem. Weak or declining signals suggest users are losing interest or finding alternatives.

Example Metrics
  • Daily active users / monthly active users ratio: a high ratio means users come back frequently, not just once a month
  • Retention rate: what percentage of users return after 7, 14, or 30 days
  • Depth of use: how many features or actions a user touches per session
  • Content creation or contribution rate: whether users invest effort into the product (uploading, posting, configuring)
  • Repeat action frequency: how often users perform the core action the product is built around

Data sources: Internal product analytics, user behavior logs, feature usage data, conversion funnel data, support tickets, customer segmentation from the existing user base.

The company is building something from scratch. There is no existing platform, no existing users, and no internal data to analyze. The analyst cannot validate assumptions against real usage because there is no usage yet.

Instead, Discovery relies entirely on external evidence. The analyst's role shifts from "check the internal data" to "find the best available external evidence." The quality of Discovery depends on how well the analyst can source and synthesize evidence the team does not already have.

Data sources: Industry reports (Gartner, Forrester), public datasets (government filings, census data), customer interviews and surveys, competitor analysis (app store reviews, pricing pages, job postings), and analogous product benchmarks from adjacent markets.

▶ 🔗 Why This Phase Matters: What Happens to Discovery Work Later

Nothing in Discovery is done for its own sake. Every deliverable feeds directly into a decision in a later phase. If Discovery is skipped or done poorly, the downstream phases are working blind — making assumptions without evidence.

Discovery → Validation

Strategic thesis
→
The thesis determines what the North Star metric should measure. "Replaces manual review" points to time saved; "captures missed revenue" points to incremental revenue. Without the thesis, the team picks a metric that sounds good but may not reflect the actual bet leadership is making.
Segment definitions
→
The analyst uses the segments identified in Discovery to define who the North Star metric applies to. A product that serves three segments may need different success thresholds for each one. Without segments, the team sets one generic target and misses that the product works for large customers but fails for small ones.
Market sizing numbers
→
The Total Addressable Market sets the ceiling for what "success" can look like. Kill/pivot thresholds in Validation are grounded in these numbers. For example: "If the addressable whitespace is $10 million, the product needs at least 15% penetration within 18 months to justify continued investment." Without market sizing, the team has no basis for deciding what counts as enough traction.
Conversion gap analysis
→
The baseline measurements in Validation are scoped by what Discovery found. If Discovery showed that 22% of sessions end without completing the core action, Validation uses that as the "before" benchmark. The North Star target is then set relative to closing that gap: "Reduce incomplete sessions from 22% to 12%."

Discovery → Build

Strategic thesis
→
The thesis tells the analyst which events matter most in the tracking plan. If the bet is time saved, the tracking plan must capture task start and task end timestamps. If the bet is revenue capture, it must capture value per action. The thesis shapes which events are critical vs. nice to have.
Segment definitions
→
The event tracking plan needs properties that allow filtering by segment. If Discovery identified that enterprise and mid-market customers behave differently, every tracked event needs a "customer tier" property so the analyst can break down adoption and outcomes by segment at launch. If this is missing from the tracking plan, the analyst cannot do segment-level analysis later.
Competitive landscape
→
The competitive mapping tells the analyst what to instrument. If the main competitor's weakness is slow time-to-value, the analyst ensures the tracking plan captures time-to-first-value from the moment the customer starts onboarding. The dashboard design is shaped by what the team needs to prove the product is better than alternatives.

Discovery → Rollout

Strategic thesis
→
The thesis defines what "impact" means in A/B tests and pre/post analysis. If the thesis is about time saved, the primary evaluation metric is time on task reduction. If it's about revenue, the primary metric is incremental value per customer. Without the thesis, the team runs experiments but doesn't know which outcome to optimize for.
Conversion gap analysis
→
The gaps Discovery found become the "before" picture that Rollout measures against. If Discovery showed 38% of users leave after viewing one item, Rollout tracks whether the new product reduces that drop-off. Without Discovery's baseline, the team cannot prove the product improved anything — they just know what the numbers are now, not whether they changed.
Segment prioritization
→
Discovery's ranking of segments by need, readiness, and revenue potential determines the rollout sequence. The team launches to the highest-priority segment first, because that is where the product is most likely to succeed. Early wins build internal confidence and external credibility before expanding to harder segments.

Discovery → Scale

Strategic thesis
→
At Scale, the team revisits whether the original thesis still holds as the product expands to new segments and markets. If the thesis was "captures missed revenue for large hospitals" but expansion targets small clinics, the analyst checks whether the same value proposition applies or if the thesis needs to evolve for the new audience.
Segment definitions and market sizing
→
The Ideal Customer Profile at Scale is Discovery's segmentation updated with real performance data. Discovery said "these three segments should get the most value." Scale checks whether that prediction was correct by comparing retention, outcome metrics, and lifetime value across segments, then adjusts targeting. If a segment Discovery ranked as high-priority turns out to churn quickly, Scale deprioritizes it — using the original segmentation as the baseline for comparison.
Competitive whitespace
→
When the Scale team asks "where do we grow next?", they revisit Discovery's competitive landscape to find adjacent markets. If Discovery mapped which needs are unserved, Scale uses that map plus actual product performance data to predict which new segments are most likely to succeed — expanding into whitespace Discovery identified but did not pursue in the initial launch.

▶ 1 Strategic Thesis Product Manager Led

The strategic thesis is leadership's reasoning on why this product will succeed: what value it creates and for whom. The analyst needs this because it determines what the primary success metric should be. A thesis about saving time produces different metrics than a thesis about capturing revenue or reducing risk.

Deliverables
  • Product Manager: One sentence articulating the reasoning and the expected customer outcome
  • Analyst: Documented thesis statement with the implied primary metric and a mapping of how the thesis connects to measurable outcomes in Validation, Build, and Rollout
▶ 2 Market Sizing and Total Addressable Opportunity Product Manager Led

The Product Manager estimates the total market size using industry reports, competitive benchmarks, and domain knowledge. The analyst's role depends on context: on an existing platform, the analyst validates assumptions against real usage data; for a net new product, the analyst sources the best available external evidence to stress test the estimate.

Deliverables
  • Product Manager: Total addressable market estimate and revenue opportunity model
  • Analyst (existing platform): Internal data validation, conversion gap analysis, and data source documentation
  • Analyst (net new product): External evidence synthesis, comparable product benchmarking, and assumption documentation with confidence levels
📚 How to validate a market size estimate
Validating Market Size: Step by Step

Click a step to expand the details.

1 Get the source ▶
Ask the Product Manager where the estimate came from (industry report, internal model, back of envelope). This determines how much validation work is needed and what kind of evidence to look for.
2 Build a bottom up check ▶
Count the actual organizations or individuals who fit the target profile. Multiply by realistic spend per customer. Compare this number to the top down estimate. For a net new product, use public data sources such as census data, industry filings, or comparable product user counts to estimate the target population.
3 Apply a realistic conversion filter ▶
Not everyone in the target market will adopt. On an existing platform, check what percentage of eligible users actually convert today and apply that rate. For a net new product with no conversion data, use industry benchmarks from comparable products (for example, typical trial to paid rates in the product category) or estimates from customer interviews about willingness to switch. The goal is a realistic addressable number, not a theoretical ceiling.
4 Subtract competitive coverage ▶
Identify what percentage of the market is already well served by existing solutions. The remaining whitespace is the realistic opportunity. For markets with no direct competitor, look for substitute behaviors (spreadsheets, manual processes, workarounds) that signal unmet demand.
Tip: A good validation shrinks the market estimate. If the number stays the same after stress testing, either the analysis is not digging deep enough or the assumptions need more scrutiny. Document your confidence level for each assumption: high (based on real data), medium (based on credible external benchmarks), or low (based on analogies or rough estimates).
▶ 3 Customer Segmentation Analyst Led

Not all customers are equal. The analyst segments the addressable market by characteristics that predict need, willingness to pay, and likelihood of adoption. For AI products, segmentation often follows the size of the problem the product solves for each customer.

Deliverables
  • Segment definitions with clear criteria
  • Segment size distribution
  • Prioritization matrix (need times readiness times revenue potential)
📚 How to build customer segments
Building Actionable Segments: Step by Step

Click a step to expand the details.

1 Choose segmentation axes ▶
Start with the dimensions that most predict product value: company size, industry, workflow complexity, or current pain severity. Avoid demographics that do not predict behavior.
2 Create mutually exclusive groups ▶
Every potential customer should fit into exactly one segment. If segments overlap, the criteria are too broad.
3 Size each segment ▶
Count customers per segment. If a segment is too small to sustain a product, merge it. If one segment contains 80% of customers, split it further.
4 Score and rank ▶
Rate each segment on three dimensions: how much they need the product, how ready they are to adopt, and how much revenue they represent. Multiply to create a prioritization score.
Tip: The best first segment to target is not always the largest. It is the one where the product solves the sharpest pain with the least customization.

▶ 🔍 Where Does Discovery Data Come From?

The data sources available depend on whether the team is adding a new feature to an existing platform or building a net new product with no existing users.

New Feature on an Existing Platform

The company already has a product with active users. The analyst has access to real internal data that can validate whether the opportunity is real. The analyst looks at what existing users are already doing (or failing to do) inside the current product, and uses those patterns to confirm whether the proposed feature solves a real problem.

Show Example: Adding a New AI Feature to an Existing Platform

The Product Manager estimates the market opportunity at $46 billion based on industry reports. The analyst then opens the existing platform data and checks:

  • 22% of user sessions end without the user completing the core action
  • Average engagement depth is low (users interact with only 1.3 items per session)
  • 38% of users who view one item leave entirely instead of exploring further

This internal data tells the team the problem is real. Without this step, the team would be relying entirely on industry averages that may not apply.

Net New Product with No Existing Users

Discovery relies entirely on external sources: industry reports and market research, public datasets, customer interviews and surveys, competitor analysis, and analogous product benchmarks from similar products in adjacent markets.

Show Example: Building a Net New Product from Scratch

The analyst has no platform data. Instead, the analyst pulls published case studies, analyzes publicly available reviews of competing products, and interviews potential customers to understand how they currently solve the problem.

The analyst's role shifts from "check the internal data" to "find the best available external evidence."

The existing platform scenario produces stronger evidence because it is grounded in real user behavior. The net new product scenario relies on proxies and estimates, which means the team carries more risk into Validation.

▶ 🔬 Analysis Methods

Click a method to learn what it is, when to use it, and what to watch out for.

▶
Top Down Market SizingStart with a big number and narrow it down
Most common
Formula
Total Market × Segment % × Realistic Capture % = Addressable Opportunity
What it is
Start with a large industry number (like total healthcare spending) and apply filters to narrow it to the realistic market. For example: total market is $50 billion, the relevant segment is 20% of that ($10 billion), and the team can realistically capture 5% ($500 million).
When to use it
When industry reports exist and the team needs a quick estimate to decide whether the opportunity is worth investigating further. Good for early stage "is this market big enough?" questions.
Watch out for
Top down estimates almost always overstate the real opportunity. Always pair with a bottom up check.
▶
Bottom Up Market SizingCount actual potential customers and multiply
Most reliable
Formula
Number of Target Customers × Revenue per Customer = Market Size
What it is
Count the actual number of people or companies who fit the target customer profile. Multiply by realistic revenue per customer. This gives a grounded estimate built from real units, not percentages of a giant number.
When to use it
Always. Even when a top down estimate exists, the bottom up check tells the team whether the big number is real. If top down says $500 million but bottom up says $50 million, the top down number is wrong.
Watch out for
The biggest risk is miscounting the target population. Be specific about who qualifies as a target customer and document the criteria.
▶
Competitive Landscape AnalysisMap who already solves this problem and where the gaps are
What it is
Systematically identify existing solutions in the market, what they do well, what they miss, and what portion of the market they serve. The goal is to find whitespace: the part of the market that is underserved or unserved.
When to use it
Alongside market sizing. A large market with strong incumbents is a very different opportunity than a large market with weak or no solutions. The analysis shapes whether the product should compete head on or target an underserved niche.
Watch out for
Do not just look at direct competitors. Look for substitute behaviors: spreadsheets, manual processes, workarounds. These signal unmet demand that does not show up in competitor lists.
▶
Customer Interview AnalysisTurn qualitative conversations into structured evidence
What it is
Systematically analyze interview or survey responses to identify patterns. Code responses into themes, count how often each theme appears, and look for segments where the pain point is strongest. This turns "customers told us they are frustrated" into "14 of 20 enterprise customers mentioned workflow X as their top pain point."
When to use it
Especially critical for net new products where no internal data exists. Also useful on existing platforms to understand problems that usage data alone cannot explain (the "why" behind the "what").
Watch out for
Small sample sizes and selection bias. Ten interviews with enthusiastic early adopters do not represent the whole market. Document sample size and how interviewees were selected.
▶
Conversion Gap AnalysisFind where eligible users drop off in the current product
Existing platform only
Formula
(Eligible Users − Users Who Completed the Action) ÷ Eligible Users × 100 = Gap %
What it is
  • Compare the number of users who are eligible for an existing action against how many actually complete it today, before the new product exists
  • "Completed" means users who finished the current action on the existing platform, not users who adopted something new
  • The gap between eligible and completed is the opportunity the new product could address

Example

  • 10,000 users are eligible for the premium tier, but only 800 have upgraded — that is a 92% conversion gap

How to read the gap

  • High gap (most people drop off): something is broken or missing in the current experience, and that is exactly where a new product can step in and capture the lost demand
  • Low gap (most people finish): the current product already works well enough, so there is less reason to build something new for that workflow
When to use it
Only when building a new feature on an existing platform with real user data. This method does not apply to net new products with no users.
Watch out for
A large gap does not automatically mean a large opportunity. Users might be eligible but uninterested. Pair with qualitative data (interviews, support tickets) to confirm the gap represents real unmet demand, not just indifference.
▶ 🛠️ Technology Considerations

Discovery has the lightest technology requirements of any phase. The minimum viable stack is:

  • Data Warehouse (or even just SQL access and spreadsheets) for querying internal data if it exists
  • Business Intelligence tool for visualizing market sizing and presenting findings to stakeholders

Event tracking, experimentation platforms, and model monitoring are not needed yet. If the company has no data warehouse, Discovery can be conducted with spreadsheets, public datasets, and customer interviews.

The key question for the analyst: "Do I have access to internal product data, and if so, what query tools are available?" If yes, use them to validate external estimates. If no, budget extra time for external research.

▶ ⚖️ Trade-offs

Click a trade-off to see the tension and how the analyst helps resolve it.

▶
Go big or go focusedShould the team target a large market or a narrow segment?
The tension
A bigger market sounds better, but it is harder to serve everyone well. A narrow segment is easier to win, but there may not be enough customers to build a business.
How the analyst helps
Break the Total Addressable Market into segments and size each one. Show the revenue opportunity at different focus levels so the team can see the real numbers, not just a gut feeling about "big" versus "small."
▶
Move fast or research firstShould the team launch quickly or invest more time understanding the market?
The tension
Spending months on research means a competitor could launch first. Skipping research risks building something nobody wants.
How the analyst helps
Define "minimum viable research": the smallest number of data points needed to make a confident go or no go decision. This gives the team enough evidence to move forward without getting stuck in analysis.
▶ 🚨 Warning Signs and Kill Signals
Product-Market Fit Signal — Discovery
If Discovery reveals that the total addressable market is small, the competitive landscape is saturated, and no underserved segment exists, this is an early product-market fit red flag. The analyst should quantify the risk: "The addressable market is $X, existing solutions cover Y% of it, and the remaining whitespace supports Z customers." If Z is too small to sustain a business, the product concept may need to change before investing further.

What to watch for:

  • Total addressable market that is smaller than initially estimated after internal data validation
  • No identifiable underserved segment — every part of the market has existing solutions
  • Internal data shows the problem the product aims to solve does not exist (users are not struggling with the workflow the product targets)
  • Competitive landscape shows recent entrants have failed, suggesting the market may not support a new product

Phase 2: Validation — Define and Measure Success

Once the opportunity is sized, the team needs to define what a successful product looks like in measurable terms. This is where the North Star metric and supporting metrics tree are established, baselines are measured, success and kill thresholds are defined, and a validation pilot is designed and conducted — all before emotions and sunk costs enter the picture.

▶ 🔗 Why This Phase Matters: What Happens to Validation Work Later

Validation is where the team defines what "winning" looks like before the product exists. Every number set here becomes the benchmark that later phases measure against. If Validation is skipped, the team launches with no shared definition of success and no agreed criteria for when to stop.

Validation → Build

North Star metric
→
The North Star tells the analyst what the dashboards need to measure on day one. If the North Star is "revenue captured per customer per year," the event taxonomy and dashboards must track every step from suggestion to accepted action to revenue impact. Without a defined North Star, the Build team instruments everything generically and misses the specific events that matter.
Metrics tree
→
Every branch of the metrics tree maps to a specific event or calculation the tracking plan must support. If the tree includes "user acceptance rate" as a driver, the event taxonomy needs events for suggestion shown, suggestion accepted, and suggestion rejected. The metrics tree is essentially the spec for what Build needs to instrument.
Pilot results
→
The pilot results confirm which segments responded, which metrics moved, and whether the product hypothesis holds. Build uses these results to prioritize what to instrument first and which features to scale, rather than building blindly across the full product.

Validation → Rollout

Baseline measurements
→
Every impact claim in Rollout needs a "before" number. If Validation measured that customers currently complete a task in 45 minutes, and after launch the product brings it to 30 minutes, that is a 33% improvement. Without the baseline, the team knows the current number but cannot prove the product caused the change.
Success thresholds
→
The thresholds set in Validation become the criteria for the expand/iterate/pause decision in Rollout. "Activation rate above 60% within 30 days" is not a number the team invents during Rollout — it was agreed upon in Validation, before emotions and sunk costs entered the picture. This keeps the decision objective.
Pilot design
→
The pilot results are what Rollout evaluates to decide whether to expand, iterate, or pause. The pilot design defined which segments to test, the duration, and the success criteria — so by the time Rollout begins, the team already knows exactly what data to look at and what the benchmarks are.

Validation → Scale

Kill/pivot thresholds
→
The thresholds defined in Validation are the tripwires that trigger the hardest conversation in Scale: should this product continue? When the analyst presents the product-market fit scorecard at Scale, those thresholds are the benchmark. "We agreed that if Week-4 retention stayed below 10% across all segments, we would trigger a formal review. Here is the data." The power of this comes from having set the criteria before the team was emotionally invested.
North Star metric
→
The same North Star defined in Validation is what Scale measures to assess product market fit. If the North Star has not moved meaningfully after multiple cohorts, that is a signal. The consistency of measuring the same metric from Validation through Scale is what makes the longitudinal comparison meaningful.

▶ 1 Define the North Star Metric Collaborative

The North Star is the single metric that best captures the value the product delivers to customers. It should reflect customer outcomes, not just product usage.

Criteria for a good North Star
  • It reflects customer value (not vanity metrics like page views)
  • It is measurable with available data
  • It leads revenue (not lags it)
  • It is moveable by the product team
📚 How to identify the right North Star
Finding the North Star: A Decision Process

Click a step to expand the details.

The North Star is not something the team invents. It is discovered by asking: "What is the one thing that, if it improves, means the product is definitely working for customers?"

1 Start with the customer outcome ▶
What changes in the customer's world when the product works? Write this as a sentence: "Customers [verb] more/faster/better." Examples: "Customers process claims faster," "Customers find relevant products," "Customers close deals sooner."
2 Quantify the outcome ▶
Turn the sentence into a number. "Customers process claims faster" becomes "claims processed per day" or "average time from submission to resolution." Pick the version that is closest to the value the customer actually cares about.
3 Test the four criteria ▶
Does it reflect value (not just usage)? Can the team actually measure it? Does it move before revenue does? Can the product team influence it through feature changes?
4 Check the "so what" test ▶
If this metric doubles, would leadership care? If this metric drops to zero, would the product be considered a failure? If the answer to both is yes, it is likely the right North Star.
5 Structure for downstream use ▶
Write the North Star as: "[Metric name]: [unit] per [entity] per [time period]." Example: "Revenue Impact: dollars recovered per customer per year." This format makes it immediately usable in the metrics tree, baseline calculations, and target setting.
Common mistake: Choosing "monthly active users" or "sessions" as the North Star. These measure product activity, not customer value. A customer who logs in every day but gets no value will eventually churn. The North Star should capture what keeps them coming back.

📚 How to build a metrics tree
Building a Metrics Tree: The Universal Method

Click a step to expand the details.

Every metrics tree follows the same logic regardless of the product or domain: break the North Star into a math equation, then break each variable into its drivers.

1 Write the North Star as a formula ▶
Express it as a multiplication or addition of components. Example: Revenue per Customer = Volume times Rate times Value per Unit. This is the top of the tree.
2 Break each component into its drivers ▶
For each variable in the formula, ask "what makes this number go up or down?" Those answers become the next level of branches.
3 Stop when you reach something actionable ▶
Each leaf of the tree should be something the team can directly measure and influence. If a branch is still abstract ("user satisfaction"), break it further into specific measurable behaviors.
4 Label each metric as leading or lagging ▶
Leading indicators (daily active usage, activation rate) move first. Lagging indicators (revenue, retention) follow later. The tree should have both types.
5 Assign baselines and targets ▶
For each leaf metric, record the current value (baseline) and the target that indicates success. This turns the tree into a scorecard.
The universal formula: Almost every product's North Star can be decomposed as: North Star = Reach x Activation x Engagement x Value per Engagement. This pattern works across Business-to-Business software, consumer apps, marketplaces, and AI products. The specific metric names change, but the structure stays the same.

📚 How to measure reliable baselines
Measuring Baselines: Step by Step

Click a step to expand the details.

1 Decide the time window ▶
Baselines should cover at least 4 to 8 weeks of data to account for normal variation. If the business is seasonal, include a full cycle.
2 Measure by segment ▶
Do not just calculate one overall average. Break baselines by the customer segments defined in Discovery.
3 Calculate variability ▶
Report the range, not just the mean. If the baseline metric swings wildly, the team will need a larger sample to detect real changes.
4 Document the methodology ▶
Write down exactly how each metric was calculated and what was excluded. Future analysts need to reproduce the same calculation.
Tip: If no internal baseline exists (net new product), use published industry benchmarks or analogous product data. Label these as "proxy baselines" with lower confidence.
▶ 4 Set Success and Kill Thresholds Collaborative

Using the baselines and metrics tree, define what "good enough to continue" and "bad enough to stop" look like. These thresholds are agreed upon before launch so the team has objective criteria, not emotional reactions, when the data comes in.

Deliverables

  • Kill/pivot thresholds (the numbers that trigger a stop or change in direction)
  • Time windows for evaluation (how long to wait before making the call)
📚 How to set thresholds
Setting Thresholds: Step by Step

Click a step to expand the details.

1 Start with the baseline ▶
Every threshold is relative to the baseline. "15% improvement over current state" is more meaningful than an arbitrary number. Use the baselines from Step 3 as the starting point.
2 Use benchmarks to calibrate ▶
Compare against industry benchmarks to make sure the target is realistic. If the industry average is 25% and you're targeting 80%, something is off. If the best in class is 40%, targeting 35% is ambitious but achievable.
3 Define success thresholds ▶
For each key metric, set the number that means "this is working well enough to move to full Rollout." These should be specific: "Activation rate above 60% within 30 days" not just "good activation."
4 Define kill/pivot thresholds ▶
Set the numbers that trigger a hard conversation. "If Week 4 retention is below 10% across all segments, we stop and reassess." This must be agreed upon with leadership before launch. The power comes from setting the criteria before the team is emotionally invested.
5 Set evaluation time windows ▶
Decide how long to wait before checking each threshold. Leading indicators might be checked weekly, lagging indicators monthly. Don't let the team make premature calls on slow metrics.
Common mistake: Setting thresholds that are too vague ("good engagement") or too generous ("any improvement counts"). Thresholds should be specific numbers with time windows that the team commits to before launch.
▶ 5 Design the Validation Pilot Analyst Led

Before the product goes wide, design a controlled test with a small group of users. The pilot proves whether the product works for real customers in real conditions, and gives the team data to make the go/no go decision for full Rollout.

Deliverables

  • Power analysis: sample size needed to detect meaningful results
  • Test vs control group definitions
  • Decision criteria: what results trigger go, iterate, or stop
📚 How to design a validation pilot
Designing a Pilot: Step by Step

Click a step to expand the details.

1 Select the pilot segment ▶
Choose users from the highest priority segment identified in Discovery. They should be representative of the target customer, not just the friendliest or most enthusiastic users.
2 Run the power analysis ▶
Calculate how many users and how much time you need to detect a meaningful improvement. Use the baseline variability from Step 3 and the expected effect size. If the answer is "we need 50,000 users but only have 500," the experiment design needs to change.
3 Define test vs control ▶
The test group gets the product, the control group does not. Random assignment is ideal. If randomization is not possible, match on key characteristics (segment, usage level, tenure).
4 Set the duration ▶
Long enough to capture lagging indicators but short enough to make decisions. Typically 4 to 8 weeks depending on the product cycle and user frequency.
Watch out for: Pilot users often get more attention and support than regular users will. This inflates results. Plan for what the experience looks like without hand holding when designing success criteria.

Experiment Design Metrics
▶ Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)
What it measures

The smallest improvement the experiment is designed to detect with statistical confidence. Any real effect smaller than the MDE will likely be missed.

Formula
MDE = Success Threshold − Baseline Value
Example
Baseline activation rate is 40%. The success threshold is 52%. MDE = 52% − 40% = 12 percentage points (a 30% relative lift). The pilot must be powered to detect at least this difference.

The MDE connects baselines and thresholds to experiment design. A smaller MDE requires more users and more time. If the team wants to detect a 5% lift instead of a 12% lift, the pilot needs roughly 6x more users.

▶ Required Sample Size
What it measures

The number of users needed in each group (test and control) to detect the MDE with statistical confidence.

Formula (for proportions)
n per group = (Zα/2 + Zβ)² × [ p₁(1 − p₁) + p₂(1 − p₂) ] ÷ (p₂ − p₁)²
where p₁ = baseline rate, p₂ = baseline + MDE, α = 0.05 (significance), β = 0.20 (power = 80%)
Example
Baseline activation rate p₁ = 0.40. Target p₂ = 0.52 (MDE = 12pp). At 80% power and 95% confidence: n ≈ (1.96 + 0.84)² × [0.40 × 0.60 + 0.52 × 0.48] ÷ (0.12)² ≈ 7.84 × 0.4896 ÷ 0.0144 ≈ 267 users per group (534 total).

This tells the team whether a meaningful pilot is even feasible. If the calculation says 10,000 users per group but the company has 200 customers, the experiment design needs to change: increase the MDE, use a different method, or accept lower statistical power.

▶ Statistical Power
What it measures

The probability that the experiment will correctly detect a real effect of the specified MDE size. Expressed as a percentage.

Formula
Power = 1 − β (where β is the false negative rate)
Example
At 80% power (the standard), there is a 20% chance the experiment misses a real 12pp activation lift even though it exists. At 90% power, the miss rate drops to 10% but the required sample size increases by roughly 30% — from 267 to ~350 per group.

80% power is the industry standard for product experiments. Going higher is worthwhile when the stakes are high (expensive to re-run) or the decision is irreversible. Going lower is acceptable for exploratory tests where a false negative just means "run another test."

▶ Experiment Duration
What it measures

How many weeks the pilot or experiment must run to reach the required sample size given available traffic.

Formula
Duration (weeks) = Total Required Users ÷ New Eligible Users per Week
Example
Required sample: 534 total users. The product onboards 80 new users per week. Duration = 534 ÷ 80 ≈ 7 weeks. Add 1 to 2 weeks for lagging indicators to mature, so plan for an 8 to 9 week pilot.

This is the evaluation time window — how long the team waits before making the go/no-go call. If the math says 6 months but leadership wants answers in 4 weeks, the analyst needs to surface that conflict during Validation, not after the pilot starts.

▶ 🔬 Analysis Methods

Click a method to learn what it is, when to use it, and what to watch out for.

▶
BaselinesMeasure the current state before changing anything
Always do this first
What it is
Measure what is happening right now before the product launches. If the goal is to reduce manual review time, measure how long manual review takes today. If the goal is to increase conversion, measure the current conversion rate. Without a baseline, there is no way to know if the product made things better or worse.
When to use it
Always, for every metric that matters. Set baselines during Validation so the team can measure lift during Rollout. This applies to both existing platforms (measure current performance) and net new products (measure the current workaround or manual process).
Watch out for
Baselines can shift with seasonality. A baseline measured in December may not reflect January. Measure over a long enough window (at least 4 weeks, ideally 8) to capture natural variation.
▶
Pilot DesignTest the product on a small group before going wide
Core method
What it is
Give a small, carefully selected group of users access to the product and measure what happens. A pilot is not a full launch. It is a controlled test designed to answer the question: "Does this product actually work for real users?" The pilot group should be representative of the target customer, not just the friendliest or most enthusiastic users.
When to use it
When the product is ready enough to test but the team is not confident enough to launch broadly. Pilots are especially useful when the product involves behavior change, integration complexity, or high stakes decisions where a bad experience could damage customer trust.
Watch out for
Pilot users often get more attention and support than regular users will. This inflates results. Plan for what the experience looks like without hand-holding.
▶
Benchmark AnalysisCompare against industry standards to set realistic targets
What it is
Look at how similar products or competitors perform on the same metrics. If the industry average for week 4 retention in this product category is 25%, setting a target of 80% is unrealistic. If the best in class is 40%, a target of 35% is ambitious but achievable.
When to use it
When setting success thresholds for the North Star metric and kill/pivot criteria. Benchmarks prevent the team from setting targets that are either impossibly high or embarrassingly low.
Watch out for
Benchmarks from different product categories are misleading. A consumer social app and an enterprise workflow tool have completely different retention curves. Match the benchmark to the product type.
▶
Power AnalysisCalculate how many users and how much time an experiment needs
Core method
Formula (for proportions at 80% power, 95% confidence)
n per group = (Zα/2 + Zβ)² × [ p₁(1 − p₁) + p₂(1 − p₂) ] ÷ (p₂ − p₁)²
Zα/2 = 1.96 (for 95% confidence), Zβ = 0.84 (for 80% power), p₁ = baseline rate, p₂ = baseline + MDE
What it is
A calculation that answers: "Given our current number of users and the size of the improvement we expect, how long does the experiment need to run before we can trust the result?" It prevents the team from stopping an experiment too early (and seeing false signals) or running it too long (and wasting time).
Example calculation

Scenario: The baseline activation rate is 40%. The success threshold is 52% (MDE = 12 percentage points). The product onboards 80 new users per week.

Step 1 — Plug into the formula:

p₁ = 0.40, p₂ = 0.52
n = (1.96 + 0.84)² × [0.40 × 0.60 + 0.52 × 0.48] ÷ (0.52 − 0.40)²
n = (2.80)² × [0.24 + 0.2496] ÷ (0.12)²
n = 7.84 × 0.4896 ÷ 0.0144
n ≈ 267 users per group

Step 2 — Total sample and duration:

Total users needed = 267 × 2 = 534
At 80 users/week: 534 ÷ 80 ≈ 7 weeks
Add 1–2 weeks for lagging indicators → 8–9 week pilot

Step 3 — Sensitivity check:

If MDE = 5pp instead of 12pp → n ≈ 1,568 per group → 39 weeks
If MDE = 20pp instead of 12pp → n ≈ 93 per group → 3 weeks

Takeaway: Show the team this sensitivity table. It turns abstract statistics into a concrete trade-off: "How precise do you need the answer to be, and how long are you willing to wait?"

When to use it
Before any experiment starts. Do the calculation during Validation so the team knows in advance whether a meaningful experiment is even possible with the available user base. If the answer is "we need 50,000 users but only have 500," the experiment design needs to change.
Watch out for
Requires an estimate of the expected effect size. If unsure, run the calculation at multiple effect sizes (the sensitivity check above) and show the team the trade-off between precision and time. Also: for continuous metrics (like revenue per user) the formula uses means and standard deviations instead of proportions — the logic is the same but the inputs change.

Validation requires a step up from Discovery's lightweight stack:

  • Transformation layer (dbt or equivalent) — raw data needs to be cleaned and modeled into analytics-ready tables before baselines can be trusted
  • Business Intelligence tool — for presenting baseline reports, metrics trees, and target thresholds to stakeholders

▶ ⚖️ Trade-offs

Click a trade-off to see the tension and how the analyst helps resolve it.

▶
Early signals or proven resultsShould the team trust fast metrics or wait for reliable ones?
The tension
Early signals like activation rate or session frequency give answers fast but can be misleading. Proven results like revenue or long term retention are trustworthy but take months to show up. Relying only on early signals risks false confidence. Waiting only for proven results risks moving too slowly.
How the analyst helps
Build a metrics tree that shows how the early signals connect to the proven results. If historical data shows that higher activation rate reliably leads to higher retention, the team can trust the early signal. If there is no proven link, flag it.
▶
Ambitious targets or safe targetsShould success thresholds stretch the team or protect against failure?
The tension
Setting the bar too high means the product always looks like a failure even when it is making real progress. Setting the bar too low means the team declares success without actually solving the problem.
How the analyst helps
Use baseline data and industry benchmarks to set two targets: a "minimum viable success" threshold (the product must clear this bar to justify continued investment) and an "aspirational success" target (what great looks like). This gives the team a clear floor and a stretch goal.
▶ 🚨 Warning Signs and Kill Signals
Product-Market Fit Signal — Validation
This is the phase where kill/pivot criteria should be defined — before the product launches, not after. The analyst should work with leadership to set explicit thresholds: "If Week-4 retention is below X%, if the North Star metric does not move by Y% within the first 3 cohorts, or if no customer segment shows above-average engagement, we will trigger a formal pivot/kill review." Documenting these thresholds now prevents the team from moving goalposts later when sunk costs make objectivity difficult.

What to watch for:

  • Inability to define a clear North Star metric — if the team cannot agree on what success looks like, the product concept may be too vague
  • Baselines that are already high — if customers are already performing well without the product, the room for improvement may be too small to justify the investment
  • No agreement on kill/pivot thresholds — if leadership refuses to define conditions for stopping, the team is planning to succeed regardless of data

▶ 🛠️ Technology Considerations

Build is where the analytics infrastructure becomes critical. The minimum viable stack now requires:

  • Data Collection layer — event tracking must be instrumented before launch. Options include Segment, Rudderstack, Snowplow, or custom event pipelines
  • Data Warehouse — must be operational and receiving data
  • Transformation layer (dbt or equivalent) — raw events need to be modeled into analytics-ready tables
  • Business Intelligence tool — dashboards need to be built and tested before launch

Infrastructure Audit Checklist

Before the product ships, the analyst should verify:

  • Where does product data live today? (Warehouse, application database, third-party SaaS?)
  • What transformation layer exists? (dbt models, custom SQL scripts, nothing?)
  • How are dashboards currently built?
  • Is there an event tracking system in place?
  • What experimentation infrastructure exists? (Feature flags, A/B test tooling, manual rollouts?)
Common Anti-Pattern
Do not wait until rollout to discover the stack has gaps. A frequent failure mode: product launches with no event tracking, early users go unmeasured, baseline data is lost forever, and leadership asks "is the product working?" with no way to answer. The infrastructure audit should happen during Validation, so gaps can be closed during Build.

▶ 🚨 Warning Signs and Kill Signals

Build phase warning signs are less about product-market fit and more about readiness risk:

  • Event taxonomy keeps changing late in development: if the tracking plan is unstable, the team does not have a clear picture of what matters. Stabilize the event taxonomy at least 2 weeks before launch
  • No data quality validation process: if no one is checking whether events fire correctly, launch day will reveal data gaps that could have been caught earlier
  • Dashboards are "coming soon": if dashboards are not built and tested before launch, the analyst will be building them while also trying to analyze results — a recipe for missed insights
  • Engineering pushback on instrumentation: if engineering treats analytics events as low-priority, critical tracking may be cut from the launch. The analyst should frame tracking as a launch requirement, not a nice-to-have

→

Experiment Design: Step by Step

1 Choose the method based on constraints ▶

Adoption Metrics
▶ Activation Rate
What it measures

Percentage of new users who reach the product's core value moment.

Formula
Users Who Reached Core Value Moment ÷ Total New Users × 100
Example
200 users signed up this month. 130 completed their first successful workflow. Activation rate = 130 ÷ 200 × 100 = 65%.

Did the user actually experience what the product does? This is the "Aha moment" metric. Target depends on product type, but below 40% in the first 30 days signals onboarding or value delivery problems.

▶ Time-to-Activation
What it measures

How many days from first exposure to first meaningful use.

Formula
Median( Date of Core Value Moment − Date of First Login ) across all activated users
Example
130 users activated this month. The median time from first login to first successful workflow was 3 days. If the 75th percentile is 14 days, the long tail needs investigation.

The faster users reach the value moment, the more likely they stick around. Long time-to-activation means onboarding is too slow or the product requires too much setup.

▶ Funnel Conversion Rate
What it measures

The percentage of users who complete each step in the adoption funnel, from awareness through retention.

Formula (overall)
Users Completing Final Step ÷ Users Entering Step 1 × 100
Formula (step-to-step)
Users Completing Step N ÷ Users Completing Step N−1 × 100
Example
1,000 users exposed → 400 signed up (40%) → 260 activated (65%) → 130 engaged weekly (50%) → 78 retained at 30 days (60%). Overall conversion: 78 ÷ 1,000 = 7.8%. The biggest drop is exposure to signup (40%), making it the highest impact place to improve.

The step with the largest percentage drop is usually where the team should focus. Always segment the funnel — one customer type may flow through while another gets stuck at step two.

▶ DAU / MAU Ratio
What it measures

How often active users come back each day. A measure of product stickiness.

Formula
Daily Active Users ÷ Monthly Active Users × 100
Example
The product has 500 monthly active users and averages 120 daily active users. DAU/MAU = 120 ÷ 500 × 100 = 24%. Above 20% suggests a daily habit; below 10% suggests occasional use.

Tells the team how sticky the product is. A tool that is only opened once a week will have a low ratio regardless of how many people signed up.

Value Metrics
▶ Model Precision
What it measures

Of all the suggestions the model flagged as positive, how many were actually correct.

Formula
True Positives ÷ (True Positives + False Positives) × 100
Example
The model flagged 200 items. 170 were correct and 30 were wrong. Precision = 170 ÷ 200 × 100 = 85%. Target: above 85%. If below, review false positive cases by category to find patterns.

High precision means users can trust the suggestions. Low precision creates alert fatigue — users stop paying attention because too many flags are wrong.

▶ Model Recall
What it measures

Of all the items that should have been flagged, how many did the model actually catch.

Formula
True Positives ÷ (True Positives + False Negatives) × 100
Example
There were 240 items that should have been flagged. The model caught 170 and missed 70. Recall = 170 ÷ 240 × 100 = 70.8%. Target: above 70%. If below, review missed cases for unlearned patterns.

High recall means fewer problems slip through the cracks. The trade-off: pushing recall higher usually lowers precision (more false alarms).

▶ Acceptance Rate
What it measures

Percentage of AI suggestions that users accept (versus reject or ignore).

Formula
Suggestions Accepted ÷ Total Suggestions Shown × 100
Example
The product showed 1,000 suggestions this week. Users accepted 520, rejected 280, and ignored 200. Acceptance rate = 520 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 52%. Target: above 50% in first 90 days. If below, separate model accuracy issues from UX issues.

Acceptance rate bridges model performance and user experience. A model can be highly precise but still have low acceptance if suggestions appear at the wrong moment or are hard to act on.

▶ Value Delivered per Customer
What it measures

The measurable outcome the product delivers to each customer, expressed in the unit that matters to the business.

Formula (time savings example)
(Baseline Time per Task − Current Time per Task) × Tasks per Customer per Month
Formula (revenue example)
Revenue Captured with Product − Revenue Captured without Product, per Customer per Period
Example
Before the product, review took 45 minutes per case. After: 30 minutes. Each customer processes 200 cases per month. Value = (45 − 30) × 200 = 3,000 minutes saved per customer per month (50 hours). Target: within 20% of the projected estimate from Validation.

This is the metric that justifies the product's existence. If value delivered falls short of projections, check volume, use case mix, and workflow assumptions before concluding the product does not work.

Guardrail Metrics
▶ False Positive Rate
What it measures

For AI products: the percentage of items incorrectly flagged as positive out of all items that were actually negative.

Formula
False Positives ÷ (False Positives + True Negatives) × 100
Example
The model reviewed 1,000 cases. 800 were truly negative. Of those 800, the model incorrectly flagged 40. False positive rate = 40 ÷ 800 × 100 = 5%. If the guardrail threshold is 20%, this is well within bounds.

False positives erode user trust. Every wrong suggestion trains the user to ignore future suggestions. Monitor this weekly and alert if it crosses the threshold set during Validation.

▶ Workflow Disruption (Time on Task)
What it measures

Whether the product is making users faster or slower at their core workflow, compared to the baseline measured before launch.

Formula
(Current Median Time on Task − Baseline Median Time on Task) ÷ Baseline Median Time on Task × 100
Example
Baseline time on task was 45 minutes. After product launch, median time is 48 minutes. Change = (48 − 45) ÷ 45 × 100 = +6.7% slower. If the guardrail threshold is 10%, this is within bounds but trending in the wrong direction.

A product that saves time on one task but adds friction elsewhere may produce a net negative. Measure the full workflow, not just the step the product touches.

▶ User Satisfaction (NPS / CSAT)
What it measures

How users feel about the product, captured through structured surveys at key moments in the user journey.

Formula (NPS)
% Promoters (9-10) − % Detractors (0-6)
Formula (CSAT)
Users Rating 4 or 5 ÷ Total Respondents × 100
Example
100 users surveyed: 45 promoters, 35 passives, 20 detractors. NPS = 45% − 20% = +25. For CSAT: 60 users rated 4 or 5 out of 100 respondents = 60%. Watch for drops after product changes.

Quantitative metrics can look healthy while users are quietly frustrated. Satisfaction scores catch sentiment problems that usage data misses. Survey at activation and again at 30 days.

▶ 🔬 Analysis Methods

Rollout is where the heaviest analysis happens. Click a method to see what it is, when to use it, and how to choose between them.

▶
A/B Testing (Randomized Experiment)Split users randomly and compare outcomes
Gold standard
What it is
Randomly assign users into two groups: one gets the new product or feature (treatment), the other does not (control). Compare outcomes between the groups. Because assignment is random, any difference in outcomes can be attributed to the product, not to pre-existing differences between users.
When to use it
When the team has enough users to split into groups and can randomly assign without business constraints. This is the strongest evidence that the product actually caused an improvement. Use it whenever possible.
Watch out for
Requires enough users to detect a meaningful difference (use power analysis from Validation). Also cannot be used when randomization is impossible, such as when the product must be rolled out to all users in a region at once.
▶
Pre/Post AnalysisCompare "before" and "after" the product launched
When A/B is not possible
Formula
Metric After Launch − Metric Before Launch = Observed Change
What it is
Measure the metric before the product launched and again after. If manual review time was 45 minutes before and 30 minutes after, the product appears to have saved 15 minutes. Simple and easy to explain.
When to use it
When A/B testing is not possible (too few users, cannot randomize, or the product went to everyone at once). Pre/post is the simplest analysis when randomization is off the table.
Watch out for
The biggest weakness: other things changed at the same time. Maybe the improvement happened because of a seasonal trend, a policy change, or something unrelated to the product. Pre/post cannot separate the product's effect from everything else that changed.
▶
Difference in DifferencesCompare treated and untreated groups while accounting for background trends
When A/B is not possible
Formula
(Treatment After − Treatment Before) − (Control After − Control Before) = True Effect
What it is
Compare two groups over time: one that got the product and one that did not. Measure how both groups were trending before the product launched. Then measure the difference after launch. The product's true effect is the difference between how the treated group changed versus how the untreated group changed. This removes background trends that affect both groups equally.
When to use it
When A/B testing is not possible but a natural comparison group exists. For example: the product launched at Hospital A but not Hospital B. If both hospitals were trending the same direction before launch, the difference after launch is a good estimate of the product's effect.
Watch out for
Only works if both groups were on similar trends before launch (the "parallel trends" assumption). If the treated group was already improving faster, the method will overestimate the product's effect. Always plot the pre-launch trends side by side to check.
▶
Interrupted Time SeriesLook for a visible shift in the trend line after launch
When no comparison group exists
What it is
Track a metric over a long period, mark the launch date, and look for a visible change in the trend. If the metric was flat for 12 months and then jumped right after launch, the product likely caused the change. The more data points before and after, the more convincing the result.
When to use it
When there is no comparison group at all. Everyone got the product at the same time, so A/B and difference in differences are not options. The analyst needs a long pre-launch history (at least 8 to 12 data points) to establish the baseline trend.
Watch out for
If something else changed at the same time as the launch (new policy, seasonal shift, marketing campaign), the method cannot separate those effects. Document any other changes that happened near the launch date.
▶
Funnel AnalysisTrack where users drop off between steps
Core method
Formula
Users Completing Step N ÷ Users Entering Step 1 × 100 = Overall Conversion %
Users Completing Step N ÷ Users Completing Step N−1 × 100 = Step Conversion %
What it is
Define the sequence of steps a user takes from first exposure to full adoption (for example: sees product → signs up → completes setup → uses core feature → uses it again). Measure how many users complete each step and where the biggest drop offs happen. The step with the biggest drop off is usually the highest impact place to improve.
When to use it
As soon as real users start using the product. Funnel analysis is the first thing to run in Rollout because it shows where the product is losing people. A product with 1,000 sign-ups and 50 active users has a very different problem than one with 100 sign-ups and 80 active users.
Watch out for
Segment the funnel. One customer type may flow through smoothly while another gets stuck at step 2. An overall funnel can hide that the product works well for some users and terribly for others.
▶
Cohort AnalysisGroup users by when they started and compare their behavior over time
Core method
Formula
Users Active at Day N ÷ Total Users in Cohort × 100 = Day N Retention %
What it is
Group users by when they first used the product (week 1 cohort, week 2 cohort, and so on). Track each group over time. This reveals whether later cohorts are doing better than earlier ones (the product is improving) or worse (something is degrading). It also reveals retention: what percentage of each cohort is still active after 7 days, 14 days, 30 days.
When to use it
After the first few weeks of Rollout, once enough cohorts exist to compare. Cohort analysis answers the question: "Is the product getting better over time, or are we just adding more users to a leaky bucket?"
Watch out for
Early cohorts often look worse because the product was less polished. Later cohorts may benefit from bug fixes and improvements. Separate product improvements from cohort effects when interpreting the data.
▶
Segment Performance ComparisonBreak every metric out by customer segment to find where the product works and where it does not
Core method
Formula (segment-level metric)
Metric Value for Segment A ÷ Metric Value Overall = Segment Index
What it is
Take every key metric (activation rate, acceptance rate, model precision, value delivered) and compute it separately for each customer segment: tier, use case, company size, and user role. Rank segments from highest to lowest on each metric. A segment index above 1.0 means that segment outperforms the average; below 1.0 means it underperforms. This reveals which segments the product fits and which it does not.
When to use it
As soon as enough data exists per segment to be meaningful (typically 2 to 4 weeks into Rollout). Run it before the expand/iterate/pause decision because segment performance directly determines the expansion strategy: expand into segments that work, iterate on segments with potential, deprioritize segments where the product does not fit.
Watch out for
Small segments can produce misleading results. If a segment has only 15 users, a 90% activation rate may be noise. Set a minimum sample size per segment before drawing conclusions. Also watch for segments that look healthy on adoption metrics but fail on value metrics — high activation does not guarantee the product is delivering results.
▶
Guardrail Threshold MonitoringSet numeric boundaries and alert when the product crosses them
Core method
Formula (threshold check)
If Current Metric Value > Threshold → Alert and Escalate
Formula (trend detection)
If Metric Value increases for N consecutive periods → Early Warning
What it is
Define a numeric threshold for each guardrail metric (false positive rate, workflow disruption, user satisfaction) based on Validation baselines, then build automated monitoring that checks the current value against that threshold on a daily or weekly cadence. When a threshold is crossed, the system alerts the analyst and PM immediately rather than waiting for a scheduled review. Also track trends: a metric that is below the threshold but rising for three consecutive weeks is an early warning even before it crosses.
When to use it
From day one of Rollout and continuously throughout. Set up the monitoring before launch so it is running when the first users arrive. Review guardrail dashboards weekly and include them in every stakeholder update. Increase monitoring frequency during the first two weeks when problems are most likely to surface.
Watch out for
Two failure modes: thresholds set too tight (constant false alarms that the team starts ignoring) and thresholds set too loose (real problems slip through). Start with the thresholds from Validation, then tighten them as the team gains confidence. Also define what happens when an alert fires before it fires — the escalation protocol should not be invented during a crisis.
▶
Sequential TestingCheck experiment results as data comes in instead of waiting until the end
What it is
Instead of running an A/B test for a fixed time and then looking at the result, check the results continuously as data accumulates. Uses adjusted statistical thresholds so that peeking at the data does not inflate the chance of a false positive. This allows the team to stop the experiment early if the result is clearly positive or clearly negative.
When to use it
When the business cannot wait for a fixed experiment window. Sequential testing is faster on average because obvious results are detected early. Especially useful when the expected effect is large or when running the experiment has a cost (for example, showing users a worse experience in the control group).
Watch out for
Must use the correct statistical method (like the sequential probability ratio test or always-valid confidence intervals). Simply peeking at a standard A/B test and stopping early will produce unreliable results.
▶
Multi Armed BanditAutomatically send more traffic to the winning version while still testing
What it is
Instead of splitting traffic 50/50 for the entire experiment, the system gradually shifts more users to whichever version is performing better. This reduces the cost of showing users the worse version while still gathering enough data to confirm the winner.
When to use it
When optimization matters more than precise measurement. For example, testing three different onboarding flows where the cost of a bad experience is high. Bandits are also useful when testing many variants at once (more than two or three) where a standard A/B test would require impractical sample sizes.
Watch out for
Bandits are weaker at estimating the exact size of the difference between versions. If the team needs to know "Version B is 12% better," use an A/B test. If the team just needs to know "which version is best," a bandit works.
▶ 🛠️ Technology Considerations

Rollout is where the full analytics stack is needed. The minimum viable stack now requires:

  • All Build phase infrastructure must be operational (data collection, warehouse, transformation, dashboards)
  • Product Analytics platform (Amplitude, Mixpanel, Heap, or PostHog) — needed to track adoption funnels, feature usage, and engagement patterns
  • Experimentation platform (LaunchDarkly, Statsig, or Eppo) — needed for A/B testing and controlled rollouts

The Business-to-Business Adoption Funnel

Awareness: Organization leadership knows the product exists ↓ Contract: Organization signs and integration begins ↓ Activation: First user completes first real workflow ↓ Engagement: Multiple users using the product daily ↓ Habit: Product is embedded in standard operating procedure
Common Pitfall
Do not conflate model accuracy with product success. A model can have high precision and recall but fail in production because it was trained on a different distribution or the product does not surface suggestions at the right moment in the workflow. Always track both the model layer and the product layer independently.
▶ ⚖️ Trade-offs

Click a trade-off to see the tension and how the analyst helps resolve it.

▶
Wait for certainty or decide nowHow confident does the team need to be before acting on experiment results?
The tension
Waiting until the data is 95% certain takes time. The business wants answers now. But deciding too early risks acting on random noise instead of a real signal.
How the analyst helps
Calculate in advance how long the experiment needs to run to get a reliable answer, given the current number of users. Tell the team: "At our current volume, a trustworthy result takes X weeks." This removes the guesswork.
▶
Catch everything or avoid false alarmsFor AI products: should the model flag more things or only flag what it is sure about?
The tension
Flagging more things means fewer problems slip through the cracks, but users get overwhelmed with false alarms. Flagging only high confidence items means fewer false alarms, but real problems get missed. The right balance depends entirely on the product and its users.
How the analyst helps
Test different confidence thresholds and measure the outcome at each one. Find the setting where the product creates the most value: enough catches to be useful, few enough false alarms that users trust it.
▶
Launch wide or roll out graduallyShould the product go to all users at once or expand in stages?
The tension
Launching to everyone captures market share quickly. Rolling out in stages lets the team catch and fix problems before they affect the full user base. But going too slowly gives competitors time to catch up.
How the analyst helps
Set clear expansion criteria with numbers: "Expand to the next user group when activation rate exceeds X% and no guardrail metrics have been breached for Y consecutive weeks." This gives the team a data driven greenlight instead of debating readiness.
▶ 🚨 Warning Signs and Kill Signals
Product-Market Fit Signal — Rollout
Rollout is where product-market fit is tested against real users. Compare these patterns against the kill/pivot thresholds established in Phase 2 (Validation). If thresholds are triggered, escalate to the formal pivot/kill review defined earlier.

Warning patterns to watch for:

  • High acquisition, low activation: People sign up but never reach the core value moment — the product may not deliver what marketing promises
  • Strong Week-1 engagement, steep Week-2+ drop: Novelty effect — users explore but do not find lasting value
  • A/B tests show no significant lift on any variation: When no version of the product moves the needle, the problem may be the concept, not the execution
  • Guardrails are consistently breached: The product is causing harm (workflow disruption, false positives) that outweighs its benefits
  • Model performance is strong but product outcomes are weak: High precision/recall but low acceptance rate signals that the AI works technically but does not fit into the user's workflow

Model Performance Monitoring: Step by Step

1 Establish performance baselines from Rollout ▶

▶ 5 Continue / Pivot / Stop Recommendation Collaborative

▶ 📊 Key Metrics

Click a metric to see what it measures, its formula, and an example calculation.

Retention Metrics
▶ N-Day Retention
What it measures

Percentage of users still active at Day 1, Day 7, Day 30, and Day 90 after their start date.

Formula
Users Active on Day N ÷ Users Who Started in Cohort × 100
Example
A cohort of 500 users started in January. At Day 30, 175 are still completing core actions. Day 30 Retention = 175 ÷ 500 × 100 = 35%. For B2B SaaS, Week 4 retention above 25% is generally healthy.

A retention curve that flattens (even at a low percentage) is better than one that keeps declining. The flat portion represents core retained users who found lasting value.

▶ Cohort Retention Trend
What it measures

Whether later cohorts retain better or worse than earlier ones at the same point in their lifecycle.

Formula
(Cohort N Day-30 Retention − Cohort N−1 Day-30 Retention) for each successive cohort
Example
January cohort had 28% Day 30 retention, February had 32%, March had 35%. Each cohort improves by 3 to 4 points. Positive trend — the product or onboarding is getting better.

Improving cohort retention means the product is getting better. Declining means something is degrading — this is one of the most critical signals at Scale and feeds directly into the PMF scorecard.

▶ Net Revenue Retention
What it measures

Revenue from existing customers compared to the same period last year, including expansion and churn.

Formula
(Starting Revenue + Expansion Revenue − Churned Revenue − Contraction Revenue) ÷ Starting Revenue × 100
Example
Starting MRR from existing customers: $100K. Expansion: $15K. Churn: $8K. Contraction: $2K. NRR = ($100K + $15K − $8K − $2K) ÷ $100K × 100 = 105%. Above 100% means existing customer growth outpaces losses.

The gold standard B2B metric. Above 100% means the business grows even without new customers. Below 100% means the company must acquire new customers just to maintain revenue.

▶ Customer Lifetime Value
What it measures

Total expected revenue from a customer over their entire relationship with the product.

Formula
Average Revenue per User per Month × Average Customer Lifespan in Months
Unit economics check
Lifetime Value ÷ Customer Acquisition Cost = LTV:CAC Ratio (target 3:1 or higher)
Example
Average revenue per customer: $2,000/month. Average lifespan: 18 months. LTV = $2,000 × 18 = $36,000. If acquisition cost is $10,000, LTV:CAC = 3.6:1 — healthy unit economics.

Determines how much the company can spend to acquire customers and still be profitable. LTV estimates are sensitive to the retention assumption — always show the sensitivity range.

AI Model Health Metrics
▶ Precision (at Scale)
What it measures

Of all suggestions the model flags as positive, how many are actually correct — monitored over time for degradation.

Formula
True Positives ÷ (True Positives + False Positives) × 100
Example
Week 1: precision was 87%. Week 8: precision dropped to 79%. Two consecutive weeks below 80% triggers investigation. Break down false positives by data category to find the pattern.

At Scale, precision can degrade as the customer base diversifies. Alert threshold: below 80% for 2 consecutive weeks. Action: investigate false positive patterns by category and segment.

▶ Recall (at Scale)
What it measures

Of all items that should have been flagged, how many the model actually caught — monitored over time for degradation.

Formula
True Positives ÷ (True Positives + False Negatives) × 100
Example
The model should have flagged 300 items this month. It caught 210 and missed 90. Recall = 210 ÷ 300 × 100 = 70%. If recall drops below 65%, check for distribution shift in input data.

Recall can decline at Scale when new customer segments bring data patterns the model was not trained on. This is a signal for retraining, not a product failure.

▶ Confidence Calibration
What it measures

Whether the model's confidence scores match actual accuracy — a model that says "90% confident" should be correct 90% of the time.

Formula (Expected Calibration Error)
Sum of |Actual Accuracy in Bin − Predicted Confidence in Bin| × (Number in Bin ÷ Total), across all confidence bins
Example
For items the model scored at 90% confidence, actual accuracy is 82%. For items at 70% confidence, actual accuracy is 71%. The high confidence bin is miscalibrated by 8 points. ECE = 4.2% (weighted average). Alert if ECE exceeds 10%.

Miscalibrated confidence means the team cannot use confidence scores to prioritize reviews. If high confidence items are often wrong, users learn not to trust any score.

▶ Suggestion Acceptance Rate (at Scale)
What it measures

Percentage of AI suggestions that users accept — tracked over time and by segment to detect trust erosion.

Formula
Suggestions Accepted ÷ Total Suggestions Shown × 100
Example
Month 1 acceptance rate: 55%. Month 3: 48%. Month 6: 41%. Declining for 3 consecutive periods. Alert: below 40% or declining 3 consecutive cohorts. Separate model accuracy issues from UX and user tenure issues.

A declining acceptance rate does not always mean the model is getting worse. It could be a UX change, less trained new users, or remaining cases being harder. Always decompose by model confidence level and user tenure.

▶ Data Drift
What it measures

Whether the distribution of incoming data has shifted significantly from the data the model was trained on.

Formula (Population Stability Index)
Sum of (Actual % in Bin − Expected % in Bin) × ln(Actual % in Bin ÷ Expected % in Bin), across all bins
Example
PSI calculated across 10 feature bins. PSI = 0.08. Below 0.1 = no significant drift. Between 0.1 and 0.25 = moderate drift, investigate. Above 0.25 = significant drift, trigger retraining pipeline.

Drift detection is a leading indicator — it catches problems before accuracy metrics visibly degrade. New customer segments are the most common cause of drift at Scale.

Product-Market Fit Scorecard
▶ Week 4 Retention
What it measures

The percentage of users still active 4 weeks after starting — a key indicator of whether the product delivers lasting value.

Formula
Users Active at Week 4 ÷ Users Who Started × 100
Example
200 users started in the March cohort. At Week 4, 60 are still active. Week 4 Retention = 60 ÷ 200 × 100 = 30%. Healthy: above 25%. Warning: 10 to 25% and declining. Critical: below 10% across all segments.

Week 4 is the standard checkpoint because most users who will churn have done so by then. If no segment retains above 10% at Week 4, the product has a fundamental fit problem.

▶ Cohort Lifetime Value Trend
What it measures

Whether the projected lifetime value of later cohorts is higher or lower than earlier cohorts.

Formula
Projected LTV of Cohort N − Projected LTV of Cohort N−1
Example
Q1 cohort projected LTV: $28,000. Q2 cohort projected LTV: $33,000. Q3 cohort projected LTV: $31,000. Generally positive trend but Q3 dip warrants investigation. Healthy: later cohorts higher. Warning: flat. Critical: declining.

Improving cohort LTV means the product is getting better at delivering value over time. Declining LTV means the product may be attracting lower quality users or losing its edge.

▶ North Star Movement
What it measures

Whether the North Star metric (the single metric capturing customer value) is improving as the customer base grows.

Formula
(Current Period North Star − Prior Period North Star) ÷ Prior Period North Star × 100
Example
North Star (revenue captured per customer per month): Q1 = $4,200. Q2 = $4,800. Change = ($4,800 − $4,200) ÷ $4,200 × 100 = +14.3%. Healthy: above target in 2 to 3 cohorts. Warning: below target but trending up. Critical: no movement after 3+ cohorts.

If the North Star stops improving as the customer base grows, the product may be hitting a ceiling. Segment the metric to find whether the stall is universal or concentrated in specific customer types.

▶ Segment Traction
What it measures

How many distinct customer segments show healthy retention and outcome metrics.

Formula
Count of Segments Where Week 4 Retention > 25% AND North Star Above Target
Example
5 segments defined. 3 have Week 4 retention above 25% and North Star above target. Segment Traction = 3 of 5 healthy. Healthy: multiple segments. Warning: only 1 to 2 niche segments. Critical: no segment above average.

A product that only works for one niche segment has limited scale potential. Multiple healthy segments signal broad product-market fit and a clear path to growth.

▶ User Sentiment (NPS / CSAT)
What it measures

How users feel about the product, measured through Net Promoter Score or Customer Satisfaction surveys.

Formula (NPS)
% Promoters (9 to 10 rating) − % Detractors (0 to 6 rating)
Example
Survey of 200 users: 90 promoters (45%), 60 passives (30%), 50 detractors (25%). NPS = 45% − 25% = +20. Healthy: NPS above 30. Warning: 0 to 30 and stagnant. Critical: negative and declining.

Sentiment is a leading indicator — it drops before retention does. A product with high usage but declining NPS is heading for a retention cliff.

▶ 🔬 Analysis Methods

Scale phase methods focus on understanding long term behavior, predicting churn, evaluating product-market fit, and monitoring AI model health. Click a method to learn more.

▶
Retention Curve AnalysisTrack what percentage of users are still active over time
Core method
Formula
Users Still Active at Day N ÷ Users Who Started × 100 = Day N Retention %
What it is
Plot the percentage of users who are still active at day 1, day 7, day 14, day 30, day 60, and day 90. The shape of this curve tells the story. A curve that flattens out means the product has found a core user base. A curve that keeps dropping means users are not finding lasting value.
When to use it
As soon as the first cohorts have 30 or more days of history. Retention curves are the single most important chart for evaluating product-market fit. If the curve does not flatten, almost nothing else matters.
Watch out for
Define "active" clearly. Logging in is not the same as getting value. Choose a definition that reflects meaningful engagement, like completing a core action, not just opening the app.
▶
Cohort AnalysisCompare performance across groups of users who started at different times
Core method
Formula (cohort comparison)
Cohort N Day-30 Retention − Cohort N−1 Day-30 Retention = Cohort Improvement
What it is
Group users by when they joined and compare retention, engagement, and outcome metrics across cohorts. Three types: time based cohorts (by signup date), behavioral cohorts (by first week behavior), and segment based cohorts (by customer characteristics). Time based cohorts reveal whether the product is improving. Behavioral cohorts reveal which early actions predict long term retention.
When to use it
When you need to understand whether the product is improving over time or whether early users were just more enthusiastic. Also use to identify which first week behaviors predict 90 day retention, which becomes input for the churn prediction model.
Watch out for
Cohort size matters. A cohort with 12 users and 90% retention looks great but is statistically meaningless. Always note the sample size when presenting cohort results.
▶
Survival Analysis (Kaplan-Meier)Estimate how long users stay before leaving
Core method
Formula (hazard rate)
Number Who Churned in Period ÷ Number at Risk at Start of Period
What it is
A statistical method that estimates the probability of a user still being active at any given point in time. Unlike simple retention curves, survival analysis handles users who joined recently and have not had time to churn yet (called "censored" data). It answers: "What is the median time before a user stops using the product?"
When to use it
When comparing retention across segments or time periods where cohorts have different amounts of history. Standard retention curves cannot fairly compare a 90 day old cohort with a 30 day old cohort. Survival analysis can. Also use to produce statistically valid median lifetime estimates for LTV calculations.
Watch out for
Requires a clear definition of "churned." For some products a user who is inactive for 14 days has churned. For others, 90 days of inactivity is normal. Choose the threshold based on the product's natural usage pattern.
▶
Churn Prediction ModelingIdentify users likely to leave before they actually leave
Advanced method
Typical features
Sessions in Week 1, Feature Diversity Used, Session Duration Trend, Support Tickets Filed, Days Since Last Login → Churn Risk Score (0 to 100)
What it is
Build a model that uses user behavior patterns (declining usage, fewer logins, support tickets) to predict which users are at risk of leaving. The output is a "churn risk score" that the customer success team can act on before the user is gone.
When to use it
Once there is enough historical data to train the model, typically after several months of Rollout data with enough churned users to learn from. The model needs examples of users who stayed and users who left to identify the differences.
Watch out for
A prediction is only useful if the team acts on it. Build the intervention process (who gets notified, what action they take) before building the model. A model that predicts churn but generates no action is wasted effort.
▶
Segmentation AnalysisFind which types of customers behave differently
Core method
Formula (segment index)
Segment Metric Value ÷ Overall Average Metric Value = Segment Index (above 1.0 = outperforms)
What it is
Group users by shared characteristics (company size, industry, usage pattern, acquisition channel) and compare how each group retains, engages, and generates revenue. This reveals that "the product" may actually be multiple products serving different types of users with different levels of success.
When to use it
When overall metrics look mediocre but the team suspects that some users are thriving while others are struggling. Segmentation often reveals that a product has strong fit for one customer type and poor fit for another, which changes the growth strategy entirely. Feeds directly into the ICP and growth targeting deliverable.
Watch out for
Too many segments dilute the analysis. Start with 3 to 5 meaningful segments based on characteristics the business can act on. A segment is only useful if the team can do something different for that group.
▶
Lifetime Value AnalysisCalculate how much revenue a customer generates over their entire relationship
Core method
Formula
Average Revenue per User × Average Customer Lifespan = Lifetime Value
Lifetime Value ÷ Customer Acquisition Cost = LTV:CAC Ratio (aim for 3:1 or higher)
What it is
Estimate the total revenue a customer will generate from signup to churn. This combines average revenue per user, expected retention duration, and expansion revenue. It answers: "Is it worth spending $X to acquire this type of customer?"
When to use it
When making investment decisions about retention versus acquisition, deciding which customer segments to prioritize, or evaluating whether the unit economics are sustainable. LTV is the metric that connects product performance to business viability.
Watch out for
LTV estimates are sensitive to the retention assumption. A small change in expected retention duration creates a large change in the LTV number. Always show the sensitivity: "If average retention is 12 months, LTV is $X. If retention is 18 months, LTV is $Y."
▶
Model Drift DetectionDetect when incoming data no longer matches the training data
Advanced method
Formula (Population Stability Index)
Sum of (Actual % − Expected %) × ln(Actual % ÷ Expected %), across all bins
What it is
Statistical tests that compare the distribution of incoming production data against the distribution the model was trained on. When distributions diverge significantly, the model's learned patterns may no longer apply and performance will degrade.
When to use it
Continuously for AI products at Scale. Run weekly checks against training data distributions. Drift is a leading indicator — it catches problems before accuracy metrics visibly degrade. New customer segments are the most common cause of drift at Scale.
Watch out for
Not all drift is harmful. Some distribution shifts reflect legitimate business changes (new customer types, seasonal patterns). Investigate the cause of drift before triggering retraining. PSI below 0.1: stable. PSI 0.1 to 0.25: investigate. PSI above 0.25: retrain.
▶
Product-Market Fit ScoringCombine multiple signals into a single fit assessment
Core method
Scorecard dimensions
Week 4 Retention + Cohort LTV Trend + North Star Movement + Segment Traction + User Sentiment = Composite PMF Score
What it is
A structured scorecard that rates the product across multiple dimensions of product-market fit, each with green/yellow/red thresholds. No single metric proves PMF — the scorecard combines retention, value delivery, segment breadth, and sentiment into a holistic assessment.
When to use it
Monthly, once enough cohorts have matured (typically 60 to 90 days of data). Use it to drive the continue/pivot/stop conversation. The scorecard should be presented to leadership with the thresholds that were agreed on during Validation.
Watch out for
A product can score green on most dimensions and red on one critical one (for example, strong retention but no North Star improvement). Do not average away the red — a single critical failure can invalidate the others.

Understanding NRR with examples

NRR answers one question: if you stopped acquiring new customers today, would your revenue still grow, shrink, or stay flat?

Three things happen to existing customers over time:

  • Expansion: they pay you more (add users, upgrade, buy additional features)
  • Churn: they leave entirely and you lose all their revenue
  • Contraction: they stay but pay less (downgrade, remove users, drop a feature)

SmarterDx at 175% NRR: a hospital starts the quarter paying for 20 coders. By end of quarter they have expanded to 35 coders because other departments saw the value and wanted in. No one left. Starting revenue was $20k/month, now $35k/month. NRR = ($20k + $15k - $0 - $0) / $20k = 175%. The product is growing itself inside the account.

A SaaS tool at 95% NRR: you start the quarter with $100k from existing customers. One customer churns ($8k gone), another downgrades ($2k lost), but a few others add seats ($5k gained). NRR = ($100k + $5k - $8k - $2k) / $100k = 95%. You are slowly losing ground. Every quarter you need to sell more just to stay even.

Slack at 140%+ NRR (real world): teams would start with 10 people on a paid plan. Within a year, entire departments adopted it. The company did not have to sell to those new users. The product pulled them in.

The key insight: above 100% means existing customers are your growth engine. Below 100% means you are filling a leaky bucket and need to acquire faster just to stay flat.

Interview Playbook: Answering Open-Ended Product Analytics Questions

This section distills the entire framework into a repeatable approach for answering open-ended product analytics questions in an interview setting. When an interviewer says "we have a product idea — what analytics would you provide to support development and rollout?" they are looking for structured thinking, not a memorized checklist.

The 5-Minute Structure

In a 60-minute interview, you will have approximately 40-45 minutes of working time after introductions. Use the first 3-5 minutes to structure your approach before diving into specifics. Here is the structure:

▶ Step 1: Clarify the Product and the Stage (2 minutes)

Ask questions to understand the product and determine which phase of the framework to focus on. Click a question to see what it really asks and how to use the answer.

▶
"What is the core value proposition for the customer?"What problem does this product solve, and why would someone pay for it?
How to use the answer
Anchors the North Star metric. The value proposition tells you what outcome to measure: revenue saved, time reduced, decisions improved, risk avoided.
▶

How to use the answer
Determines which adoption funnel to build. If user and buyer differ (common in Business-to-Business), you need metrics for both: usage metrics for the user, value metrics for the buyer.

How to use the answer
Tells you where to focus. Pre-launch: emphasize Discovery and Validation. Post-launch: emphasize Rollout analytics (adoption, A/B testing, guardrails). Mature product: emphasize Scale analytics (retention, churn prediction, expansion).

▶ Step 2: State the North Star (1 minute)

Based on the product description, propose a North Star metric. State it clearly:

  • Template: "The single metric I would anchor our analytics around is [X], because it captures the value the product delivers to the customer."
  • Key distinction: The North Star should measure the customer's outcome, not just product usage
  • Example: "Sessions per week" is usage; "time saved per task" or "revenue gained per customer" is an outcome

Then briefly acknowledge the supporting metrics — you will expand on these in the next step.

▶ Step 3: Build the Metrics Tree (5 to 8 minutes)

Walk through the components that drive the North Star. For each branch, explain what it measures, why it matters, and how you would track it. This is the core of your answer. Suggested branches:

  • Branch 1: Adoption funnel (Acquisition → Activation → Engagement → Retention)
  • Branch 2: Model / product performance metrics
  • Branch 3: Revenue and outcome metrics
  • Cross-cutting: Guardrail metrics (things that should not get worse)
▶ Step 4: Propose the Analysis Approach (8 to 10 minutes)

Get specific. Describe the analytics you would actually build — this is where you show technical depth. Match the analysis to the stage:

  • Discovery: Segmentation and market sizing
  • Validation: Baseline measurement, pilot design, and pilot results
  • Rollout: A/B testing, cohort analysis, and adoption tracking
  • Scale: Retention analysis, regression-based targeting models

Name specific techniques and explain why each is appropriate for this situation.

▶ Step 5: Address Risks and Trade-offs (3 to 5 minutes)

Every analytics approach has limitations. Call them out proactively:

  • What are the guardrail metrics?
  • What could go wrong with the measurement approach?
  • What would you do if the data showed ambiguous results?
  • For AI products: model accuracy vs. product adoption are separate concerns
  • The risk of optimizing for a metric that does not actually capture customer value

Acknowledging trade-offs demonstrates maturity and practical experience.

What goes wrong

How to avoid it

Common
What goes wrong

How to avoid it
For every metric, ask: "If this number goes up, does the customer's life get better?" If the answer is unclear, the metric is measuring activity, not value. Include at least one outcome metric (time saved, revenue gained, errors reduced) in every branch of the tree.

  • Baselines for every metric in the metrics tree
  • Pilot design and results: target segments, duration, success criteria, instrumentation scope, and go/iterate/stop recommendation
Because AI/machine learning is involved
Add a model performance branch to the metrics tree: precision, recall, confidence calibration, and suggestion acceptance rate. Set separate thresholds for model accuracy vs. product adoption since they can diverge.
Because user ≠ buyer
The metrics tree needs two outcome branches: usage metrics for the daily user (task completion rate, time-on-task, feature adoption) and value metrics for the buyer (return on investment, cost savings, revenue impact). The North Star should map to the buyer's outcome; leading indicators should track the user's experience.
Key Trade-offs
Leading vs. Lagging indicators Simple metrics vs. Composite scores Aggressive vs. Conservative success thresholds
Phase 3 Build: Instrument and Prepare
Deliverables
  • Event taxonomy (every user action mapped with properties and business context)
  • Dashboard specifications: operational dashboard for the team, executive dashboard for leadership
  • Data quality validation plan (completeness, accuracy, latency checks)
Because instrumentation needs to be built
Prioritize the event taxonomy as the first deliverable. No measurement is possible until tracking is in place. Work with engineering to define events before development begins, not after. Budget 2 to 4 weeks for instrumentation before expecting any analytics output.
Because AI/machine learning is involved
The event taxonomy must capture every AI interaction: suggestion generated, suggestion displayed, suggestion accepted, suggestion rejected, suggestion modified. Log the model confidence score with each event. This is the foundation for every model performance analysis downstream.
Key Trade-offs
Track everything vs. Track what matters Real-time dashboards vs. Batch reporting Build custom vs. Buy analytics tooling
Phase 4 Rollout: Launch and Measure
Deliverables
  • Adoption funnel analysis (Awareness → Contract → Activation → Engagement → Habit)
  • A/B test or pilot design with sample size calculation
  • Guardrail monitoring dashboard with automated alerts
  • Expand / Iterate / Pause decision framework with pre-agreed thresholds
Because AI/machine learning is involved
Track two layers independently: model performance (precision, recall, calibration) and product outcome (acceptance rate, value delivered). A model can be accurate but poorly integrated. Monitor the Aha! moment: the first time a user accepts an AI suggestion and sees a measurable improvement.
Key Trade-offs
Statistical rigor vs. Speed to decision Expand fast vs. Iterate carefully Precision vs. Recall threshold
Phase 5 Scale: Sustain and Grow
Deliverables
  • Retention analysis (N-day curves, cohort analysis, survival analysis)
  • Churn prediction model and early warning system
  • Ideal Customer Profile refinement with segment-level performance data
  • Expansion and upsell analytics (net revenue retention, cross-sell conversion)
  • Product-market fit assessment and kill/pivot decision framework
Because AI/machine learning is involved
Add model degradation monitoring: track precision, recall, calibration, and data drift over time. A declining acceptance rate may signal model degradation, UX changes, or success (easy cases caught, hard cases remain). Always decompose by confidence level and user tenure before concluding the model needs retraining.
Key Trade-offs
Retention investment vs. Acquisition investment Serve existing customers vs. Chase new segments Continue vs. Kill the product

Narrate your reasoning at each step. The interviewer is evaluating your structured thinking, not your ability to list metrics.

Usage Patterns

What users actually do in the product: which features they use, how often they come back, and where they spend their time.

Examples
  • 70% of users visit the dashboard daily but only 15% use the export feature
  • Average session length is 12 minutes, with most time spent on the review screen
  • Users who complete onboarding within 3 days retain at 2x the rate of those who don't
Conversion Funnels

The step by step path users take to complete a goal. Each step has a drop off rate that shows where users stop and where demand exists but something is blocking completion.

Examples
  • Signup > onboarding > first action > repeat action: 1,000 > 600 > 200 > 80
  • 40% drop off between signup and onboarding means something in onboarding is failing
  • These "before" numbers become the baselines you measure improvement against after launch
Segment Behavior

How different groups of customers use the product differently. Segments that look the same on paper can have completely different usage, retention, and value.

Examples
  • Enterprise customers log in 5x per week, mid market logs in 2x per week
  • CDI teams spend 80% of time on the review screen, coders spend 60% on the lookup screen
  • Customers in the top segment retain at 90% after 30 days, bottom segment retains at 40%
Baselines

The "before" numbers you measure against after launch. Baselines tell you what performance looks like today so you can prove whether the new product actually moved the needle.

Examples
  • Current average time to complete a coding review is 45 minutes per chart
  • Today's conversion rate from signup to first completed action is 22%
  • Existing customers contact support an average of 3.2 times per month for this workflow
Retention Curves

A chart that shows what percentage of customers are still active over time. The shape tells you whether customers stick around or drop off quickly after signing up.

Examples
  • 60% of customers are still active after 30 days, but only 35% after 90 days
  • A flat curve after week 4 means the customers who stay past that point tend to stay long term
  • A curve that keeps dropping without flattening signals the product is not delivering lasting value
Revenue per Segment

How much money each customer group generates. Comparing this across segments shows which groups are most valuable and where the biggest growth opportunity is.

Examples
  • Enterprise customers generate $50K per year, mid market generates $12K per year
  • The top 20% of customers account for 70% of total revenue
  • A segment with high usage but low revenue may need a pricing change, not a product change
Market Size

Total potential customers who could adopt. This number becomes the denominator in every adoption and penetration calculation you run.

Examples
  • 500 hospitals in the addressable market, 50 adopt = 10% adoption rate
  • 500 hospitals × 50,000 charts/month = ceiling for total impact
  • Sizing segments within the total tells you which cohorts to prioritize first
Target Segment

The group of customers the product is built for first. Defines who shows up in your earliest cohorts and whose behavior you optimize around.

Examples
  • CDI teams vs. coders = entirely different user journeys and success signals
  • Tells you which workflows to baseline before launch
  • Determines which cohorts to instrument from day one
Adoption Denominator

The "out of how many" in every adoption rate calculation. Without it, adoption numbers are raw counts with no context.

Examples
  • Adoption rate = activated customers / total eligible customers
  • 50 activated out of 200 eligible = 25%
  • Denominator shifts as you expand segments, so define it clearly in Discovery
Cohorts

Groups of users defined by a shared characteristic (when they started, which segment they belong to). You track each group over time to compare performance and detect trends.

Examples
  • By time: January vs. February activations — shows if onboarding is improving
  • By segment: CDI teams vs. coders — shows which group gets more value
  • By size: large hospitals vs. small clinics — shows if the product scales
Strategic Thesis

The reason this product should exist. Determines what your primary success metric should be.

Thesis → Primary Metric
  • "Replaces manual chart review" → time saved per chart
  • "Captures missed revenue" → incremental revenue per customer
  • "Reduces compliance risk" → error rate reduction
Segment Definitions

Groups of customers sorted by shared characteristics that predict how they will use the product. Not demographics, but behavior and need based groupings.

Examples
  • Enterprise customers (500+ employees) vs mid-market (50-500)
  • High-frequency users vs occasional users
  • Customers in regulated industries vs unregulated

Market Sizing Numbers

The estimated dollar value or customer count of the total opportunity. Usually expressed as Total Addressable Market (everyone who could theoretically buy) and Serviceable Addressable Market (the realistic portion the product can reach).

Examples
  • Total Addressable Market of $2 billion based on industry reports
  • Serviceable Addressable Market of $200 million after filtering for geography and company size
  • Bottom up estimate of 15,000 target organizations multiplied by $12,000 average annual spend
Conversion Gap Analysis

A measurement of where users start an action but fail to complete it. Each gap represents a point where the product is losing potential value.

Examples
  • 38% of users view a product page but never add to cart
  • 60% of signups never complete onboarding
  • 25% of users who start a workflow abandon it at the payment step
Competitive Landscape

A map of who else solves the same problem and how. Includes direct competitors, indirect alternatives, and manual workarounds customers currently use.

Examples
  • Three direct competitors offering similar SaaS products at different price points
  • Customers using spreadsheets and email as a manual workaround
  • In house teams building custom solutions instead of buying software
Segment Prioritization

A ranked list of customer segments ordered by which ones the product should target first. Ranked by a combination of need, readiness to adopt, and revenue potential.

Examples
  • Enterprise healthcare companies ranked first because they have the sharpest pain and largest budgets
  • Mid-market retail ranked second because they are ready to adopt but have smaller deal sizes
  • Small businesses ranked last because onboarding costs exceed their lifetime value
Segment Definitions and Market Sizing

The combination of customer groupings and the estimated value of each group. Together, they answer who are the customers and how much is each group worth?

Examples
  • Enterprise segment representing $150 million of the total $200 million serviceable market
  • Mid-market segment with 8,000 potential accounts at $6,000 average deal size
  • A high-need niche segment that is small in count but has the highest willingness to pay
Competitive Whitespace

The portion of the market where no existing solution adequately serves customer needs. These are the gaps between what competitors offer and what customers actually want.

Examples
  • Competitors serve large enterprises but nobody targets mid-market with a simplified product
  • Existing tools handle English language content but no solution covers multilingual workflows
  • Manual processes exist for compliance reporting but no automated solution has been built
North Star Metric

The single number that best captures whether the product is delivering value to customers. It should reflect a real customer outcome, not just product usage, and should be something the team can measure and influence.

Examples
  • Revenue recovered per customer per year (for a billing accuracy product)
  • Time from submission to resolution in hours (for a claims processing tool)
  • Percentage of recommendations accepted by the end user (for an AI suggestion engine)
Metrics Tree

A visual breakdown of the North Star metric into its component parts. Each branch represents a driver that the team can measure and act on. Think of it as a formula where improving any branch improves the overall number.

Examples
  • North Star "Revenue per Customer" breaks into Volume x Rate x Value per Unit
  • "Acceptance rate" breaks into suggestions shown, suggestions seen by user, suggestions acted on
  • Each leaf metric has a current baseline and a target threshold
Baseline Measurements

The "before" numbers that capture how things perform today, before the new product launches. These are the reference points that let the team prove whether the product actually improved anything.

Examples
  • Average task completion time is currently 45 minutes (measured over 6 weeks)
  • Current conversion rate from trial to paid is 12% across all segments
  • Customer satisfaction score averages 3.2 out of 5 based on last quarter survey data
Success Thresholds

The specific numbers the team agrees on in advance that define whether the product is working. Set before launch so emotions and sunk costs do not influence the decision later.

Examples
  • Activation rate must exceed 60% within 30 days of launch
  • Net revenue retention must be above 110% by the end of the first year
  • Customer reported time savings must be at least 20% compared to baseline
Kill/Pivot Thresholds

Pre agreed numbers that trigger a formal decision to stop or fundamentally change the product. Set during planning specifically so the team has an objective basis for the hardest conversations.

Examples
  • If Week 4 retention stays below 10% across all segments, trigger a formal review
  • If adoption rate is below 25% after 90 days in the primary segment, escalate to leadership
  • If the cost to acquire a customer exceeds twice the first year revenue, pause expansion
North Star Metric Definition

The formal written specification of the North Star metric including exactly how it is calculated, what time period it covers, and what entity it measures. This definition stays consistent from Validation through Scale.

Examples
  • "Revenue Impact: dollars recovered per customer per year" measured as the sum of all billing corrections attributed to the product
  • "Time to Resolution: hours from ticket creation to closure" calculated as the median across all tickets in the measurement window
  • "Acceptance Rate: percentage of AI suggestions acted on by users within 24 hours of display"
Event Taxonomy

A structured catalog of every user action the product tracks. Each event has a name, properties (metadata like user role or account size), and a description of when it fires. This is the foundation for all analytics.

Examples
  • "suggestion_displayed" fires when the AI shows a recommendation to the user, with properties for model confidence score and suggestion type
  • "report_exported" fires when a user downloads a report, with properties for report type, format, and user role
  • "onboarding_step_completed" fires at each step of the setup wizard, with properties for step number and time spent
Dashboards

Pre built visual reports that show key metrics in real time. Usually two types: an operational dashboard for the product team (detailed, updated frequently) and an executive dashboard for leadership (high level, focused on outcomes).

Examples
  • Operational dashboard showing daily active users, activation funnel, and error rates updated every hour
  • Executive dashboard showing North Star metric trend, revenue impact, and adoption rate by segment updated weekly
  • Alert dashboard that highlights any guardrail metric that has crossed its threshold
Data Quality Checks

Automated tests that verify the tracking data is complete, accurate, and arriving on time. These catch instrumentation bugs before they corrupt the metrics everyone relies on.

Examples
  • Completeness check: verify that every event has all required properties filled in (no null values in mandatory fields)
  • Volume check: compare actual event counts to expected ranges and flag anything 10x higher or lower than normal
  • Latency check: confirm that events arrive in the data warehouse within the expected time window (for example, under 15 minutes)
Event Properties and Segmentation

The metadata attached to every tracked event that allows the analyst to filter and group data by customer type. Without these properties, the analyst can only see overall averages and cannot identify which segments are succeeding or failing.

Examples
  • Customer tier property (enterprise, mid-market, small business) attached to every event
  • User role property (admin, end user, viewer) enabling analysis of who uses which features
  • Account size or contract value property allowing revenue weighted analysis of product usage
Model Performance Logging

For AI products, the practice of recording detailed information about every prediction or suggestion the model makes. This data is essential for detecting when the model starts performing worse over time.

Examples
  • Logging the model confidence score alongside every suggestion so the team can track whether confidence correlates with user acceptance
  • Recording the input features used for each prediction so the team can detect when the incoming data distribution changes (data drift)
  • Capturing user feedback (accepted, rejected, modified) on every AI output to measure real world accuracy over time
Adoption Funnel Data

Measurements of how users progress through the stages of starting to use the product, from first hearing about it to becoming a regular user. Each stage has a conversion rate showing what percentage of users move forward.

Examples
  • Awareness to Contract: 40% of eligible accounts signed up for the product
  • Contract to Activation: 65% of signed accounts completed the first meaningful action within 30 days
  • Activation to Habit: 30% of activated users returned and used the product at least 3 times per week
A/B Test Results

The outcomes of controlled experiments where different groups of users saw different versions of the product. The results show which version performed better on specific metrics.

Examples
  • Onboarding flow A produced 40% higher activation than flow B with 95% statistical confidence
  • Showing AI suggestions inline increased acceptance rate by 18% compared to showing them in a sidebar
  • Sending a "getting started" email on day 2 improved Week 1 retention by 12 percentage points
Segment Level Performance

A breakdown of how the product is performing separately for each customer group. The overall average often hides the fact that the product works well for some segments and poorly for others.

Examples
  • Enterprise customers accept AI suggestions at 52% while mid-market customers accept at only 14%
  • Healthcare accounts retain at 85% after 90 days while retail accounts retain at just 40%
  • Users who completed onboarding in the first week activate 3x more features than those who took longer
Guardrail Metric Baselines

The starting values for safety metrics that the team monitors to make sure the product is not causing unintended harm. These protect against situations where the main metrics look good but something else is going wrong.

Examples
  • False positive rate baseline of 15% measured before launch, with a threshold of 20% that triggers an alert
  • Customer support ticket volume baseline of 50 per week, monitored for spikes that could indicate a usability problem
  • System response time baseline of 200 milliseconds, with a guardrail that pages engineering if it exceeds 500 milliseconds
Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)

A data driven description of the type of customer most likely to retain, expand, and generate the highest lifetime value. Built from actual retention, usage, and revenue data rather than assumptions. The ICP is used by sales and marketing to prioritize which prospects to pursue and by product teams to decide which segments to build for next.

Examples
  • For SmarterDx: academic medical centers with 200+ beds, dedicated coding teams, and existing workflow integration retain at 2x the rate of smaller hospitals
  • The ICP updates quarterly as more retention data accumulates and early assumptions get validated or overturned
  • Prospects are scored against ICP characteristics so the sales team focuses on the accounts most likely to succeed
Ideal Customer Profile Data

Real performance data that reveals which types of customers get the most value from the product. Built from actual retention, usage, and revenue data rather than assumptions.

Examples
  • Customers with 200+ employees in healthcare retain at 90% and expand usage by 40% annually
  • Accounts that complete onboarding within 7 days have 3x higher lifetime value than those that take longer
  • Companies that integrate the product with their existing workflow tools show 60% higher engagement than standalone users
Unserved Segment Data

Information about customer groups that want the product but cannot use it effectively in its current form. These represent opportunities for new products or major feature investments.

Examples
  • Mid-market customers churn at 45% because the workflow requires a dedicated admin they cannot afford
  • International customers cannot use the product because it only supports English language content
  • Solo practitioners need the core functionality but the pricing model is designed for teams of 10+
Feature Adoption Heatmaps

A visual representation of which product features are used heavily, which are barely touched, and which correlate with long term retention. Tells the team where to invest and what to sunset.

Examples
  • Dashboard customization is used by 80% of retained users but only 15% of churned users, suggesting it drives value
  • The export to PDF feature has less than 2% usage across all segments and could be a candidate for removal
  • The collaboration features show high initial exploration but low repeat usage, suggesting the experience needs improvement
Model Performance Trends

For AI products, the tracked changes in how well the model performs over weeks and months. Helps the team detect when the model needs retraining or when changes in the real world are affecting predictions.

Examples
  • Accuracy declining from 92% to 84% over 3 months as the underlying data distribution shifts
  • Confidence scores trending upward while acceptance rates trend downward, indicating the model is becoming overconfident
  • Precision stable at 88% for the primary use case but dropping to 65% for a newly added use case
Kill/Pivot Outcomes

The documented results of formal decisions to continue, fundamentally change, or discontinue a product. This institutional memory helps future teams make better decisions.

Examples
  • Product A was killed after 6 months because retention never exceeded 8% in any segment despite three iteration cycles
  • Product B pivoted from a consumer app to a B2B tool after Scale data showed enterprise users retained at 5x the rate of individual users
  • Product C was continued with a narrowed focus on healthcare after Scale revealed that was the only segment with strong product-market fit signals
Benchmark Library

A collection of internal performance data from past products that gives future teams realistic reference points. Replaces generic industry averages with company specific numbers.

Examples
  • Average Week 4 retention across company products is 35% with a range of 20% to 55%
  • Typical trial to paid conversion rate is 15% for self serve and 40% for sales assisted accounts
  • AI suggestion acceptance rates in the company range from 25% to 60% depending on the use case complexity
Warning Signals

Early indicators during Discovery that suggest the product idea may not be viable. Identifying these before committing engineering resources saves the team from investing in something the data does not support.

Examples
  • The total addressable market is too small to sustain a business after filtering for realistic adoption rates
  • Every segment of the market already has a well established solution and no identifiable whitespace exists
  • The Product Manager cannot articulate the problem clearly or names no conditions that would kill the idea
  • Internal data shows the conversion gap is small, meaning users are already completing the action successfully
Instrumentation

The process of adding tracking code to a product so that user actions are recorded as data. Without instrumentation, the team has no way to measure whether the product is working once it launches.

Examples
  • Adding code that records when a user clicks "Accept Suggestion" so the team can calculate acceptance rate
  • Tagging each event with properties like customer tier and user role so the data can be filtered by segment later
  • Setting up a tracking event for each step of the onboarding flow so the team can see where users drop off

Product-Market Fit

A state where a product deeply satisfies genuine market demand — customers actively seek it out, retention is strong across diverse segments, and the product works consistently as the user base grows. Product-market fit is verified through repeated cohort behavior, not a single metric, and is the north star of product development.

Key indicators: Retention curves flatten (good) not decline (bad) across cohorts, the North Star metric continues improving as users grow, and the product works in multiple segments not just cherry-picked early adopters.

Activation Rate

The percentage of new users who complete a key action that signals they have experienced the product's core value. This is a leading indicator because it predicts whether users will stick around and eventually generate revenue.

Examples
  • A coding review tool: the user submits their first code suggestion within 7 days of signing up
  • A clinical documentation tool: the coder accepts their first AI suggested diagnosis code
  • A project management tool: the user creates their first project and invites a teammate
Session Frequency

How often users return to the product within a given time period. This is a leading indicator because frequent usage predicts retention, and retention predicts revenue.

Examples
  • Users log in an average of 4 times per week
  • Daily active users divided by monthly active users (DAU/MAU ratio)
  • Median time between sessions is less than 48 hours
Revenue

The total income generated by the product over a given period. This is a lagging indicator because it only moves after customers have already adopted, used, and decided to pay for the product.

Examples
  • Monthly recurring revenue (MRR) from subscriptions
  • Annual contract value (ACV) from enterprise deals
  • Revenue per customer per year
Retention

The percentage of users who continue using the product over time. This is a lagging indicator because it takes weeks or months to confirm whether users are actually sticking around after their initial experience.

Examples
  • Week 4 retention: 35% of users who signed up 4 weeks ago are still active
  • Month 3 retention: 60% of enterprise accounts renew after the first quarter
  • Cohort retention curve flattens at 25% after 8 weeks
Customer Lifetime Value

The total revenue a single customer is expected to generate over their entire relationship with the product. This is one of the slowest lagging indicators because it requires months or years of data to calculate accurately.

Examples
  • Average customer stays 3 years at $40K per year = $120K lifetime value
  • Enterprise customers have 2x the lifetime value of mid-market due to longer contracts
  • Customers who activate within 7 days have 40% higher lifetime value than those who take longer
Revenue per Customer

The total revenue generated by a single customer over a defined time period. This is a lagging indicator because it only moves after customers have adopted the product, used it repeatedly, and continued paying. It confirms whether the product is delivering enough value to sustain the business model.

Examples
  • A clinical AI tool: $15K per hospital per quarter in recovered revenue from corrected claims
  • A SaaS platform: $2,400 per customer per year from subscription fees
  • A coding efficiency tool: $8K per engineering team per quarter in saved developer hours
False Positive Rate

The percentage of times the product flags something as positive (e.g., a diagnosis, a risk, a recommendation) when it is actually incorrect. This is a guardrail metric because a high false positive rate erodes user trust and creates extra work, even if the product is catching real issues.

Examples
  • A clinical AI tool suggests a diagnosis code that the coder rejects because it doesn't apply to the patient
  • A fraud detection system flags a legitimate transaction, forcing a manual review
  • A code review tool flags valid code as a bug, causing developers to waste time investigating
Time on Task

The amount of time it takes a user to complete a specific workflow or action within the product. This is a guardrail metric because the product should reduce time on task, not increase it. If users are spending more time after adoption, the product may be adding friction instead of removing it.

Examples
  • A clinical AI tool: time to review and finalize a patient chart drops from 12 minutes to 7 minutes
  • A coding tool: time to complete a code review drops from 45 minutes to 20 minutes
  • A data platform: time to generate a monthly report drops from 4 hours to 30 minutes
Satisfaction Scores

Quantitative measures of how users feel about the product, typically captured through NPS (Net Promoter Score) or CSAT (Customer Satisfaction) surveys. These are lagging indicators because satisfaction only reflects value after the user has experienced the product over time.

Examples
  • NPS: "On a scale of 0 to 10, how likely are you to recommend this product?" Score above 50 is excellent.
  • CSAT: "How satisfied are you with this feature?" measured on a 1 to 5 scale after key interactions
  • Post-pilot survey: 80% of pilot users rate the product 4 or 5 out of 5
Leading Indicator

A metric that moves before the North Star does. Leading indicators predict future success or failure, giving the team time to react. If leading indicators are strong but the North Star hasn't moved yet, the product is likely on track. If they're weak, trouble is coming even if current numbers look fine.

Examples
  • Activation rate: users who complete the key action early are more likely to retain and generate revenue later
  • Feature adoption rate: high early usage of a new feature predicts it will drive the North Star
  • Time to value: users who reach the "aha moment" faster have higher lifetime value
Lagging Indicator

A metric that moves after the North Star has already changed. Lagging indicators confirm whether the product actually delivered value, but by the time they move, it's too late to change course. That's why the team monitors leading indicators day to day and uses lagging indicators to validate the overall trend.

Examples
  • Revenue per customer: only increases after users have adopted, used, and continued paying
  • Churn rate: only visible weeks or months after the experience that caused the user to leave
  • Customer lifetime value: takes months or years of data to calculate accurately
Model Latency

The time between when the system sends data to the AI model and when it returns a prediction. High latency slows down the user workflow and can cause users to bypass the feature entirely.

Examples
  • SmarterDx: time from chart submission to AI code suggestions appearing on screen
  • Target: under 2 seconds for real time suggestions, under 30 seconds for batch processing
Error Rates

The percentage of requests or actions that fail due to system errors, timeouts, or unexpected behavior. High error rates erode user trust and inflate drop off at every stage of the funnel.

Examples
  • API errors: percentage of requests returning 4xx or 5xx status codes
  • Model errors: percentage of charts where the AI fails to return any suggestions
Event Volume

The total count of tracked events firing per time period. Monitored against expected baselines from the event taxonomy to catch tracking bugs: a sudden spike or drop signals an instrumentation problem, not a usage change.

Examples
  • Expected: 500 suggestion_displayed events per hospital per day
  • Red flag: volume drops to zero after a deploy (broken tracking) or jumps 10x (double firing bug)
API Uptime

The percentage of time the product's API is available and responding to requests. Downtime directly blocks users from accessing the product and corrupts adoption metrics because users who wanted to use the product could not.

Examples
  • Target: 99.9% uptime (less than 8.7 hours of downtime per year)
  • SmarterDx: if the AI suggestion service is down, coders revert to manual coding and adoption metrics drop
Suggestion Acceptance Rate

The percentage of AI generated suggestions that users accept. Measures whether the model output is useful enough for users to trust and act on. A low acceptance rate means the model is generating noise, not value.

Examples
  • Formula: suggestions accepted / suggestions displayed × 100
  • SmarterDx: percentage of AI suggested diagnosis codes that coders accept without modification
Funnel Conversion

The percentage of users who complete each step in a sequence of actions. Measures where users drop off in the journey and identifies the biggest opportunities to improve adoption.

Examples
  • SmarterDx: chart opened → suggestions viewed → suggestion accepted → claim submitted
  • A 40% drop between suggestions viewed and suggestion accepted signals a trust or relevance problem
Time to First Accepted Suggestion

The elapsed time from a user's first login to the moment they accept their first AI suggestion. A leading indicator of activation: shorter time means the product delivers value faster and users are more likely to retain.

Examples
  • SmarterDx: time from coder's first login to accepting their first AI suggested diagnosis code
  • Target: within the first session; if it takes multiple sessions, the onboarding flow needs work
Denied Claims Caught

The number of claims that would have been denied but were corrected before submission thanks to the AI suggestions. This is the direct measure of value delivered to the hospital: each caught denial represents recovered revenue and reduced rework.

Examples
  • Formula: claims flagged by AI that were corrected before submission and subsequently paid
  • SmarterDx: denied claims caught per hospital per month is a candidate North Star metric
Revenue Recovered

The dollar value of claims that would have been denied or underpaid but were corrected using AI suggestions before submission. Translates the product's clinical accuracy into a financial outcome that leadership cares about.

Examples
  • Formula: denied claims caught × average claim value
  • SmarterDx: if AI catches 200 denied claims per hospital per month at an average of $1,500 per claim, revenue recovered is $300,000 per hospital per month
Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)

The smallest improvement in a metric that an experiment is designed to reliably detect. Set before the experiment begins based on what would be a meaningful business outcome. If the true effect is smaller than the MDE, the experiment will not have enough statistical power to detect it.

Examples
  • If baseline suggestion acceptance rate is 40%, an MDE of 5 percentage points means the experiment is powered to detect a lift from 40% to 45%
  • SmarterDx: "we need to detect at least a 5% improvement in acceptance rate to justify expanding to the next hospital tier"
Sequential Testing

A statistical method that allows checking experiment results at multiple points during the test without inflating false positive rates. Uses adjusted significance thresholds at each interim check so that the overall error rate stays at the target level (typically 5%).

Examples
  • Standard A/B testing at 5% significance checked 4 times during the test inflates the real false positive rate to ~14%. Sequential testing corrects for this
  • SmarterDx: if leadership asks for a progress update at week 2 of a 6 week experiment, sequential testing lets you report interim results without compromising the final conclusion
Difference in Differences (DiD)

An observational method for estimating causal impact when randomization is not possible. Compares the change in outcomes over time between a treatment group and a comparison group. The key assumption is that both groups would have followed the same trend without the intervention (parallel trends assumption).

Examples
  • Formula: (Treatment After - Treatment Before) - (Control After - Control Before)
  • SmarterDx: compare denied claims caught at hospitals that adopted AI coding in Q1 versus hospitals that adopted in Q2, using the Q2 hospitals as the control group during Q1
Opportunity Cost

The value of the best alternative use of the same resources (engineering time, budget, team focus). When deciding whether to continue investing in a product, the relevant question is not "how much have we already spent?" (sunk cost) but "what else could this team build instead?" Framing decisions around opportunity cost removes emotional attachment and forces a comparison between the current product and the next best option.

Examples
  • For SmarterDx: if the AI coding tool is underperforming, the same team could build a payer denial prediction model or an AI audit tool. Comparing the expected ROI of each path makes the decision data driven
  • A team spending 6 months iterating on a feature with flat metrics could instead ship a new feature that addresses an unserved segment with strong demand signals
  • Opportunity cost framing helps leadership accept a stop recommendation because the conversation shifts from "we failed" to "we found a better use of these resources"
Data Drift

A shift in the distribution of incoming data compared to what the model was originally trained on. As the customer base changes (new segments, new use cases, seasonal patterns), the data the model sees in production starts to look different from the data it learned from. Drift is a leading indicator of accuracy degradation because the model may still appear to work but is increasingly making predictions on data it was not designed for.

Examples
  • For SmarterDx: adding radiology departments introduces coding patterns the model has never seen, causing drift even if inpatient performance remains stable
  • Measured using Population Stability Index (PSI): a score above 0.25 typically indicates significant drift that warrants investigation
  • Not all drift is harmful: seasonal patterns or legitimate business changes can shift distributions without degrading accuracy. Always investigate the cause before triggering retraining
Retention Curve

A chart that plots the percentage of users who remain active over time after their first use of the product. The x axis is time (days, weeks, or months since activation) and the y axis is the percentage still active. The shape of the curve reveals whether the product has lasting value or just novelty appeal.

Examples
  • A healthy curve drops steeply in the first week then flattens, meaning users who survive the first week tend to stay permanently
  • A declining curve that never flattens means users keep leaving over time and the product has not found a sticky use case
  • Overlaying curves from different cohorts shows whether the product is improving (later cohorts flatten higher) or degrading (later cohorts flatten lower)
Kaplan Meier Survival Analysis

A statistical method for estimating how long users remain active when different groups have been observed for different lengths of time. Standard retention curves unfairly penalize newer cohorts because they have not had enough time to prove they will retain. Kaplan Meier adjusts for this by accounting for users who are still active but have not yet reached the same time point as older users (called censored observations).

Examples
  • Comparing a 90 day old cohort with a 30 day old cohort: Kaplan Meier gives a fair comparison even though the newer cohort has less history
  • Estimating median customer lifetime for LTV calculations when many customers are still active and have not churned yet
  • For SmarterDx: comparing retention between hospital tiers that onboarded months apart without waiting for the newer hospitals to catch up
Net Revenue Retention (NRR)

The percentage of revenue retained from existing customers over a given period, including expansion (upsells, more seats) and subtracting churn and contraction. NRR above 100% means the business grows even without acquiring a single new customer. This is considered the gold standard B2B metric for product market fit at scale.

Examples
  • Formula: (Starting Revenue + Expansion - Churn - Contraction) / Starting Revenue
  • NRR of 120% means existing customers generated 20% more revenue this period than last, even after accounting for customers who left
  • For SmarterDx: a hospital starts with 20 coders on the platform and expands to 35 while none churn, signaling the product spreads organically within accounts
Sunk Cost Bias

The tendency to continue investing in a product or initiative because of the time, money, or effort already spent, rather than evaluating it based on future expected value. In product decisions, sunk cost bias leads teams to expand a product that the data says should pause, because stopping feels like admitting the prior investment was wasted.

Examples
  • A team spent 6 months building an AI feature but adoption is flat. The instinct is to keep going because "we've already invested so much"
  • A hospital has trained 50 coders on a new tool but the data shows no improvement in outcomes. Leadership resists pausing because of the training investment
  • Pre agreed thresholds remove this bias by making the expand/iterate/pause decision automatic based on data, not feelings about past effort
Escalation Protocol

A pre agreed plan that defines exactly what happens when a guardrail metric breaches its threshold. Specifies who gets notified, what decisions they are authorized to make (pause rollout, restrict to specific segments, roll back), and the timeline for response. Without one, the decision to pause becomes political instead of data driven.

Examples
  • If false positive rate exceeds 20% at any hospital, the analyst notifies the PM within 24 hours and that hospital is paused until root cause is identified
  • If NPS drops more than 5 points in any segment, the product team holds an emergency review within 48 hours
  • If time on task increases more than 10% from baseline, engineering is notified to investigate whether a model update or UI change caused the regression
Workaround Behavior

The actions users take to solve a problem when no dedicated solution exists. Identifying workarounds in usage data reveals the real pain points and shows what the product needs to replace.

Examples
  • Hospital coders manually re-reviewing charts in a spreadsheet instead of using a dedicated tool
  • Users exporting data to Excel because the product dashboard doesn't support the analysis they need
  • Teams using email threads to track tasks because the project management tool is too rigid
Drop Off Points

The specific moments in a user journey where people stop engaging or abandon a process. These signal friction, confusion, or lack of value and are key indicators of where a product needs improvement.

Examples
  • 80% of users start onboarding but only 40% complete it, so the drop off is in the onboarding flow
  • Coders open AI suggested codes but 60% dismiss them without reviewing, so the drop off is at the review step
  • Users visit the pricing page but don't convert, so the drop off is at the purchase decision
Revenue Ceiling

The maximum revenue a product can generate based on the total addressable market and current pricing. This sets the upper bound for projections and helps determine whether the opportunity justifies the investment.

Examples
  • If there are 500 hospitals in your segment and each pays $100K/year, the revenue ceiling is $50M
  • If the average revenue per customer is $20K and you can realistically reach 200 customers, the ceiling is $4M
  • If bottom up analysis shows a $10M ceiling but leadership expects $50M, the assumptions need revisiting
ROI Calculations

Return on investment analysis that compares the expected value a product delivers against the cost to build and maintain it. Market size provides the denominator because you need to know the total opportunity to calculate whether the return justifies the spend.

Examples
  • If development costs $2M and the product captures $500K/year from 50 hospitals, ROI break even is 4 years
  • If each hospital recovers $200K in denied claims and the product costs $50K/year, the ROI per customer is 4x
  • Without market size, you can't calculate total ROI because you don't know how many customers the return applies to
Churn Patterns

The trends in how and when customers stop using or paying for a product. Analyzing churn by segment reveals which groups are a poor fit and helps focus targeting on segments that retain well.

Examples
  • Small hospitals churn at 40% after 6 months while large hospitals churn at 10%, signaling that small hospitals are a poor fit
  • Customers who don't activate within the first 2 weeks churn at 3x the rate of those who do
  • Churn spikes after contract renewal, suggesting the product isn't delivering enough ongoing value to justify the cost
Conversion Rate

The percentage of users who complete a desired action out of the total who started. In Discovery, the existing conversion rate becomes the baseline that Rollout A/B tests measure improvement against.

Examples
  • If 1,000 coders see AI suggested codes and 300 accept them, the conversion rate is 30%
  • If the current manual review process catches 60% of missed codes, that's the baseline the new product needs to beat
  • A/B tests in Rollout compare the new conversion rate against this baseline to measure lift
Sample Size

The number of users or observations needed to detect a meaningful difference in an experiment. Market size determines whether you have enough users to run valid A/B tests with statistical significance.

Examples
  • To detect a 5% improvement in acceptance rate with 80% power, you might need 1,600 users per group
  • If your total market is 200 hospitals, you may not have enough for a traditional A/B test and need alternative methods
  • Small markets may require sequential testing or pre/post analysis instead of randomized experiments
Growth Targets

The specific adoption, revenue, or expansion goals set for the Scale phase. Market size sets the ceiling for these targets because you cannot grow beyond what the market supports.

Examples
  • If the addressable market is 500 hospitals and you have 50, a realistic growth target might be 150 in year one, not 600
  • Revenue growth targets are bounded by market size times average revenue per customer
  • Growth targets inform hiring, infrastructure, and investment decisions in Scale
Rollout Order

The sequence in which different customer segments receive the product. Segments that showed the strongest retention and need in Discovery launch first because they are most likely to succeed and provide clean signal for experiments.

Examples
  • Large teaching hospitals launch first because retention curves show they get the most value
  • Start with the segment where the problem is most acute to maximize early adoption signal
  • Avoid launching to poor fit segments early because their churn will distort your metrics
Segment Level Success Thresholds

Different success criteria for different customer segments, because each group may have different baselines, behaviors, and value expectations. A single threshold across all segments can mask poor performance in one group or set unrealistic targets for another.

Examples
  • Large hospitals might need 80% acceptance rate to justify the cost while small hospitals need 90% because their volume is lower
  • Enterprise customers might have a 60 day activation window while SMBs need to activate in 14 days
  • Setting one threshold for all segments hides that enterprise is thriving while SMB is failing
Expansion Targets

Goals for growing revenue and adoption beyond the initial launch segments. Target segment data from Discovery tells you which new segments to expand into and in what order based on their expected fit.

Examples
  • After succeeding with large teaching hospitals, expand to mid size community hospitals that showed moderate retention
  • Expansion targets are set based on which segments had the next strongest signal in Discovery
  • Net revenue retention above 100% signals that expansion within existing customers is working
Experiment Success Criteria

The pre defined outcomes that an A/B test or pilot must achieve to be considered successful. The strategic thesis determines these criteria because it defines what the product is supposed to improve.

Examples
  • If the thesis is "AI catches more missed codes," the success criterion is a statistically significant increase in denied claims caught
  • If the thesis is "faster review time," the success criterion is a measurable decrease in time per chart
  • Without clear criteria before the experiment, teams rationalize any result as success
Competitor Analysis

A structured review of existing solutions in the market to understand what alternatives customers have, what those alternatives do well, and where they fall short. In Discovery, this tells you what benchmarks you need to beat and whether there is room for a new product.

Examples
  • Reviewing competing coding tools to see what accuracy rates and workflow integrations they offer hospitals
  • Comparing pricing models across alternatives to understand what customers are willing to pay
  • Identifying which competitor features get the most complaints in reviews or support forums to find weaknesses
Market Gap Research

Research focused on identifying unmet needs that current solutions do not address. Unlike competitor analysis which evaluates existing products, market gap research looks for what is missing entirely, giving you the whitespace where a new product can win.

Examples
  • Discovering that no existing coding tool provides real time suggestions during chart review, only retrospective audits
  • Finding through customer interviews that small hospitals are completely underserved because competitors only target large systems
  • Identifying that existing tools flag errors but don't explain why, leaving coders without the context to act on suggestions
Adoption Funnel Analysis

A step by step measurement of how users move through the product, from first exposure to repeated use. At each stage you measure how many users advance and how many drop off, so you can pinpoint exactly where the product loses people and focus fixes there before expanding to more users.

Why We Need This in Rollout
  • Tells you whether users are actually reaching the value moment, not just signing up
  • Identifies the biggest drop off point so the team knows where to invest next
  • For SmarterDx: measures how many coders go from seeing AI suggestions to accepting their first one to using the tool daily
Segment Performance Analysis

A comparison of how different user groups experience the product. Aggregate numbers hide the real story because one segment may be thriving while another is failing. This analysis breaks results down by group so you can see who the product works for, who it does not, and why.

Why We Need This in Rollout
  • Prevents the team from celebrating an average that masks a struggling segment
  • Informs which segments to expand to next and which need fixes first
  • For SmarterDx: compares adoption and accuracy across hospital tiers, departments, and coder experience levels
Experiment Design and Impact Measurement

The process of running controlled tests (like A/B tests) to prove that changes to the product actually cause improvements, not just correlate with them. You design the experiment before making changes, run it on a subset of users, and measure the difference against a control group.

Why We Need This in Rollout
  • Without experiments, you cannot tell if a metric improved because of your change or because of something else
  • Gives the team confidence to scale what works and stop what does not
  • For SmarterDx: testing whether a redesigned suggestion panel increases acceptance rate vs the current version
Guardrail Metrics Monitoring

Ongoing tracking of metrics that protect against unintended harm. While success metrics tell you if the product is working, guardrail metrics tell you if the product is breaking something else in the process. You set thresholds in advance and monitor them continuously so you can catch problems before they become serious.

Why We Need This in Rollout
  • A product can hit its success metrics while silently making another part of the workflow worse
  • Guardrails are the early warning system that triggers a pause before damage spreads
  • For SmarterDx: monitoring false positive rate and time on task to make sure AI suggestions are not slowing coders down or flooding them with bad codes
Expand, Iterate, or Pause Recommendation

The final deliverable of Rollout where the analyst synthesizes all the data (funnel, segments, experiments, guardrails) into a clear recommendation. Expand means the data supports rolling out to more users. Iterate means the product works but needs specific fixes before expanding. Pause means the data shows fundamental problems that need to be resolved first.

Why We Need This in Rollout
  • Forces a data driven decision instead of letting momentum or opinion drive the next step
  • Ties every piece of Rollout analysis together into one actionable output
  • For SmarterDx: recommending expand to mid tier hospitals because adoption and accuracy are strong, but iterate on small clinics where activation is lagging
Retention and Expansion Analysis

A study of whether users who started using the product during Rollout are still using it over time, and whether existing customers are growing their usage (adding more users, departments, or use cases). Retention tells you the product has lasting value. Expansion tells you the product is valuable enough that customers want more of it without being asked.

Why We Need This in Scale
  • If users are churning, acquiring new ones just fills a leaky bucket. Retention must be healthy before growth spending makes sense
  • Expansion within existing accounts is the cheapest growth. If it is happening organically, the product has real pull
  • For SmarterDx: measures whether coders keep accepting AI suggestions after the first month and whether hospitals expand the tool from one department to others on their own
